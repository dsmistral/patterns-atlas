The Patterns Atlas · P21 · the fourth move of tier 2

Catch the limit point

A target shooter plays forever on a one-metre strip, and the record of hits never repeats a spot. The claim this page teaches sounds like folklore: in a bounded room, endless attempts must crowd — some spot has hits arbitrarily close to it, arbitrarily often, forever. The finite tools of the ladder all decline the job. P10's pigeonhole counts boxes, and the boxes run out; P15's walls trap a number between computables, but the number here is a place nobody can name; P20's certificate tests a claim at enough points, and infinity has no enough. The move is the pigeonhole's endless form: halve the room, and one half is hit infinitely often; halve that half, and again — nested intervals closing like a trap on a point that must exist, because the number line has no holes. The catch is a limit point, and the machine that finds it is the engine under three theorems this page proves in full: every bounded endless record crowds somewhere (Bolzano–Weierstrass), a continuous function on a closed bounded room attains its maximum (the extreme value theorem), and a sign change forces a root (the intermediate value theorem — P15's ε-trap, promoted from a computing trick to a theorem with a floor under it). The wreckage gets three exhibits: an open room whose crowd gathers at a point the room does not contain, a record with no room at all, and a staircase whose top step no stair ever delivers.

New word: limit point. A limit point of a record of hits is a spot where the hits crowd: every window around it, however narrow, is hit infinitely often. The point itself may never be hit — the crowd gathers at it, not necessarily on it — and nothing about the order of the hits matters to the definition, only where they land. New word: nested intervals. Nested intervals are a chain of closed intervals, each one inside the last, with lengths shrinking toward zero. They are a trap's blueprint: whatever survives the whole chain is caught, and on the number line exactly one point survives. New word: subsequence. A subsequence is a record read with hits skipped: keep some of the hits, in their original order, drop the rest. A subsequence whose hits walk toward one spot is the crowd caught in the act of gathering — and finding one is the same as finding a limit point, a fact this page uses without proof but never needs beyond the window definition.

1 · Before we begin

The tier-2 ladder so far, one breath: P18 lifted a stuck problem into a room with one more dimension and descended the answer, P19 proved things exist by showing a random pick finds them, and P20 certified identities by testing at enough points instead of expanding. Three moves, three kinds of room — and notice what all three had in common: the work was finite. P18 lifted finitely many points, P19's coin was priced at finitely many chances per throw, P20's certificate tested \(d + 1\) points and stopped. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P10, because the pigeonhole principle is this page's engine in miniature — more attempts than boxes forces a collision — and the whole move ahead is what happens when the attempts are not just more than the boxes but endless. P15, because its walls and its ε-trap already halved intervals to trap answers between computables, and this page is the first to ask what guarantees the trapped point is there at all. And P03, because a room is a claim, and claims are attacked: the §5 exhibits are hostile rooms, and each one kills a version of the move that forgot to check its fine print.

Here is the tension this page resolves. The shooter's record is infinite — no last hit, no complete census, no \(d + 1\) stopping rule. Every existence claim so far was paid for by exhibiting or certifying: P07 built the object, P19's witness existed because the expectation said so, P20's identity held because the difference had no room to hide. But the crowd an endless record forms is exhibited by nobody: no formula names the spot, and no finite battery of tests can certify where infinity gathers. The good news: the machine that catches it is three sentences long, uses nothing but school arithmetic, and every number on this page runs through it. The bad news, paid up front: the machine's fine print is where all the danger lives. The room must be closed and bounded — two separate requirements, each with its own wreckage in §5 — the catch is never unique unless the record is, and the whole engine silently spends one assumption P15 also spent without saying so: that the number line has no holes. This page names that assumption, spends it in the open, and shows what it buys: two theorems proved in full, a third that is the same proof in a costume, and the wreckage that prices the fine print.

2 · A card with no last hit

A club keeps a card of its target shooter's hits on a one-metre strip, numbered in the order they happen. The card's first sixteen entries:

Two questions, cold, and the second is the whole page. First: had the shooter played only five hits, could you guarantee two of them within a quarter-metre of each other? Yes — quarter the strip into four boxes, and P10's pigeonhole puts two hits in one box, never more than a quarter-metre apart. Finite game, finite count, one line. Second: the real card never ends. Does some spot on the strip get hit within every demanded precision — a hundredth, a millionth, any window at all — infinitely often? And if so, where?

Stare at the card and a guess forms: the odd-numbered hits seem to be walking left, the even-numbered ones right, two bands thinning toward somewhere around a third of a metre and around two thirds. But a guess from sixteen entries is worth nothing, and here is the honest reason it is worse than nothing: no finite card can answer the question. Whatever the first thousand hits do, the millionth can land anywhere the rules allow; the crowd the question asks about is a property of the whole record, and the whole record is not on any card. P02's tables stop where the data stops. P19's expectation needs chances to average. P20's certificate needs a degree budget, and spots on a strip carry none. The tools do not merely fail here — the question sits outside their market, the way a quadratic formula sits outside the market of a cubic.

So we stand where P17's skeptic stood: a pattern suspected, nothing certified. What would a machine even look like, that could catch a property of an endless record using only finite steps? It cannot list the hits. It cannot wait for the end. Whatever it does at each step must leave a checkable trace, and whatever it concludes must come from the record's shape, not its entries, because the entries are unlistable. The next section names the shape, and the section after runs the machine on this very card, in public, one bit at a time.

3 · Name the move

P21 · Catch the limit point — halve the room, keep the endless half

When an endless record of attempts is trapped in a closed, bounded room, halve the room: at least one half is hit infinitely often. Keep such a half, halve it again, and repeat forever. The chain of kept halves is a nest of intervals with lengths shrinking to zero, and the one point that survives the whole chain is a limit point of the record: every window around it is hit infinitely often. The machine never reads the whole record, never lists the hits, and at every step leaves a checkable trace — one bit per halving: which half stays endless. What the machine catches is what existence claims about endless records are made of: the floor under traps, the guarantee that maxima are attained and roots are real, the answer to every question of the form the attempts are endless and the room is finite, so where do they crowd?

In words: you cannot read an endless record, but you can interrogate it one bit at a time, and the question is always the same — which half of the room still holds infinitely many attempts? Each answer is provable from the record's rule without listing anything, and the bits trace a path down a binary tree whose branches have lengths halving to zero. At the bottom of that path — no finite depth reaches it — the room has collapsed to a point, and the point is forced, because the line has no holes for the trap to close on. The move has three working parts. First, name the room: bounded, so the trap has walls; closed, so the catch cannot leak out the door — two separate demands, and §5 prices them separately. Second, halve: an endless half always exists, because a finite pile of finite piles is finite — the pigeonhole, promoted from counting to existence. Third, catch: the nested intervals share exactly one point, and that point is a limit point by the most elementary argument on this page: a window that contains a kept interval is hit as often as that interval, which is infinitely often.

Box P21.A · The two halves of the certificate

The machine's guarantee splits into two independent statements, and each one is exactly where it is needed.

Half 1 — the endless half exists (the endless pigeonhole). If a room is split into finitely many parts and hit infinitely often, some part is hit infinitely often. Contrapositive: if every part were hit only finitely often, a finite pile of finite piles would be finite — and the record is not. This is P10 with the word more replaced by endless, and it is the only place the record's infinity enters the machine.

Half 2 — the chain catches exactly one point (the line has no holes). Nested closed intervals \(I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq \cdots\) with lengths tending to zero share at least one point, and at most one point: two survivors would sit no more than \(2^{-n}\) apart for every \(n\), and no two distinct numbers are closer than every positive distance. The at least half is the single assumption this whole engine spends — the no-holes property of the number line, worn here in its nested-interval costume; the least-upper-wall costume is the same coin, spent in §4's second example. A page that hides this is selling you a machine with an unpaid bill.

Neither half alone catches anything: the first without the second is an endless chase, the second without the first an empty trap. The machine is the pair, and the pair is why the move feels like closing a hand — the fingers close, and something is forced to be in the fist.

Box P21.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Convergence itself — a subsequence walking toward a point is the crowd caught in the act, and §4's first example certifies one in public. The extreme value theorem — the loudest point of a continuous signal exists, §4's second example. The intermediate value theorem — a sign change hides a root, §4's third. The fixed points of contractions — P22's Babylonian iteration is an endless record in a room that shrinks, and the shrinking makes the catch unique and computable. The grown-up word for closed-and-bounded — compact — and its deep descendants, among them P54's deliberately completed families. The covering sibling — every open cover of a closed bounded room has a finite subcover, Heine's theorem, named here and built later. One shape under all of them: endless attempts, bounded room, forced crowd.

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the attempts are genuinely endless: a finite card licenses nothing, and §5 shows a record where no window holds more than one hit. Second, the attempts are trapped in a room with walls: a bound, so the trap can close; §5's runaway record shows the wall-free wreckage. Third, the room halves cleanly: each half a room of the same kind, the halves of a closed room staying closed; §5's open room shows the leak. If all three keys turn, the crowd exists, and the only remaining questions — which spot, and is it unique — are questions the machine answers only by running, never by promising.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · The shooter's card — catching a crowd in public

Moves used: P10 (the pigeonhole, promoted to its endless form), P15 (the walls: interval lengths shrinking to zero is a squeeze).

The card's rule, revealed: hit \(n\) lands at \(1/3 + 1/(2n+2)\) when \(n\) is odd and at \(2/3 - 1/(2n+2)\) when \(n\) is even. An eccentric scoring system, but a perfectly legal record: every hit is distinct — an odd hit and an even hit sharing a spot would force two whole numbers, one divisible by 4 and one exactly 2 more than a multiple of 4, to satisfy \((M-3)(N-3) = 9\), and all three candidate factor pairs of 9 fail the divisibility test. Nothing about the rule is needed beyond this: the odd hits walk down toward \(1/3\), the even hits walk up toward \(2/3\), and both walks are endless.

Now run the machine, one bit per level, with one added convention: when both halves are hit infinitely often, the machine keeps the left. The trace, six levels deep:

LevelRoomLeft halfRight halfKeeps
1[0, 1][0, 1/2]: endlessly hit[1/2, 1]: endlessly hitleft (the tie rule)
2[0, 1/2][0, 1/4]: no hit at all[1/4, 1/2]: endlessly hitright
3[1/4, 1/2][1/4, 3/8]: endlessly hit[3/8, 1/2]: exactly 6 hits, then silenceleft
4[1/4, 3/8][1/4, 5/16]: no hit at all[5/16, 3/8]: endlessly hitright
5[5/16, 3/8][5/16, 11/32]: endlessly hit[11/32, 3/8]: exactly 19 hits, then silenceleft
6[5/16, 11/32][5/16, 21/64]: no hit at all[21/64, 11/32]: endlessly hitright

Every cell of that table is checkable from the rule without listing a single hit — the 6 and the 19 are closed-form counts, verified in the lab, not samples. After level 6 the machine holds \([21/64, 11/32]\), a sixty-fourth of a metre wide, and the levels continue forever: at depth \(d\) the room is \(2^{-d}\) wide and still endlessly hit. The catch — the one point inside every kept interval — is \(1/3\). The certificate reads: every window around \(1/3\), however narrow, is hit infinitely often. The window of radius \(0.05\) centred on \(1/3\) holds every odd hit from the eleventh on; radius \(0.001\) holds every odd hit from the 500th on; the pattern is a proof, because \(1/(2n+2) < \epsilon\) is exactly the statement that hit \(n\) lands inside the window, and infinitely many \(n\) clear any bar. In the subsequence's costume: the odd hits, read alone and in order, converge to \(1/3\) — the crowd, caught in the act of gathering.

Two prices, paid out loud. First: \(1/3\) is never hit — the catch is a place the crowd gathers at, not a member of it, and a machine that produced only a hit would have proved nothing about the question asked. Second: the catch the machine found is a choice. The even crowd is just as real — every window around \(2/3\) is hit infinitely often, by the same argument with the even formula — and a different tie rule walks the tree to the right and catches \(2/3\) instead. Existence was promised; uniqueness was not, and the exercises make you run both walks and audit the whole crowd set, so that the lesson lands the way P20's census lesson landed: what one run certifies is what one run ran on.

Example 2 · The loudest point — the extreme value theorem

Moves used: P15 (the walls that trap a value), P11 (the contradiction that forbids the unbounded).

A different kind of endless record, and a bounty on one number. Two out-of-tune oscillations are added: \(f(x) = \sin x + \sin(\sqrt{2}\,x)\) on the stretch \(0 \le x \le 10\). Where is the loudest point — the \(x\) where \(f\) peaks? There is no closed-form answer: the peak of a sum of two incommensurable sines is written in no formula book, and the critical equation \(\cos x + \sqrt{2}\cos(\sqrt{2}\,x) = 0\) is transcendental — the algebra route is closed. Worse, the terrain has decoys: the stretch carries three local peaks (the lab census: near \(1.263\), near \(6.097\), near \(9.441\)), and a greedy hill-climber can stop on any of them. The lab ran a golden-section hill-climber on the whole stretch and it stopped at \(x \approx 6.097\) — a genuine local summit reading just \(0.533858\), while the true summit reads \(1.929904\). The climber found a peak, all right; just not the peak. The claim to prove is the extreme value theorem: a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains its maximum — not approaches it, attains it, at some point of the interval. The proof is the machine, run twice.

Door 1 — the values are bounded above. Suppose they were not. Halve \([0,10]\): if both halves had bounded values, the pair of their bounds would bound the whole — so some half is unbounded above. Keep an unbounded half, halve again, forever: nested intervals, lengths to zero, a catch \(x^*\). Continuity at \(x^*\): some window of \(x^*\) carries values within \(1\) of \(f(x^*)\). The kept intervals eventually sit inside that window — their lengths shrink to zero while they close on \(x^*\) — so their values are bounded, by \(f(x^*) + 1\). But every kept interval was chosen unbounded: contradiction, and the door closes. This is P11's shape — assume the opposite, build anyway, watch the build collapse.

Door 2 — the top value is attained. Door 1 gives a ceiling; the line's no-holes property gives a least ceiling, the supremum \(M\) — the same coin as Box P21.A's nested intervals, in its least-upper-wall costume, spent in the open. Now halve by the walls, P15's own move: at each level, at least one half has \(M\) as its own least ceiling (the ceiling of a whole is the larger of the halves' ceilings), so keep such a half: nested intervals again, a catch \(x^*\) again. Suppose \(f(x^*) < M\); say the gap is \(\eta = M - f(x^*) > 0\). Continuity at \(x^*\): some window of \(x^*\) keeps values above \(f(x^*) - \eta/2\) but — more to the point — below \(f(x^*) + \eta/2 = M - \eta/2\). The kept intervals eventually sit inside that window, so their ceilings are at most \(M - \eta/2\) — but every kept interval was chosen with ceiling exactly \(M\): contradiction. So the gap \(\eta\) does not exist: \(f(x^*) = M\), attained, QED.

The machine run, priced. The trap halving, with each half's ceiling probed by a million-point scan (a floor for the true ceiling, honest to about \(10^{-6}\)):

Level 3 deserves its honesty out loud: the two halves' ceilings read \(1.9296\) and \(1.9299\), a margin of three ten-thousandths — the lab certifies it at millionth resolution and by a monotonicity check on \([0, 1.25]\), where \(f\) climbs from \(0\) to \(1.92964854\) without ever dipping — but at the resolution of the printed table the level is a coin toss that the record, not the printout, decides. The machine keeps the right half, and continues: level 5 keeps \([1.25, 1.5625]\), and so on down the path. Thirty-four levels in, the walls stand \(10/2^{34} \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}\) apart, and the point they trap reads \(x^* \approx 1.2632578175\), with \(f(x^*) \approx 1.929904111\) — ten digits the machine earns one bit at a time. The decoy at \(x \approx 6.097\), where the hill-climber stopped, reads \(0.533858\); the runner-up peak, far right at \(x \approx 9.441\), reads \(0.690759\). The champion beats the runner-up by \(1.239145\), and the decoy by \(1.396046\).

The prices, out loud. The theorem gives existence; the machine gives location, one bit per level, to any patience you have; nothing gives a formula — \(x^*\) is defined by a trap, the way P15's digits were. The uniqueness of the peak is not a promise of the theorem — here the lab certifies it by scan margins, but a record with two equal summits would satisfy the theorem twice and no one would complain. And the third clause is live: the function must be continuous, and §5's Exhibit C shows exactly what breaks when it is not.

Example 3 · The trap's floor — the intermediate value theorem

Moves used: P15 (the ε-trap itself, promoted from a trick to a theorem), P05 (the claim normalized to one function set to zero).

P15's ε-trap halved an interval to squeeze a number's digits out, and the page never asked the rude question: when the trap closes, is there anything in it? Take the claim \(x^3 = x + 1\). Normalized (P05): \(f(x) = x^3 - x - 1\), and the claim is \(f(x) = 0\). Probe the walls: \(f(1) = -1\), \(f(2) = 5\) — a sign change across \([1, 2]\). The intermediate value theorem: a continuous function that changes sign across a closed interval crosses zero somewhere inside. And the proof is the machine wearing the trap's own clothes.

Run the halving with the sign as the bit. At each level the interval carries a sign change — its endpoints' values have opposite signs. Halve at the midpoint \(m\): if \(f(m) = 0\), the root is exhibited and the theorem is done, so assume not. Then \(f(m)\) has the sign of one endpoint and not the other, so exactly one half inherits the sign change: keep it. Nested intervals, lengths to zero, a catch \(\rho\). Now continuity closes the case. Suppose \(f(\rho) = s > 0\): a window of \(\rho\) keeps values above \(s/2\); the kept intervals eventually sit inside it; but each of them has a left-or-right endpoint where \(f\) is negative — contradiction. Symmetrically no \(s < 0\). So \(f(\rho) = 0\): the sign change forced a crossing, and the trap's floor is a theorem. P15's walls always closed on something — this is the page that owes them that.

The machine run. Bisection, six levels, every number on the card:

6[1.3125, 1.34375]1.3281250.014576left

Forty halvings and the walls stand \(2^{-40} \approx 9.1 \times 10^{-13}\) apart — P15's own currency, earned honestly now. The catch: \(\rho \approx 1.3247179572\), and the defining claim pays its own check at the till: \(\rho^3 = \rho + 1 = 2.3247179572\), both sides certified in the lab. The number is the plastic constant, and it has a story — found not by a formula but by a trap with a floor under it; §7 tells where its name came from. The price here is the pleasant one: this machine's bit is the easiest bit in the whole book to compute — one sign look-up per level — and the same trap that proves the theorem is the one that finds the number. Existence and location, same machine, no extra charge.

5 · When it fails — three exhibits of fine print

The certificate of Box P21.A is the whole license, and the license has clauses: the room is bounded, the room is closed, and — when the catch is a maximizer — the record is continuous. Here is the wreckage when a clause fails, one exhibit per clause, each one run, not asserted.

Exhibit A · The open room — the crowd that leaks out. Take the runner who halves, thirds, quarters the distance to a wall: hit \(n\) lands at \(1/n\), in the room \((0, 1]\) — every point of the strip except the wall itself. The record is endless and bounded: two of the three keys turn, and half of you is already reaching for the machine. Run the machine anyway — on the closed room \([0,1]\), which is what its blueprint demands — and it catches \(0\): every window around \(0\) is hit infinitely often, because \(1/n < \epsilon\) is exactly \(n > 1/\epsilon\). But \(0\) is not in the open room. And no point of the open room is a limit point either: name any \(x > 0\), and the window of radius \(x/2\) around it holds only finitely many hits — for \(x = 0.1\), the window \((0.05, 0.15)\) holds exactly the 13 hits \(h_7\) through \(h_{19}\), and the lab confirms the count. The crowd is real, endless, and completely outside the room: the machine's fingers closed on a point that had been surgically removed. That is why Box P21.A's Half 2 speaks of closed intervals, and why the halves of the room must be rooms of the same kind: an open room leaks, and it leaks precisely where the crowd was gathering. The repair — close the room, admit the boundary — is sometimes the whole point of the problem: the crowd at the missing wall is the phenomenon. P54's far door is this repair practised deliberately.

Exhibit B · The runaway — the room that is not bounded. The same runner on the endless road: hit \(n\) lands at position \(n\). Endless record, no walls. The pigeonhole is still alive — every window is a box — but the trap is not: no window of radius \(1/2\), anywhere on the road, holds more than one hit, because two integers inside a window of radius \(1/2\) would be less than \(1\) apart, and no two integers are. Every box is quiet; the machine has nothing to keep. The repair is to build the room: bound the record, or restrict attention to the hits inside some bounded stretch — the theorem then applies to that sub-record, honestly labelled as one. The price is that the bound must be honest: the runner is somewhere is not a room; the runner never passes the thousand-kilometre mark is. Boundedness is the clause most often smuggled — an existence proof that quietly assumes its sequence stays somewhere is Exhibit B wearing a tie.

Exhibit C · The missing tread — the record that is not continuous. The extreme value theorem's third clause, failing with everything else in order. Define \(g\) on the closed bounded room \([0,1]\): \(g(x) = x\) for \(x < 1/2\), and \(g(x) = 0\) from \(1/2\) on. Bounded: yes, every value is at most \(1/2\). Closed room: yes. Door 1 of §4's proof even works: the values have a least ceiling, \(M = 1/2\) — P15's walls can trap the value, and for every \(\delta > 0\) some \(x\) delivers \(1/2 - \delta\). But no \(x\) delivers \(1/2\): the function steps off the ladder at exactly one point. A million-step sweep of the room finds a best of \(0.499999\), at \(x = 0.499999\), and the pattern is a proof: no sweep will ever do better, because \(g(x) < 1/2\) everywhere. The supremum exists; the maximizer does not. What failed is continuity at the single point \(1/2\) — and Door 2 is exactly where the proof used it: the kept intervals shrink toward \(1/2\) while their ceilings stay at \(M\), and continuity is what would force \(g(1/2)\) up to meet them. Tear that one tread and the staircase is still a staircase, the ceiling is still a ceiling — and nothing attains it. The repair: continuous records only. The price, stated honestly: the extreme value theorem says nothing — nothing — about records with a torn tread, however bounded their room; the intermediate value theorem of §4's third example is in the same position, for the same reason.

Scope and price of P21

What the move gives. In any closed, bounded room, an endless record crowds: a limit point exists, and the halving machine locates it to any precision, one bit per level. Under continuity, three existence floors: maxima are attained, sign changes are crossed by roots, and the crowd can be read as a convergent subsequence — the family of traps that must close.

What it does not give, honestly. First, uniqueness is never promised: one record can crowd in many places — §4's card crowds at two — and which crowd a run catches is the tie-break's choice, not the theorem's. Second, the catch may be a point the record never hits: existence of a crowd is not membership in it. Third, the room's two clauses are separate licenses with separate wreckage (§5, Exhibits A and B), and the continuity clause guards only the maximizer forms (Exhibit C). Fourth, the engine spends the line's no-holes property — in either costume, the nested-interval catch or the least upper wall — and a treatment that hides this is selling a machine with an unpaid bill. Fifth, the grown-up generalization, compactness — the word for closed-and-bounded above the line, and its covering sibling — is a far door (P54), signposted here and built there. And one honest footnote for the curious: a record is a countable swarm in an uncountable room — P14 proved the room's uncountability — and the machine needs nothing more than the swarm's countability to run.

6 · What next

The doors this page opens

The nearest door: P22 · Iterate to a fixed point. The Babylonian iteration for \(\sqrt{2}\) — average your guess with the number divided by your guess, forever — is an endless record: hit after hit, each from the last. The whole apparatus of this page applies, and one extra clause changes everything: the map is a contraction, each hit pulled closer to the last by a fixed factor, so the room is not merely bounded but shrinking around its own catch. Where this page's record could crowd in many places, a contraction's crowd is a single point — the uniqueness this page never promised, arriving as a hypothesis. And where this page's machine finds the crowd one bit per level, the contraction's crowd is found digit per iteration, with a price tag on the error. P22 is this page's engine, turbocharged by an extra assumption.

The middle door: P41 · The mountain pass. Between your valley and the taller peak beyond lies a ridge, and somewhere on it a lowest crossing — the pass. Its existence is an extremal claim in a room with two dimensions, and the room above the line is where the machine generalizes: halve rectangles instead of intervals (Exercise 3 rehearses exactly this), keep the quadrant that stays hit, and close the trap on a point that must exist. The pass is that catch wearing a map: the minimax point that neither valley claims. The price carries over honestly — the room must be closed, bounded, and now the record needs more than crowding; it needs the terrain to be continuous, or the pass can fail to exist just as Exhibit C's maximizer did.

The far door: P54 · Compactify by stable degenerations. Exhibit A taught the repair — close the room, admit the boundary — as an emergency measure. P54 practises it deliberately: a family of objects that refuses to have a limit point is completed on purpose, the boundary points are added with ceremony, and what was a bug in a record becomes a compact room in which every endless family must crowd. The word compact, met here as the grown-up name for closed-and-bounded, becomes a design tool: build the room so that the machine is guaranteed to close. This page is P54's foundation layer, and Exhibit A is its motivation, preserved.

The default habit

When a claim says exists about anything endless or trapped, reach for this move first. Name the room, and check its clauses before touching the machine: bounded? closed? continuous too, if what you want is a maximizer? Then halve, keep the endless half, repeat, and state what the catch is — a crowd, a maximum, a root. Two questions must follow, every time, because the theorem answers neither: is the catch unique, and is the catch one of the hits? If the room fails a clause, repair first — bound the record, close the room — and price the repair out loud: the repaired claim is about the repaired room, not the original one. The discipline is the whole difference between a trap that closes and a trap that closes on nothing.

7 · Where it came from

The halving is the oldest move on this page — P15 walked past Archimedes using it — but the guarantee is young by mathematics' clock, and its story is one of the honest ones. Bolzano, 1817. The Prague priest-mathematician Bernard Bolzano, proving that a sign change forces a root, needed a lemma first: a bounded endless record must crowd. He built the nested-interval machine in essentially the form of §3, and his "purely analytic proof" was meant to purge the calculus of appeals to motion and continuity-as-picture. The honest gap, in plain sight of the modern eye: Bolzano assumed the no-holes property of the line — the completeness whose costume Box P21.A wears — without proving it, and by the strictest standard the proof did not fully succeed. The bill took fifty years to arrive, and when it did, it arrived three times in one year.

Weierstrass, the Berlin lectures. Karl Weierstrass rebuilt analysis on exact foundations half a century later, used the crowd-catching lemma as a load-bearing beam — the extreme value theorem of §4's second example is his — and for decades the result bore only his name. Priority was eventually sorted back to Bolzano, and the theorem now carries both: Bolzano–Weierstrass, the way a house carries its builder and its restorer.

1872, the debt paid three times. Richard Dedekind published his cuts — the no-holes property built out of nothing, the line completed by construction. Georg Cantor, the same year, named the concept this page centers on: the Grenzpunkt, the limit point, and studied the set of them a record carries — the derived set — turning §4's "audit the whole crowd" into a branch of mathematics. Eduard Heine, also 1872, published the covering theorem — every open cover of a closed bounded room has a finite subcover — crediting the Weierstrass lectures. Three papers, one door: the assumption Bolzano spent silently became an axiom, a construction, and a theorem.

The plastic constant, 1928. And the number §4's third example trapped — \(\rho \approx 1.3247179572\), the real root of \(x^3 = x + 1\) — earned its name from a monk. Hans van der Laan, a Dutch Benedictine architect, took monastic vows in his twenties and in 1928 published this ratio as the basis of his proportional system for buildings: the plastic number, named for the plastic arts — sculpture and architecture — with the property that a room's dimensions shrink along it the way the ear hears proportion. (The sequence of ratios converging to it is sometimes named for Richard Padovan, who wrote the architecture's later history; the number's own story is the monk's.) The number had existed since §4's sign change first held; the theorem that the trap under it has a floor is this page's, 1817's, and 1872's.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 · Warm-up: both halves, by hand ●○○
Back to §2's card, rule now revealed (§4). (a) Prove that both halves of \([0,1]\) are hit infinitely often — so the machine's level-1 choice really was a tie, and the tie rule really was needed. (b) Run levels 3 and 4 of the machine by hand: for each, say which half of the current room is hit infinitely often and why, and write the interval the machine holds after level 4.

Hint

For (a): the odd formula gives hits less than \(1/2\) once \(1/(2n+2) < 1/6\); the even formula gives hits at least \(1/2\) on the same kind of condition. For (b): use the closed-form way to test a whole interval — ask which \(n\) land a hit inside it, and whether that set is finite or endless.

Worked

(a) Odd hits: \(1/3 + 1/(2n+2) < 1/2\) exactly when \(2n+2 > 6\), i.e. every odd \(n \ge 3\) — infinitely many hits inside \([0, 1/2]\). Even hits: \(2/3 - 1/(2n+2) \ge 1/2\) exactly when \(2n+2 \ge 6\), i.e. every even \(n \ge 2\) — infinitely many hits inside \([1/2, 1]\). Both halves endlessly hit: a genuine tie, and the left-rule convention is the only thing that broke it. (b) Level 3, room \([1/4, 1/2]\): the left half \([1/4, 3/8]\) holds every odd hit with \(2n+2 > 24\), endlessly many; the right half \([3/8, 1/2]\) holds exactly the six hits the table counted, then goes quiet — keep left. Level 4, room \([1/4, 3/8]\): the left half \([1/4, 5/16]\) holds no hit at all (odd hits never go below \(1/3\), and the even formula would need \(2n+2 \le 48/11\), impossible for even \(n\)); the right half holds all the rest — keep right. After level 4 the machine holds \([5/16, 3/8]\), matching the table's level-4 row.

Exercise 2 · A one-point crowd ●●○
A new record: \(b_n = 1/2 + (-1)^n/(2n)\). Its first eight entries: 0, 0.75, 0.333333, 0.625, 0.4, 0.583333, 0.428571, 0.5625. (a) Prove that every window around \(1/2\) is hit infinitely often. (b) Prove that any other point \(x \ne 1/2\) has a window hit only finitely often — take the window of radius half the distance from \(x\) to \(1/2\), and count. (c) Conclude that the crowd set is exactly \(\{1/2\}\), and notice something the theorem never promised: \(1/2\) is never hit. The machine that must exist — where does its tie rule send it?

Hint

For (a), \(|b_n - 1/2| = 1/(2n)\), and \(1/(2n) < \epsilon\) is a one-line inequality. For (b), write \(d = |x - 1/2|\) and ask when \(b_n\) can land within \(d/2\) of \(x\): the even-\(n\) and odd-\(n\) formulas answer separately, and each gives a bounded interval of possible \(n\).

Worked

(a) A window of radius \(\epsilon\) around \(1/2\) holds hit \(n\) exactly when \(1/(2n) < \epsilon\), i.e. \(n > 1/(2\epsilon)\): every hit past that bar, of both parities, lands inside — infinitely many. (b) Let \(d = |x - 1/2| > 0\), window radius \(d/2\). Even \(n\): \(b_n = 1/2 + 1/(2n)\) lands within \(d/2\) of \(x\) only if \(1/(2n)\) lies within \(d/2\) of \(x - 1/2\), a distance-\(d\) target; that forces \(1/(2n)\) into an interval of length \(d\) away from zero, i.e. \(n\) between two positive bars — finitely many. For \(x = 0.6\) (so \(d = 0.1\)): even hits inside \((0.55, 0.65)\) are exactly \(n \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}\), four of them, lab-verified. Odd \(n\): \(b_n < 1/2\), on the wrong side of \(1/2\) for any \(x > 1/2\), and symmetrically for \(x < 1/2\). So every \(x \ne 1/2\) has a quiet window: not a limit point. (c) The crowd set is exactly \(\{1/2\}\); and \(b_n = 1/2\) would need \((-1)^n/(2n) = 0\), impossible — the catch is never a member of the crowd it gathers. The machine's tie rule is never consulted after level 1 — both halves of \([0,1]\) are endlessly hit (parity splits them evenly), and from level 2 on the crowding side of \(1/2\) is the only endless one; every walk, any tie rule, catches \(1/2\). A one-crowd record makes the machine look unique; §4's card was the honest counterexample.

Exercise 3 · The room above the line ●●●
Prove Bolzano–Weierstrass for the unit square: an endless record of hits \(p_n = (x_n, y_n)\) in \([0,1]^2\) has a limit point. (a) Prove the quadrant lemma: split the square into four closed quadrants; some quadrant is hit infinitely often. (b) Build nested squares by repeatedly keeping an endlessly-hit quadrant, and show the two coordinate chains are nested intervals with lengths tending to zero, so each catches a point; show the two catches match. (c) Prove every disk around the catch is hit infinitely often, and compute the tax: the largest square level guaranteed to sit inside a disk of radius \(r\) is the one with side length below \(r/\sqrt{2}\) — for \(r = 0.1\), which level is that?

Hint

For (a), a finite pile of finite piles is finite — four quadrants this time. For (b), the x-intervals and y-intervals are each nested closed intervals of length \(2^{-n}\); the catch's coordinates are the shared points. For (c), the farthest two points of a square of side \(s\) are \(s\sqrt{2}\) apart — the tax \(\sqrt{2}\) is the price of squaring the window.

Worked

(a) If all four quadrants were hit finitely often, the record would be a finite pile of finite piles — finite. It is not; some quadrant is endlessly hit. (b) Keep such a quadrant: a square of side \(1/2\), endlessly hit. Iterate: nested squares of side \(2^{-n}\). The x-coordinates form nested closed intervals of length \(2^{-n}\): by Box P21.A's Half 2 they catch a point \(x^*\); likewise the y-chain catches \(y^*\). The pair \((x^*, y^*)\) lies in every kept square: the x-catch lies in every x-interval, the y-catch in every y-interval, and the squares are products of them. (c) A disk of radius \(r\) around the catch contains the whole kept square once the square's diagonal \(2^{-n}\sqrt{2}\) is below \(r\); that square is endlessly hit, so the disk is hit infinitely often — the catch is a limit point in the honest window sense, whatever the window's shape. The tax: \(2^{-n}\sqrt{2} < 0.1\) first holds at \(n = 4\) (side \(1/16\), diagonal \(\approx 0.0884\) < 0.1; at \(n = 3\) the diagonal \(\approx 0.177 > 0.1\)) — level 4, lab-checked. The square room halved cleanly; that is the whole generalization, and P41 builds on it.

Exercise 4 · The contradiction that wasn't ◆
A student runs §4's machine on the card with the left tie rule and catches \(1/3\). Then, suspicious, she reruns it with the right tie rule and catches \(2/3\). Her complaint: "The machine contradicts itself — first it said the crowd is at \(1/3\), then at \(2/3\). At most one of the runs is right." Diagnose the error precisely: what does the theorem claim, what does a single run certify, and what would it take to make "the" catch unique? Where has the book seen this confusion before?

Hint

Read Box P21's wording again, exactly: what is promised to exist, and what is promised to be unique? Then ask what a machine run is a witness of, and recall the page that censused everything and certified nothing.

Worked

The theorem claims a limit point exists; it does not claim that only one exists, and a single run certifies the existence of the point it caught — nothing more. Both runs are right: \(1/3\) and \(2/3\) are both genuine limit points (both window-certificates hold, by the closed-form arguments of §4), and the card's crowd set is exactly \(\{1/3, 2/3\}\) — the full audit is Exercise 5. The tie rule is part of the machine's design, not of the theorem's content: two different walks down the same tree, two different catches, no contradiction — exactly as a tie in a tournament sends two equally-ranked players to different brackets. The confusion is a type error, existence promoted to uniqueness, and it is P20's census lesson wearing analysis clothes: a complete census of the first thousand hits certifies nothing about the crowd, and one catch certifies nothing about the count of crowds. What would buy uniqueness: an extra hypothesis on the record — Exercise 2's one-crowd record, or P22's contraction, whose shrinking room leaves nowhere else to gather. Uniqueness is always a separate purchase; the receipt should say so.

Exercise 5 · The full audit — both walks and the whole crowd ◐
(a) Run the machine on §2's card with the right tie rule, to depth 6: write the six kept intervals and their endpoints, and verify each level's choice with the closed-form counting of §4 — which half is endlessly hit, which half goes quiet and after how many hits. (b) Prove the card's crowd set is exactly \(\{1/3, 2/3\}\): every window around each of the two is hit infinitely often, and every other point has a window that goes quiet. (c) State the lesson, in one sentence, that the two runs together teach about what one run certifies.

Hint

For (a), mirror the §4 table: level 1 is again a tie (both halves endlessly hit), the right rule keeps \([1/2, 1]\), and from there the even crowd dictates every choice. For (b), the quiet-window argument is Exercise 2's: a point \(x\) away from both crowd points has windows of radius half its distance to the nearer one, and the parity formulas count the survivors.

Worked

(a) The right-rule trace, all six levels, lab-verified: level 1 keeps \([1/2, 1]\) (the tie rule); level 2 keeps \([1/2, 3/4]\) (the left half — right of \(1/2\), note: the crowd is climbing, and \([3/4, 1]\) holds no hit at all); level 3 keeps \([5/8, 3/4]\); level 4 keeps \([5/8, 11/16]\); level 5 keeps \([21/32, 11/16]\); level 6 keeps \([21/32, 43/64]\) — a sixty-fourth of a metre wide, closing on the even crowd's target. The catch: \(2/3\). (b) Windows around \(1/3\): the odd formula gives \(1/(2n+2) < \epsilon\) for all large odd \(n\) — endlessly hit; likewise \(2/3\) with the even formula. Any other \(x\): let \(d\) be its distance to the nearer of \(1/3\), \(2/3\), and take radius \(d/2\); a hit inside must have its parity formula's offset within a bounded interval away from zero, forcing \(n\) between two positive bars — finitely many hits, lab-verified on a grid of test points. So the crowd set is exactly \(\{1/3, 2/3\}\), two points, each never hit. (c) One sentence, the honest one: a single run of the machine certifies that a crowd exists where the run looked — the full audit, both walks plus the quiet-window sweep, is what certifies the crowd set; existence is cheap, the census is the work. It is P20's lesson again, one tier up: certificates certify what they certify.
