2 · Meet the problem

You are standing at \(A = (0, 4)\) — four kilometres north of a long, straight river (take the river to be the \(x\)-axis). Your friend is at \(B = (6, 2)\): six kilometres east of you and two north of the river. You must walk to the river's bank, fill a bucket, and then walk on to your friend. Where should you touch the river so that the total walk is as short as possible?

Try it before reading on. The intuitive guess is to head for the bank more or less between the two of you. Measure a few candidates honestly:

  • touch at \(x = 1\): the walk is \(\sqrt{1 + 16} + \sqrt{25 + 4} = \sqrt{17} + \sqrt{41} \approx 10.5262\) km;
  • touch at \(x = 3\): the walk is \(\sqrt{9 + 16} + \sqrt{9 + 4} = 5 + \sqrt{13} \approx 8.6056\) km;
  • touch at \(x = 4\): the walk is \(\sqrt{16 + 16} + \sqrt{4 + 4} = 4\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} \approx 8.4853\) km.

So the table says the answer is near \(x = 4\), where the walk is about \(8.4853\) km. But a scan is a detector, not an answer — it is P02's move, and P02 was honest about its limits. This is a prove claim in disguise: you want to know the touch point is exactly right, and why. Class-12 calculus can do it — minimize \(f(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + 16} + \sqrt{(6-x)^2 + 4}\) — but hold on, and watch something better. The problem gave you two points and a line. The proof needs a third point that nobody mentioned. Build it.

The build. Reflect your friend's position across the river: \(B = (6, 2)\) becomes \(B' = (6, -2)\), the same distance south of the river as \(B\) is north. This point \(B'\) is the auxiliary object — the problem forgot to give it to you. Now two things happen, and both are one line each:

  • Every candidate walk to \(B\) is also a walk to \(B'\). If you touch the bank at \((x, 0)\), then the leg from \((x, 0)\) to \(B = (6, 2)\) has exactly the same length as the leg from \((x, 0)\) to \(B' = (6, -2)\): the river is the perpendicular bisector of the segment \(BB'\), so the two legs are mirror twins. So your total walk \(A \to (x,0) \to B\) has the same length as the broken path \(A \to (x,0) \to B'\).
  • Among paths from \(A\) to \(B'\), the straight one is shortest. The straight segment from \(A = (0,4)\) to \(B' = (6,-2)\) crosses the river exactly once — at \((4, 0)\) — and any broken path \(A \to (x, 0) \to B'\) is longer than it (two sides of a triangle against the third; here the triangle is degenerate but the inequality is the same one).

Put together: the shortest walk touches at \((4, 0)\), and its length is just the straight-line distance

\[|AB'| = \sqrt{(6-0)^2 + (-2-4)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 36} = 6\sqrt{2} \approx 8.4853\ \text{km},\]

matching the scan — and replacing it with a proof. One built point, and the minimization evaporated: no derivative, no formula, just the triangle inequality wearing a mirror.

And there is a dividend. The two marked angles at \(P\) — the angle your inbound leg makes with the bank and the angle your outbound leg makes — are equal, because the second leg is the mirror of the leg toward \(B'\), and the straight line makes one angle with the bank. Light, bouncing off a mirror, takes exactly this path; Heron of Alexandria knew it (block 7). The auxiliary point did not just answer the question; it answered a question you had not asked yet.

Notice what actually happened, because it is the whole move in miniature. The problem gave you objects that could not connect: two points and a line, with the minimizing fact running through a point \(B'\) that existed nowhere in the statement. You built \(B'\) — by a recipe (reflect across the line: \((6, 2) \mapsto (6, -2)\)), with a defining property you could check (the bank is the perpendicular bisector of \(BB'\)), proved with it, and the answer mentions only the original objects: touch at \((4, 0)\), walk \(6\sqrt{2}\) kilometres. \(B'\) never appears in the answer. It was scaffolding. The move has a name, and it is the seventh pattern of the spine.
