Example 2 (geometry) — the half-hypotenuse median, built twice

The claim, pinned as prove-for-all (P01): in every right triangle, the median from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse is exactly half the hypotenuse. Concrete witness first (P02's instinct): the \(6\)-\(8\)-\(10\) triangle, \(C = (0,0)\), \(A = (6,0)\), \(B = (0,8)\); the hypotenuse midpoint is \(M = (3,4)\), and \(|CM| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5 = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 10\). True there — but a witness is a detector, not a proof, and the claim says every right triangle.

Stare at the triangle and you see the problem: \(M\) is easy to build (a midpoint is a recipe), but the relation \(|CM| = |AB|/2\) has nothing to grip. The distance \(|CM|\) and the distance \(|AB|\) live in different corners of the picture. The missing object, named by its job: something that puts \(CM\) and \(AB\) inside one figure where they are forced to relate.

Build one: a point. Reflect \(C\) through \(M\) — the recipe \(C' = 2M - C\), here \((0,0) \mapsto (6,8)\). Now \(M\) is the midpoint of both \(AB\) and \(CC'\): the diagonals of the quadrilateral \(A\!-\!C\!-\!B\!-\!C'\) bisect each other, so it is a parallelogram; it has a right angle at \(C\), so it is a rectangle; a rectangle's diagonals are equal, so \(|CC'| = |AB| = 10\); and \(M\) halves \(CC'\), so

\[|CM| = \tfrac{1}{2}|CC'| = \tfrac{1}{2}|AB|.\]

Four one-line steps, each school geometry, none of them the target theorem — and the last line is the theorem. The point \(C'\) is scaffolding: the statement "\(CM\) is half the hypotenuse" never mentions it.

Build two: a circle. A different builder answers the same job with a different object. Draw the circle centred at \(M\) through \(A\) (it passes through \(B\) — \(M\) is the midpoint of \(AB\)). Class-10 circle geometry says: the angle in a semicircle is a right angle, and — the direction needed here — the centre of the circle through a right triangle's vertices is the midpoint of its hypotenuse (the circle with diameter \(AB\) is the circumcircle of the right triangle). So \(C\) sits on this circle, and \(|CM|\) is a radius: \(|CM| = |AB|/2\), again. Two builds, one theorem, and a lesson in the move's nature: the job is unique, the object is not. (Honest note: build two leans on a heavier school result than build one — block 5 returns to the cost of that.)

The lab runs the theorem as a detector across all \(1{,}089\) integer right triangles with legs \(1\) to \(33\): \(|CM| = |AB|/2\) with zero mismatches, and the circle route's three-equal-radii check over all \(625\) triangles with legs \(1\) to \(25\), zero mismatches. Pythagorean witnesses worth remembering: the \((5, 12, 13)\) triangle has median \(6.5\); the \((8, 15, 17)\) has \(8.5\); every doubling of a Pythagorean triple doubles the median with it. Moves used: P07 (two builds for one job), P01 (prove-for-all pinned before any witness was computed), P02 (the \(6\)-\(8\)-\(10\) witness and the exhaustive detector grid), P03 (the demand that the build's own property — rectangle-ness — be verified from the recipe, not assumed).

Example 3 (numbers) — the equation you were never given

A class-12 favourite with a hidden move inside: given that \(x + \frac{1}{x} = 3\) (with \(x > 0\)), compute \(x^5 + \frac{1}{x^5}\) — exactly, and without ever solving for \(x\).

Look at what the problem gives you: one number \(x\), described by one symmetric equation. Look at what it asks: a power sum five steps away. The direct route — solve the quadratic, then raise to the fifth — drowns in surds: \(x = \tfrac{3 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}\), and fifth powers of that are nobody's idea of fun. Name the job: I need a machine that turns \(x + 1/x\) facts into power facts. The object: an auxiliary polynomial. Multiply the given equation by \(x\) and the machine appears:

\[x^2 - 3x + 1 = 0, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad x^2 = 3x - 1.\]

This equation is scaffolding — the problem never gave it to you — but it is a recipe object: it tells you exactly how to trade \(x^2\) for \(x\) and 1. Now define the power sums \(p_k = x^k + x^{-k}\) and multiply \(p_k\) by the given \(x + \frac{1}{x} = 3\):

\[3\,p_k = \left(x + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)\left(x^k + x^{-k}\right) = \underbrace{x^{k+1} + x^{-(k+1)}}_{p_{k+1}} + \underbrace{x^{k-1} + x^{-(k-1)}}_{p_{k-1}},\]

so the machine runs without ever touching \(x\)'s value: \(p_{k+1} = 3p_k - p_{k-1}\). Seed it with the two facts you own — \(p_0 = 2\) (that is \(1 + 1\)) and \(p_1 = 3\) (the given) — and grind:

\[p_2 = 3\cdot 3 - 2 = 7, \quad p_3 = 3\cdot 7 - 3 = 18, \quad p_4 = 3\cdot 18 - 7 = 47, \quad p_5 = 3\cdot 47 - 18 = \mathbf{123}.\]

Integers, the whole way — the surds never had a chance to appear. And the answer can be checked by two independent routes, both lab-verified: the binomial route expands \(\left(x + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)^5 = p_5 + 5p_3 + 10p_1\), so \(243 = p_5 + 90 + 30\) and \(p_5 = 123\); the radical route computes \(\left(\tfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^5 + \left(\tfrac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^5 = 123\) exactly (the irrational parts cancel; this is the route that was painful, run as a check, not as the proof). Three routes, one answer.

And a dividend, of the same kind the river problem paid: the radical route reveals who \(x\) is — \(\tfrac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2} = \varphi^2\), the golden ratio squared (check: \(\varphi \approx 1.6180340\), so \(\varphi^2 \approx 2.6180340\), and indeed \(\varphi^2 + \varphi^{-2} = 3\)). The power sums \(2, 3, 7, 18, 47, 123\) are then every other Lucas number — and \(p_5 = L_{10} = 123\) is exactly the trace of P06's tenth Fibonacci power \(Q^{10}\). The auxiliary polynomial and the auxiliary matrix are the same machine in two uniforms; P16 will have official words for such kinships. Scaffold down: "\(x^5 + 1/x^5 = 123\)" is a statement about the original \(x\) — the polynomial, the recurrence, and \(\varphi\) were all scaffolding. Moves used: P07 (the auxiliary polynomial as a recipe object), P06 (the cameo: the recurrence is the \(Q\)-machine in algebra's uniform), P02 (the small cases \(p_0\) to \(p_3\) computed by hand before the recurrence was trusted).
