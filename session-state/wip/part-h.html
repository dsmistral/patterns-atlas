6 · The doors this opens

Every pattern page ends by pointing at the doors it passes without opening. Three here, at honest distances.

The working door — P50 · Double centralizer decomposition. This page counted by asking how much of each wave an action contains; the working door asks what else commutes with the action. The operators that respect a group's waves form an algebra of their own, and the double centralizer theorem — the spine of Schur–Weyl duality — says the group and its commutant carve the same space into the same blocks, each side's character table answering for the other's. Distance: one tier step, into the orientation shelf. What it carries: the upgrade from "count the waves" to "the waves and their commutant rule each other" — the bookkeeping that makes representation theory a two-sided tool.

The far door — P51 · The Koszul dual swap. The character ledger splits a space into independent waves; the far machinery splits whole categories of spaces into dual waves, exchanging the roles of generators and relations. Characters survive there as graded traces — each wave keeping its amplitude book — but the geometry around them is homological and has no census this size. Distance: far — the reader who wants it must climb P33's bridge first. What it carries: the idea that "split into waves" scales from vector spaces to algebraic structures themselves.

The widening door — P19 · Count the outcomes. The nearest published sibling: both moves count without listing, one by expectation over coin flips, this page by inner products over symmetry. The deep rhyme: an expectation is an average over a group — the two-element group acting by "flip the coin" — so P19's averaging is this page's first example wearing probability's coat, and the pair of them bracket every counting shortcut between brute force and brute algebra. Distance: a step back down the ladder, and wider for it. What it carries: the habit of asking, of any hard count, "what am I averaging over?" — the table, the coin, or the continuum.

Default habit: when a count arrives qualified by an "up to" — rotation, relabeling, symmetry — name the group and its action before counting anything (P01), test whether the question is linear in the action (Key 2), and reach for the character table before brute force: the census answers once, the table answers the whole family, and it is reusable across every object that secretly wears the same group.

7 · Where the move came from

Non-load-bearing, and honest about it: the page above works if this section is skipped. But the character method has the strangest birth story in this book's catalog — the tool was invented for a count, not for groups — and the history is the best warning label against confusing the ledger with the mathematics.

The count before the device. Augustin-Louis Cauchy, in 1845, counted arrangements of letters — anagram families — and his counting used, without ceremony, the device now called "Burnside's lemma": the orbit count as an average of fixed counts. The attribution is a famous mislabel: William Burnside's own 1897 textbook, which popularized the average, credits it to Georg Frobenius — and history, reading the textbook and not the footnote, named the average after the wrong man. The lesson for this page's ledger: the average everyone uses was once a footnote everyone skipped.

The device, invented for a factorization count. Ferdinand Georg Frobenius, in 1896, in the Sitzungsberichte of the Prussian Academy, created character theory answering a question of Richard Dedekind's about factoring a huge determinant built from a group's multiplication table — the group determinant: how many irreducible factors, of what degrees? The answer, which Frobenius found by inventing characters, is the deepest receipt on this page: the number of irreducible factors equals the number of conjugacy classes, and their degrees are the character-table diagonal. Characters were not built to study groups; they were built to count — the whole apparatus of this page is the afterlife of one enumeration question about one determinant.

The license, and the first triumph. Heinrich Maschke's 1899 theorem — over a field whose characteristic divides no group order, every action splits into waves — made the ledger legal, and Burnside pressed it to its first great victory: his 1904 theorem, in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society, that every group of order \(p^\alpha q^\beta\) is solvable, proved not by classifying the groups but by counting their irreducible characters — the table settling a question about all groups of a family at once, exactly the move this page's Box P36.B celebrates.

The counting industry, twice born. John Howard Redfield, in 1927, in the American Journal of Mathematics (49, 433–455), published the theory of "group-reduced distributions" — the weighted inner products of Example 1, complete — and the paper slept for thirty years. George Pólya, in 1937, in Acta Mathematica (68), published the same machine under the name enumeration theory, aimed at chemistry: benzene rings, isomers, necklaces counted by the cycle index, which is the paint character of this page with a polynomial where the colors were. Pólya's paper made the career of a counting device; Redfield's priority was found later — the second warning of this section: the ledger's history has more misattributions than most, because counting tools get reinvented wherever a count wears symmetry.

The frontier. The largest character table ever computed belongs to the Monster — the 196,883-dimensional irreducible wave giving the group its name — and its entries, read against modular forms by John Conway and Simon Norton in "Monstrous Moonshine" (Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society 11, 308–339, 1979), turned a counting ledger into physics' strangest phonebook: dimensions of representations matching coefficients of modular forms, conjectured long before they were explained. From Cauchy's anagrams to the Monster's dimensions: the pattern's history is the story of mathematics learning that a count wearing symmetry is not one number but a table — and that the table, read patiently, answers questions nobody yet knows how to ask.
