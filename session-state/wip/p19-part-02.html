1 · Before we begin

Tier 2 is two climbs old in a moment: P18 taught the lift — prove the easy version in a bigger room and pay for the trip back down — and this page teaches its sideways twin. The spine's ten-plus-seven moves are all on the shelf above, and one of them matters here more than the rest. P10 forced collisions by counting: when more objects than boxes must be sorted, two objects share a box, and the collision was the conclusion. P10's counting needed two things — a map from objects to boxes, and more objects than boxes. Both are often missing exactly when existence questions are hardest: for a party of thirty people there are \(2^{435}\) acquaintance patterns, no boxes anywhere in sight, and no map that sorts them usefully. The move this page adds is the other direction of counting: when you cannot count objects into boxes, count the room of all candidates itself — how many candidates are bad, and is that smaller than the room? P02 is the close ancestor: its small tables are censuses of little rooms, and this page is the same census habit run on rooms so large that only an average can tour them. P11 is the skeleton underneath, as you will see in §3: the whole method is one contradiction wearing probability clothes. And P15 supplies the arithmetic idiom — the bounds here are walls, sums of small probabilities pushed under one.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Some objects are so buried that no search reaches them and no formula constructs them — yet the problem only asks that one exist. A seating of thirty people with no three mutual acquaintances and no three mutual strangers, if it exists, is one among \(2^{435}\); a good codebook, at the sizes where codes matter, is one among more candidates than particles in the visible universe; and for each of these, the classical tools are silent: construction needs an idea nobody has, the pigeonhole needs a map nobody can write, and the small-case census drowns. The probabilistic method's wager is that existence is a weaker demand than construction — weak enough to be paid for by an average. If a random candidate fails, on average, at fewer than one requirement, then some candidate fails at none — the failures cannot cover the room. The wager pays off shockingly often, and this page pays out three times in §4 alone. The bad news, and §5 spends three exhibits on it: the count can overflow and say nothing while the object exists (Exhibit A); the proof can leave the witness permanently anonymous (Exhibit B); and the experiment itself is a design choice that can be chosen badly (Exhibit C). Between the payouts and the wreckage stands one honest bookkeeping rule, the whole method in a line: expectation is counting, probability is a dialect, and the survivor is real exactly because the bad cannot fill the room.
