4 · The move at work

Example 1 · The general Ramsey bound — an inequality no search could earn

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim, in general \(k\)), P10 (the collision being dodged, one room up), P11 (the reductio in disguise, per Box P19.A).

Pin the claim the P01 way, now in general: for every \(k \ge 4\), there is a seating of \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\) people with no monochromatic group of \(k\) — that is, \(R(k,k) > \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\). The room: all \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) colorings, the coin flip per pair. The bad events, one per \(k\)-group: the group is all-red or all-blue, chance \(2 \cdot 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} = 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}\) each. The average crime count, by linearity:

\[\mathbb{E}[\text{mono } K_k\text{s in } K_n] \;=\; \binom{n}{k} \cdot 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}.\]

Two honest estimates finish it. First, \(\binom{n}{k} \le \frac{n^k}{k!}\): every ordered \(k\)-tuple of distinct people names a \(k\)-group, there are at most \(n^k\) ordered tuples, and each group is named \(k!\) times — a bound, not a count, and the looseness is priced below. Second, with \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\) so that \(n^k \le 2^{k^2/2}\), the exponents collapse:

\[\mathbb{E} \;\le\; \frac{2^{k^2/2}}{k!} \cdot 2^{1-k(k-1)/2} \;=\; \frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}.\]

Is \(\frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}\) below \(1\)? At \(k = 4\) it is \(\frac{2^3}{24} = \frac{1}{3}\), and each step from \(k\) to \(k+1\) multiplies it by \(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{k+1}\) — below \(1\) already at \(k = 4\), so the bound only shrinks. For every \(k \ge 4\): average crime count under \(1\), witness exists, \(R(k,k) > \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\). The actual values, computed exactly for each \(k\) — this is what the argument earns:

Now the honesty owed. The \(k = 4\) row certifies only \(R(4,4) > 4\) — while the truth is \(R(4,4) = 18\). The count is a floor, not an estimate: it says good seatings are plentiful, never that the bound is anywhere near tight. The gap has a name and a cure. The named cure: seat seventeen people at vertices \(0\) through \(16\), color the pair \(\{i,j\}\) red exactly when \(i - j\) is a quadratic residue mod \(17\) — the residues are \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16\}\), eight of the sixteen nonzero differences, a perfect split. This is the Paley seating, and it has no monochromatic group of four in either color: all \(\binom{17}{4} = 2380\) four-groups fail to be monochromatic, so \(R(4,4) > 17\), one short of the truth. The page's lab runs all 2380 checks. Note what each method paid: the coin argument bought abundance — a \(0.97\)-fraction of good colorings at \(k=4\) — while the Paley seating bought a name — a witness you can write on one line, at the price of one hundredfold more people. Existence is cheap; named existence is a different market.

Moves used, one level up: the room was designed (colorings of \(K_n\)), the experiment designed (fair coin), the count linear — and the price paid was the truth's location: the floor sits far below the fact.
