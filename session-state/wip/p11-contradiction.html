The Patterns Atlas · P11 · the eleventh move of the spine

Prove by contradiction

Some claims cannot be won head-on — no example witnesses them, no construction builds them, no table ever finishes them. So grant the enemy the opposite of what you want, run the machine honestly, and let the wreckage be the certificate. Assume the opposite, derive without mercy, watch it break.

New word: negation. The negation of a statement is its exact opposite — and for claims about every object, the opposite is a claim about one: the negation of "every attempt fails" is "one attempt succeeds"; the negation of "no rational squares to \(2\)" is "some rational squares to \(2\)". Flipping a "for all" into a "there exists" is the whole ignition of this page. New word: contradiction. Two statements that cannot both hold — "this fraction is in lowest terms" and "its numerator and denominator are both even". Logicians use the word for joint impossibility, not for anything ridiculous; a contradiction is a collision of facts, and it is the most decisive sound in mathematics. New word: reductio ad absurdum. Latin, "reduction to the absurd" — the scholastics' name for proving a claim by destroying its opposite. The name is furniture now; the move is older than the name, and this page trains it on purpose.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, and P10 forced collisions by counting. P10's closing pointer promised exactly this page: the pigeonhole argument is a proof by contradiction in miniature — suppose no two of your five numbers share a pair; then the four pairs hold at most four of them; but you chose five; contradiction. P11 names the general shape and trains it on purpose.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Some of the most decisive claims in mathematics are negatives: no rational squares to \(2\); there is no largest prime; this equation has no solution. A negative cannot be witnessed — you cannot show me the object that fails to exist — and it cannot be tabled: P02's table checks one candidate per row, and the candidates never run out. The good news: the opposite of a negative is a positive, an existence, and an existence has handles. Assume some rational does square to \(2\), and you may put it in lowest terms, square it, factor it — compute with it until it breaks something you already own. The bad news, stated up front: the machine only certifies, it never constructs; the absurdity must collide with a proven fact, not with your surprise; and the assumption must be the claim's true opposite — one quantifier slipped and the proof, however pretty, proves nothing. This page teaches the ignition, the cornering, and the three ways the machine jams.

2 · Meet the problem

Here is a claim you already believe, and a proof you almost certainly have not met in this form. The claim: no fraction \(\frac{p}{q}\) of whole numbers satisfies \(\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)^2 = 2\) — no matter how enormous you allow \(p\) and \(q\) to become. Note what the claim refuses to offer you: no example, since the claim says examples do not exist; no boundary, since no matter how large a \(q\) you rule in, the claim already covers it. Try anyway — P02's instinct, and the right one. \(\frac{7}{5}\) squares to \(\frac{49}{25}\): \(49\) against \(2 \cdot 25 = 50\), a whisker short. \(\frac{17}{12}\) squares to \(\frac{289}{144}\): \(289\) against \(2 \cdot 144 = 288\), a whisker over. \(\frac{41}{29}\): \(1681\) against \(1682\), short again. \(\frac{99}{70}\): \(9801\) against \(9800\), over again. The tries alternate low, high, low, high and close in — and every single one misses. The table could run for a lifetime and prove nothing: a detector, not a proof, exactly as P10 taught you to say.

Now grant the enemy everything. Suppose some fraction does square to \(2\) — some \(p\) and \(q\), positive whole numbers, with \(\frac{p}{q}\) written in lowest terms, because a fraction that squares to \(2\) can be reduced until it cannot be reduced further, and we may as well watch that one. Then:

\[p^2 \;=\; 2q^2.\]

One line of parity finishes it. \(p^2 = 2q^2\) is even, and an even square means an even root — odd numbers square to odd numbers, since \((2r+1)^2 = 4r^2 + 4r + 1\) is one more than a multiple of \(2\). So \(p = 2k\) for some whole number \(k\). Substitute: \(4k^2 = 2q^2\), so \(q^2 = 2k^2\) — even again — so \(q\) is even too. But now both \(p\) and \(q\) are even, and we began with a fraction in lowest terms. The one thing we chose at the start is the thing the assumption destroys: a contradiction, and the assumption's funeral. No fraction squares to \(2\), proven for all candidates at once, without meeting a single one.

Check the edges — P03's discipline, aimed at our own proof. Could the cornering spare a fraction with \(p\) and \(q\) both odd? No: \(p^2 = 2q^2\) has an even left side, and odd squares are odd, so the odd case dies on arrival. Could the claim itself be too strong? No — and here is the honest census: for every denominator \(q\) from \(1\) to \(10{,}000\), the nearest numerators \(p\) were checked against \(p^2 = 2q^2\) directly, and not one satisfies it — ten thousand misses, the alternation low–high never breaking. And the claim stops exactly where it should: \(4\) is a square, and \(\frac{2}{1}\) squares to it; \(9\) is a square, and \(\frac{3}{2}\) squares to \(\frac{9}{4}\). The fractions that square to a whole number are precisely the square roots of squares — §5 will show a student's proof that forgets this boundary, and Exercise 5 makes the boundary itself the theorem.

And the honest fine print, paid up front: the proof never exhibits the fraction it refutes — there is none to exhibit. It never tells you how close \(\frac{99}{70}\) gets, never produces an error bound, never explains why the misses alternate low and high (a beautiful story, but a different page's). It certifies a universal refusal, in seven lines, and stops there.

3 · Name the move

What the cornering taught, stated once and for all.

Pattern P11 — Prove by contradiction. When the claim is a negative — no object of some kind exists, no attempt can succeed, there is no largest — or when the direct route offers nothing to build with, do not attack the claim: attack its opposite. (1) Ignite: write the negation precisely — quantifiers flipped, the assumed object named — and grant it as a working hypothesis. The negation of a negative is an existence, and an existence has handles: you may put it in canonical form, compute with it, squeeze it. (2) Corner: derive consequences honestly, hunting for a collision with something you already own — a proven fact, a definition you imposed at the start, or the assumption's own tail. (3) Explode: when two statements that cannot both hold are both in your hand, the hypothesis is dead: no hypothesis, no negation, and the claim stands. The two collision types are worth naming: a self-contradiction — the assumption defeats itself, as the fraction that reduced and did not reduce — and an external contradiction — the assumption breaks a theorem already on the ladder, as a "largest prime" breaks the arithmetic of remainders. Both kill cleanly; neither is a mere oddity.

Or, in words: to prove that something cannot exist, pretend it does — honestly, precisely, with its name written down — and follow the consequences until they crash. The crash is the proof. But the machine certifies only: it hands you no example, no bound, no method, because the entire content of the theorem is that the object is not there to be handed. And the machine is only as honest as its ignition and its crash: assume something the claim did not deny, or crash into a fact you never proved, and you have built a proof of nothing.

Box P11.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The negation must be the claim's true opposite. "No fraction squares to \(2\)" negates to "some fraction squares to \(2\)" — an existence you can hold. "Every prime is odd" negates to "some prime is even" — and writing "every prime is even" instead is a different, refutable statement that refutes nothing about the claim. What breaks: the straw negation — a proof that destroys a claim nobody made; block 5's first exhibit ends a whole argument on one slipped quantifier.
  2. The absurdity must be a genuine collision. Two statements that cannot both hold — "lowest terms" against "both even", or a derived fact against a theorem already proven on this ladder. A result that is merely surprising, huge, or ugly is not a contradiction; it is a discovery wearing a contradiction's coat. What breaks: the non-absurdity — the proof that announces victory at the moment it merely raised its eyebrows; block 5's third exhibit charges the full price for one misread number.
  3. The conclusion must be exactly the claim. The explosion kills the negation; the claim is exactly what remains — no more. A proof that refutes "some fraction squares to \(2\)" has proved "no fraction squares to \(2\)", and not one word about cube roots, sums, or decimals unless those were in the claim. What breaks: the scope shift — a correct explosion billed as a wider theorem; block 5's fourth exhibit watches a real contradiction pay for an unearned upgrade.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: a negative, a nonexistence, a "no largest", an impossibility — or a "for all" so wide that no direct survey can visit its cases. Problem shape: nothing to construct — the direct route has no handles — while the negation, once granted, would have obvious handles: a specific object in canonical form, a finite list, an assumed figure. Tools at hand: at least one established fact on the ladder for the consequences to collide with — parity, a theorem of angles, the division algorithm — or an internal constraint the assumption itself violates. If the claim asks you to find something, remember that this move never finds; it only guarantees that the refusers are gone. And if neither the claim nor its negation can be made to break, hold your fire — §5's last exhibit is about knowing when the machine will not start at all.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, three domains, one pose: the assumption that cannot survive its own consequences. In each, the ignition is the whole art — the crash is one line.

Example 1 (numbers) — the list that cannot be complete

The claim: there is no largest prime. A direct proof would need to produce primes forever; we produce nothing. Instead: suppose the list of all primes were complete and finite — \(p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k\), every prime on it. The assumption hands us an object with handles: a finite list can be multiplied together.

\[N \;=\; p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k \;+\; 1.\]

Now two established facts collide with the list. First, \(N > 1\), and every whole number greater than \(1\) has a prime factor — that is owned on this ladder. Second, dividing \(N\) by any \(p_i\) leaves remainder \(1\): \(N = (p_1 \cdots p_k) + 1\) is one more than a multiple of each prime on the list, by B01's division algorithm. So the prime factor of \(N\) — which exists — divides no \(p_i\), and is therefore a prime off the list. The list was complete; the list was missing a prime. Contradiction, and no largest prime exists.

Run the machine on real numbers, and watch a folklore error die honestly. Multiply the first five primes and add one: \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 + 1 = 2311\) — prime, and off any shorter list. But the sixth run breaks a legend: \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 + 1 = 30031\), and \(30031 = 59 \times 509\) — not prime. Euclid's number \(N\) is not guaranteed to be prime, and Euclid never claimed it was: the proof needs only that \(N\)'s prime factors lie off the list, and \(59\) and \(509\) are both primes no "first six primes" list contains. The census confirms the machine across its first twelve runs — every product-plus-one carries a prime factor outside its own list, composite or not.

Moves used: P11 (the complete-list assumption), B01 (remainders: \(N\) is one more than a multiple of each listed prime), P01 (pinning the claim's grammar: "no largest" negates to "a complete finite list").

Example 2 (algebra) — the exponent that cannot exist

The claim: \(\log_2 3\) is irrational — no fraction of whole numbers equals the exponent that lifts \(2\) to \(3\). The negation hands us \(p\) and \(q\), positive whole numbers, with \(\log_2 3 = \frac{p}{q}\). An equation with handles: raise \(2\) to both sides.

\[2^{p/q} = 3 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 2^p = 3^q.\]

The left side is even — \(2\) raised to any positive power is even — and the right side is odd — \(3\)'s powers are \(3, 9, 27, 81, \ldots\), every one odd. Even equals odd: contradiction, immediately, with no computation done to \(p\) and \(q\) beyond naming them. The fractions get close — \(\frac{19}{12}\) gives \(2^{19} = 524{,}288\) against \(3^{12} = 531{,}441\), a gap of only \(7{,}153\) — and closeness is exactly what closeness always is here: a detector's comfort, never a proof. The parity wall stops every fraction at once.

Sharpness, and the honest boundary: \(\log_2 4 = 2\) is rational, and nothing is contradicted — because \(4\) is a power of \(2\), the wall's one gate. The census over bases and targets up to \(30\) confirms the shape: \(\log_a b\) is rational exactly when \(b\) is a power of \(a\) — the pairs \((2,4), (2,8), (2,16), (3,9), (3,27), (4,16), (5,25)\), and no others in that range. The move proved the wall; the census drew its one gate.

Moves used: P11 (the assumed fraction, cornered by parity), P01 (the claim's grammar: a nonexistence, so ignite an existence), P02 (the near-miss table that showed the fractions pressing against a wall it could not see).

Example 3 (geometry) — the triangle that cannot close

The claim: a triangle has at most one right angle. The negation hands us a figure: a triangle \(ABC\) with right angles at \(A\) and at \(B\). Draw it — the assumption is a picture now, and pictures can be squeezed.

Two crashes, one external and one internal, and watching both is the point. The external: the angles sum to \(90^\circ + 90^\circ + C = 180^\circ + C\), which exceeds \(180^\circ\) for every possible \(C\) — and the angle-sum theorem of triangles is already on the ladder, proven long before this page. The assumed figure collides with a standing theorem. The internal, worse: draw it honestly. Base \(AB\); the third vertex \(C\) sits above it; the side from \(A\) toward \(C\) and the side from \(B\) toward \(C\). A right angle at \(A\) makes \(AC\) perpendicular to \(AB\); a right angle at \(B\) makes \(BC\) perpendicular to \(AB\) as well — and two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel, another standing fact. Parallel lines never meet. But \(AC\) and \(BC\) are supposed to meet at \(C\). The assumed figure defeats itself: the vertex \(C\) that both sides must share is a point that perpendiculars refuse to produce.

Sharpness — the boundary is exactly one. A triangle with one right angle is the healthiest object in school geometry: the \(3\)-\(4\)-\(5\) triangle carries angles of \(90^\circ\), \(36.87^\circ\) and \(53.13^\circ\), and no theorem complains. The census agrees over three hundred random integer triangles: none carries two angles of \(90^\circ\) or more — for the blunt reason that two such angles already sum to at least \(180^\circ\), leaving the third angle nothing to be. One right angle: life. Two: contradiction. The claim's edge is exact.

Moves used: P11 (the assumed two-right-angle figure), P03 (the one-right-angle control that certifies where the impossibility begins).

5 · When the move fails

P03's discipline applies to the move itself: it jams, in five named shapes, and naming the shape is the diagnosis. The first two corrupt the ignition. The next two corrupt the crash. The last is the machine's honest silence. Every exhibit below was run, not asserted.

The straw negation. The claim on trial: "every prime number is odd." A student fires back: "Assume the opposite — every prime is even. But \(7\) is prime and odd, contradiction — so every prime is odd, as claimed." Everything after the ignition is fine, and the ignition is fatal: the true negation of "every prime is odd" is "some prime is even", not "every prime is even". The student refuted a statement nobody made. Worse — the true negation is correct: the prime \(2\) is even, so the claim itself is false, and no proof could ever have saved it. One slipped quantifier destroyed the proof and masked the counterexample. Mitigation: write the negation down with its quantifiers flipped explicitly — "every" becomes "some", "some" becomes "every" — before granting it. Price: a straw negation is invisible in prose; it reads exactly like the real thing. Default: no written negation, no proof.

The smuggled step. The classic: let \(a = b\). Then \(a^2 = ab\), so \(a^2 - b^2 = ab - b^2\), so \((a+b)(a-b) = b(a-b)\), so — dividing both sides by \(a-b\) — \(a+b = b\). Take \(a = b = 1\): \(2 = 1\). Contradiction — so \(a = b\) is impossible? The absurdity was blamed on an assumption, but \(a = b\) was a definition, granted freely at the start; the criminal is the division, because \(a - b = 0\) and division by zero is not a step. Run the chain at \(a = b = 1\) and watch each line: \(1 = 1\), \(0 = 0\), \(0 = 0\), then the division by \(1 - 1\) — and only then \(2 = 1\). The absurdity was manufactured, not derived, and a machine with one smuggled step can "prove" anything, including the opposite of what you wanted. Mitigation: audit every algebraic move for the operations that bite — division by an expression that might vanish, square roots of negatives, taking inverses that may not exist. Price: the crash looks precisely as decisive as a genuine one; only the ledger distinguishes them. Default: re-run the derivation on a case you trust; if the absurdity still appears, the step is the criminal, not the assumption.

The non-absurdity. "Assume the claim is false; then the population of bacteria after \(68\) doublings is \(2^{68} = 295{,}147{,}905{,}179{,}352{,}825{,}856\) — nearly three hundred quintillion — which is absurd. Contradiction!" It is nothing of the kind. \(2^{68}\) is a perfectly honest whole number; big is not broken, and surprise is not contradiction. The number was computed, not collided: no proven fact was violated, and the assumption walks away unharmed. Absurdity has a precise meaning — two statements that cannot both hold — and "this seems unbelievable" is not one of the pair. Mitigation: name the collision in writing: statement one, statement two, and the reason they cannot both hold. If you cannot fill in the second statement, you have a discovery, not a proof. Price: the temptation peaks exactly where intuition is weakest — huge numbers, unfamiliar objects. Default: no named collision, no explosion.

The unanchored crash. A proof of \(\sqrt{2}\)'s irrationality by decimals: "Assume \(\sqrt{2}\) is rational; every rational has a repeating decimal expansion; but \(\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\ldots\) visibly never repeats — contradiction." The anchor is fake: no finite string of digits can certify that a decimal never repeats — "no repetition so far" is not "no repetition ever", and the page offering the proof never established the never. The honest census makes the point with a fraction: \(\frac{1}{97}\) has a decimal period of exactly \(96\) digits — its first thirty digits show no repeating block, yet it repeats as faithfully as any fraction does. A proof that crashes into an unproven "obvious" has not crashed into anything; it has leaned on decoration. Mitigation: the collision partner must be a theorem already owned on this ladder — anchored, never hand-waved. Price: eyeball anchors feel strongest exactly where they are weakest — long decimals, many digits, vague infinities. Default: if the second statement of the collision is not itself proven, the proof is not finished.

The unreturned verdict. Two faces of the machine's silence. The stalled hunt: consequences multiply, corners turn, nothing collides — and the honest exit is to stop, because a contradiction hunt that runs past its natural corners is usually the wrong tool: the claim may be true with a construction waiting elsewhere, or false with a counterexample hiding where you are not looking. The unreturned certificate: even at full success, the machine hands back no object — the \(\sqrt{2}\) proof exhibits no fraction (there is none), Euclid's proof names no "next" prime (the factors of \(30031\) are new, but which primes they are was not the theorem's business). Suppose you hand the machine a list that secretly already contains \(59\) and \(509\) — say \(2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 59, 509\) — and run it: the product-plus-one is \(901{,}830{,}931\), and its prime factors are still new, but the machine could not have told you that without the whole list first. Certify, yes; construct, no. Mitigation: read the claim's grammar before billing the result: "there is no" is fully paid by a contradiction; "find one" is only half paid. Price: the certificate is final and empty-handed, every time. Default: prove the impossibility here; build the object elsewhere — and never bill one as the other.

Scope, honestly. This move proves a claim true by proving its negation impossible — a certificate that leans on the law of excluded middle: either the claim or its negation holds. For finite claims nobody disputes the lever; for claims over infinite collections a serious minority tradition — the constructivists, following Brouwer — declines some of these proofs, holding that "the negation of the negation" is weaker than "the positive". The Atlas works classically and says so here, once. And the move's own ledger stays short: it certifies, never constructs; it is only as strong as the facts it collides with; and it dies by the five shapes above — a slipped quantifier, a smuggled step, a misread size, an unproven anchor, a silence billed as a verdict.

6 · What to try next

The assumption has exploded — or the machine jammed, and the jam itself is information. Each ending has a door.

P12 — name the obstruction. A successful contradiction often runs on a quantity that refuses to change — the parity census that cornered \(\sqrt{2}\) is a refusal of B02's kind. When the refusing quantity blocks a whole family of claims at once, promote it from a proof device to a named obstruction: that promotion is P12's first move, and this page is its front door.

P13 — infinite descent. The engine under Fig P11.1 has a name of its own: both \(p\) and \(q\) even means the same equation at half size, and an endless descending chain of positive whole numbers cannot exist. P13 trains that engine deliberately on harder game — and it owes this page its ignition.

P14 — the diagonal argument. Contradiction at the edge of infinity: assume the list of all real numbers is complete, then build the one number that differs from every listed entry — the assumption of completeness defeats itself, at uncountable scale. The diagonal is this page fused with P10, and it rewires what "impossible" means when the room is endless.

P67 — prove the contrapositive. The twin move, often the honest cheaper route: "if \(A\) then \(B\)" is exactly "if not \(B\) then not \(A\)", and many contradiction proofs of an "if-then" are secretly contrapositive proofs wearing a heavier coat. When you notice yours is one of them, strip the coat: the twin is shorter, cleaner, and easier to check.

M01 — \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational, all moves at once. The page you just read, replayed as a multi-pattern problem: canonical form, contradiction, and descent, each named at the moment it works — the same proof, worn three ways.

Deferred honestly, with reasons. Two deep relatives of this move wait up the ladder, and both need pages you have not climbed. Arithmetize the syntax (P34) turns contradiction into a self-referential machine — a statement that says of itself "I cannot be proved", Gödel's engine — and it needs the diagonal argument (P14) first. Build two worlds (P35) marks the move's honest outer limit: statements that are neither provable nor refutable, where the excluded middle survives for truth but not for proof. Both are this page's descendants; neither is reachable yet, and pretending otherwise would violate the ladder's one law.

7 · Lineage

The move is old enough to have a legend and a logician attached. The legend: Hippasus of Metapontum, a Pythagorean, revealed that \(\sqrt{2}\) is no ratio of whole numbers — and was drowned at sea for breaking the brotherhood's creed that "all is number". The sources telling this story are centuries late, and historians treat it exactly as it deserves: a legend, kept because it dramatizes a real revolution, not because it happened. The logician: Aristotle, in the Prior Analytics, named and classified the figure — "reduction to the impossible" — and Euclid used it in the Elements for the infinity of the primes, Book IX, Proposition 20. The modern quarrel is the one this page's scope box mentioned: in the 1920s L. E. J. Brouwer rejected the law of excluded middle for infinite totalities, David Hilbert defended classical mathematics, and the dispute shaped the foundations of the century that followed. None of this history is load-bearing here — but when a proof begins "suppose not", it is running the eleventh move of the spine, exactly as Aristotle catalogued it.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the sum that stays strange

Prove that a rational number plus an irrational number is always irrational — say \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\). Then the receipt: among all fractions with denominator at most \(10{,}000\), how close does the closest one come?

Hint

Ignite: what exactly must you assume to make the claim false? You need one rational \(r\) and one irrational \(x\) whose sum is rational. What does the arithmetic of rationals — subtracting a rational from a rational — already let you conclude about \(x\)?

Worked answer

Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\) were rational. Subtracting the rational \(\frac{1}{3}\) from a rational leaves a rational — the rationals are closed under subtraction, since \(\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad - bc}{bd}\) is again a ratio of whole numbers. So \(\sqrt{2} = \left(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\right) - \frac{1}{3}\) would be rational — and it is not: §2 cornered it. Contradiction; the sum is irrational. The shape is the general one: a one-move transfer, with §2's theorem as the wall. The receipt: the closest fraction with denominator at most \(10{,}000\) is \(\frac{16385}{9376} \approx 1.7475469\), and it still misses \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2} \approx 1.7475469\) by about \(3.26 \times 10^{-8}\) — detectors close in, and the wall stands.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the second wall

Prove that \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational. The parity census that cornered \(\sqrt{2}\) is not enough here — \(3\) is odd — so find the wall's local equivalent: prove first that a whole number whose square is divisible by \(3\) is itself divisible by \(3\), then run the corner.

Hint

B02 built the wall: what are the classes of \(n\), \(n^2\) modulo \(3\) for \(n \equiv 0, 1, 2\)?

Worked answer

The lemma first, by B02's classes: if \(n \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) then \(n^2 \equiv 0\); if \(n \equiv 1\) then \(n^2 \equiv 1\); if \(n \equiv 2\) then \(n^2 \equiv 4 \equiv 1\). So \(n^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) happens only in the first class — divisible by \(3\) means divisible by \(3\), squared. The census over \(n \le 400\) agrees: \(n^2\) divisible by \(3\) exactly when \(n\) is. Now the corner: suppose \(\frac{p}{q} = \sqrt{3}\) in lowest terms, so \(p^2 = 3q^2\). Then \(3\) divides \(p^2\), so \(3\) divides \(p\): write \(p = 3k\). Then \(9k^2 = 3q^2\), so \(3k^2 = q^2\), so \(3\) divides \(q^2\), so \(3\) divides \(q\). Both \(p\) and \(q\) divisible by \(3\) — the fraction was not in lowest terms. Contradiction, and \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational. The census of denominators to \(10{,}000\) finds no \(p, q\) with \(p^2 = 3q^2\) — the wall's local shape, confirmed; and the same proof runs for any prime replacing \(3\), which is Exercise 5's engine.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the primes that refuse, three at a time

Prove that there are infinitely many primes of the form \(4k + 3\) — the primes \(3, 7, 11, 19, 23, \ldots\) that leave remainder \(3\) on division by \(4\). Euclid's machine from Example 1 needs a modification, and finding it is the exercise.

Hint

Suppose \(3, 7, 11, 19, \ldots, p_k\) were all of them, and consider \(N = 4 \cdot (p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1\). What remainder does \(N\) leave on division by \(4\)? And what is the remainder of a product of numbers each leaving remainder \(1\)?

Worked answer

Suppose the list \(p_1 = 3, p_2 = 7, p_3 = 11, p_4 = 19, \ldots, p_k\) contained every prime of the form \(4k + 3\), and set \(N = 4 \cdot (p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1\). Then \(N \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and \(N > 1\), so \(N\) has a prime factor. No \(p_i\) divides \(N\) — each leaves remainder \(-1\), not \(0\). Could every prime factor of \(N\) leave remainder \(1\) or \(2\) on division by \(4\)? Not remainder \(2\): one factor of \(2\) would make \(N\) even, and \(N\) is odd. Not all remainder \(1\): a product of numbers each \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is itself \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — since \((4a+1)(4b+1) = 16ab + 4a + 4b + 1\) — but \(N \equiv 3\). So some prime factor of \(N\) is \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and it is none of the \(p_i\): the complete list was missing one. Contradiction. Run the machine concretely: with the first four such primes, \(N = 4 \cdot (3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 19) - 1 = 17555 = 5 \times 3511\), and \(3511 = 4 \cdot 877 + 3\) is prime — exactly the promised kind, and not on the list. The census confirms the machine for the first six list lengths, every run producing a fresh \(4k+3\) prime. Note the honest asymmetry: the same argument fails for \(4k+1\) primes, whose infinity needs a different engine — obstructions are family-specific, which is P12's opening theme.

Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

The claim on trial: "\(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is irrational." Three students hand in proofs. Student A: "Irrational plus irrational is irrational; \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{3}\) are both irrational; done." Student B: "Suppose \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is rational. Then \(s^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\), so \(\sqrt{6} = \frac{s^2 - 5}{2}\) would be rational; write \(\sqrt{6} = \frac{a}{b}\) in lowest terms and \(a^2 = 6b^2\) forces \(3 \mid a\) and \(3 \mid b\) — contradiction." Student C: "Suppose \(s\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{3} = s - \sqrt{2}\), so \(3 = s^2 - 2s\sqrt{2} + 2\), so \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{s^2 - 1}{2s}\) is rational — contradiction." Referee all three: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

For A: test the inference form itself on \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(2 - \sqrt{2}\). For B: check the squaring \(s^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) numerically, then audit the corner: why does \(a^2 = 6b^2\) force \(3 \mid a\), and then \(3 \mid b\)? For C: check the algebra numerically, and ask what happens if \(s = 0\) — may it be?

Worked answer

Student A: rejected — the inference form is false. Irrational plus irrational is not always irrational: \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(2 - \sqrt{2}\) are both irrational, and their sum is exactly \(2\). A's conclusion happens to be true, and that is the trap: truth rescued by a broken inference is luck, not proof, and the same form "proves" that \(\sqrt{2} + (2 - \sqrt{2})\) is irrational — false. Student B: upheld. The squaring is exact: \((\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) — the receipt \(9.898979485566\) matches \(5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) to twelve digits. If \(s\) were rational, \(s^2\) and then \(\frac{s^2 - 5}{2}\) would be rational, making \(\sqrt{6}\) rational. The corner is Exercise 2's lemma twice over: \(a^2 = 6b^2\) makes \(3 \mid a^2\), so \(3 \mid a\); write \(a = 3m\), and \(9m^2 = 6b^2\) gives \(3m^2 = 2b^2\), so \(3 \mid 2b^2\), and since \(3\) divides neither \(2\) nor shares a factor with it, \(3 \mid b^2\), so \(3 \mid b\). Both \(a\) and \(b\) divisible by \(3\) contradicts lowest terms — so \(\sqrt{6}\) is irrational, so \(s\) cannot be rational. B's proof is a correct contrapositive-with-contradiction: it transfers \(s\)'s supposed rationality to \(\sqrt{6}\), then breaks it on a proven wall. Student C: upheld, with one audit. The algebra checks: \(\sqrt{3} = s - \sqrt{2}\) squares to \(3 = s^2 - 2s\sqrt{2} + 2\), so \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{s^2 - 1}{2s}\) — and the receipt agrees, \(\frac{s^2 - 1}{2s} = 1.414213562373\) at \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\). But the formula divides by \(s\), so the proof owes one line: \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \geq 1.4 + 1.7 > 0\), so the division is legal. With that line, C's proof stands — §5's smuggled-step audit, passed in advance. The referee's ledger: one broken form, one clean transfer, one clean transfer with its division certified — three verdicts, and only the receipts distinguish the honest two from the lucky one.

Exercise 5 ◐ — the hunt, the boundary, and the general wall

Compute first, conjecture second, prove last. For every \(n\) from \(2\) to \(100\): is \(\sqrt{n}\) rational? Tabulate the successes — there are exactly ten — and notice what the successful \(n\)'s have in common. Conjecture the full boundary, then prove it by contradiction: \(\sqrt{n}\) is rational exactly when \(n\) is a perfect square. Then explore outward: what is the corresponding statement for cube roots, and does your proof adapt?

Hint

For the proof direction that matters: suppose \(\sqrt{n} = \frac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms and \(n\) is not a perfect square. P05's wardrobe dresses \(n\) as \(n = a^2 b\) with \(b > 1\) squarefree — no square factor left in \(b\). What does \(p^2 = n q^2 = a^2 b q^2\) force on \(p\), and then on \(q\)?

Worked answer

The census first: the ten successes are \(\sqrt{4}, \sqrt{9}, \sqrt{16}, \ldots, \sqrt{100}\) — the perfect squares, roots \(2\) through \(10\) — and the ninety failures resist a direct search: for every non-square \(n \le 100\) and every \(q \le 200\), no \(p\) makes \(p^2 = n q^2\); that is eighteen thousand checks, all misses, and the detector never turns up a single success outside the squares. The proof: clearly a perfect square has a rational root. Conversely, suppose \(\sqrt{n} = \frac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms with \(n\) not a perfect square. Dress \(n\) by P05: \(n = a^2 b\) with \(b\) squarefree, and \(b > 1\) — the leftover non-square. Then \(p^2 = a^2 b q^2\). First, \(a \mid p\): in \(p^2\) every prime appears an even number of times — twice its count in \(p\) — and \(p^2\) contains all the copies that \(a^2\) carries, so if \(a\) carries \(e\) copies of a prime \(r\), then \(p\) carries at least \(e\) of them. Write \(p = a p_1\); the equation slims to \(p_1^2 = b q^2\). Now pick any prime \(r\) dividing \(b\) — there is one, since \(b > 1\). The lemma of Exercise 2 gives \(r \mid p_1^2\), so \(r \mid p_1\); write \(p_1 = r p_2\). Because \(b\) is squarefree, \(b = r c\) with \(\gcd(r, c) = 1\), and substituting turns \(r^2 p_2^2 = r c q^2\) into \(r p_2^2 = c q^2\). The left side is a multiple of \(r\), so \(r \mid c q^2\); since \(\gcd(r, c) = 1\), the lemma applies once more: \(r \mid q^2\), so \(r \mid q\). But \(r\) divides \(p\) too — \(p = a r p_2\) — so \(r\) divides both \(p\) and \(q\), and the fraction was not in lowest terms. Contradiction: no non-square has a rational square root. The cube-root exploration: the census finds rational cube roots exactly at the perfect cubes \(1, 8, 27, 64, 125\) up to \(200\), and the proof adapts word for word — dress \(n = a^3 b\) with \(b\) cubefree, and the same transfer forces \(b \mid p\) and \(b \mid q\). The general wall stands: \(\sqrt[\,m]{n}\) is rational exactly when \(n\) is a perfect \(m\)-th power — one engine, every root, and the engine is this page's.
