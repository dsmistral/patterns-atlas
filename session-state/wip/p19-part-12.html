8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 (●○○). (a) Rebuild the first three rows of Example 1's table yourself, as exact fractions: for \(k = 4, 5, 6\), compute the expected number of monochromatic \(K_k\)'s in \(K_n\) at \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\), and check each is below \(1\). (b) The census behind Exhibit A: prove that the triangle-free two-colorings of \(K_5\) are exactly twelve, by showing the red graph must be a five-cycle and counting the labeled five-cycles. Confirm the count against Exhibit A's \(12\) of \(1024\).

Hint

(a) The count is \(\binom{n}{k} \cdot 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}\); the table's \(n\)-values are \(4, 5, 8\). (b) First show every vertex has red degree exactly \(2\): a vertex with three red neighbors forces a blue triangle among them, and a vertex with three blue neighbors forces a red triangle. Then ask what a graph on five vertices looks like when every degree is \(2\) — and count its labeled shapes.

Worked

(a) \(k = 4\): \(\binom{4}{4} \cdot 2^{1-6} = 2^{-5} = \frac{1}{32}\). \(k = 5\): \(\binom{5}{5} \cdot 2^{1-10} = 2^{-9} = \frac{1}{512}\). \(k = 6\): \(\binom{8}{6} \cdot 2^{1-15} = 28 \cdot 2^{-14} = \frac{28}{16384} = \frac{7}{4096}\). All three below \(1\) — and all three smaller than Example 1's bound \(\frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}\), exactly as a floor should be. (b) Fix a vertex \(v\). If \(v\) has three red neighbors, those three pairwise edges cannot contain a red one (with \(v\) they would make a red triangle), so all three are blue — a blue triangle. So the red degree is at most \(2\); swapping colors bounds the blue degree by \(2\), hence the red degree is at least \(4 - 2 = 2\). Every red degree is exactly \(2\): the red graph is a single cycle through all five vertices — a triangle would violate triangle-freeness, so no \(3\)-cycle detour is available. A labeled five-cycle is fixed by a cyclic order of the five vertices, and each cycle is counted once per starting point and once per direction: \(\frac{5!}{5 \cdot 2} = \frac{4!}{2} = 12\). Twelve good colorings, matching the census of Exhibit A exactly — and each one's blue edges form the complementary five-cycle, the pentagon seating of \S2.

Exercise 2 (●●○). Exhibit C's redesign, in your own hands. (a) Run the fair-coin count for \(R(3,4)\) at \(n = 5\): red triangles and blue four-cliques, and show it overflows. (b) Bias the coin to \(p = 0.4\) and show the expected count drops below \(1\), certifying \(R(3,4) > 5\). (c) The truth is \(R(3,4) = 9\), witnessed by the eight-person seating of Exhibit C. Verify its two properties yourself: show the red graph (the cycle of eight plus the four opposite chords) contains no triangle, and — the part that is genuinely work — explain why checking all \(\binom{8}{3} = 56\) triples and all \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\) four-sets is exactly the machine-census job of P25, not a job for this page's coin.

Hint

(a) \(10\) red-triangle events at \(p^3\), \(5\) blue four-clique events at \((1-p)^6\). (b) Compute \(10 \cdot 0.4^3\) and \(5 \cdot 0.6^6\) separately, then add. (c) For the triangle claim, notice the red graph is triangle-free because a cycle plus opposite chords cannot close a triangle: every red edge connects vertices at distance \(1\) or \(4\) around the cycle, and three such steps cannot return home. For the second half, count the checks: what does the coin's E-value verify, and what does the census verify?

Worked

(a) \(10 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 + 5 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^6 = 1.25 + 0.078125 = 1.328125 > 1\): the fair coin overflows, the method is silent, and nothing about parties of five follows — Exhibit A's ambiguity all over again. (b) \(10 \cdot 0.4^3 = 10 \cdot 0.064 = 0.64\); \(5 \cdot 0.6^6 = 5 \cdot 0.046656 = 0.23328\). Sum \(0.87328 < 1\): the average count is below one, so some seating of five has no red triangle and no blue four-clique — \(R(3,4) > 5\), from a coin whose bias you chose. Note what did the work: shrinking the red-triangle term (the dominant one) by making red rare, at the price of growing the blue term — the design trade-off is the exercise. (c) The red graph is the cycle \(C_8\) (edges between vertices at cyclic distance \(1\)) plus the four chords joining opposite vertices (distance \(4\)). A triangle would be three vertices mutually at distances \(1\) or \(4\); but \(1 + 1 = 2\), \(1 + 4 = 5\) (distance \(3\) the other way), and \(4 + 4 = 0\): no sum of two allowed distances lands on an allowed distance, so no triangle closes. The blue four-clique claim is the complement side: every four-set must contain at least one red edge — \(70\) four-sets, each inspected. And here is the boundary between the moves: the coin certifies that some seating exists somewhere in a room of \(2^{28}\) colorings of \(K_8\); verifying this particular named seating is a finite, concrete, boring census — \(56 + 70 = 126\) checks — which is P25's department, deterministic and by name. Existence is the coin's job; verification of a named witness is the machine's.
