The Patterns Atlas · B08 · bridge page, group B · before P16

Diagonalization: decoupling by relabelling

One feed of a machine is a column walk; a hundred feeds look like a hundred tangles. Relabel the plane by the machine's own rails and the tangles dissolve: each rail-reading stretches by its own factor and never speaks to the other. This page names that relabelling — diagonalization — reaps its harvest (powers, orbits, closed forms), and says honestly which machines refuse it.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it is the fourth and last page of group B — the coordinates block. B05 built the measuring scheme: a basis is a pair of directions, a reading is a pair of numbers. B06 built the translation between two schemes: the dictionary, its reverse, and the determinant as the exchange rate for areas. B07 found the machine's own directions — the rails — and filed them as a scheme. This page reaps what those three sowed: relabelled into that scheme, a coupled machine becomes two uncoupled one-number stretches, and its whole future — every power, every orbit — collapses into short formulas. The name for the move is diagonalization; the box it buys is the block's last; and the two honest failures (one rail, or none) get their own worked section. All four pages stand behind P16, the pattern that solves a problem by re-dressing it: to re-dress a problem that wears a machine is exactly this relabelling. You already own every tool on the page: the rails and the hunt (B07), the dictionaries both ways (B06), the health test (B05). Take a pencil; you will feed one point three times the hard way, and watch the same computation collapse, before you meet a single new word.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

The island is back, and so is the harbourmaster's distortion machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), still on the office desk: feed it a map point, out comes a new one. Last page the office hunted the machine's two rails — the red diagonal \((1,1)\), stretched \(\times 4\), and the blue cove arrow \((1,-2)\), stretched \(\times 1\) and so not moved at all — and filed them as the machine's own measuring scheme. Today's question is the one the office has been putting off. The storm is not finished: the coastline machine now runs every year, each spring eating the whole map and returning the next year's. The harbourmaster wants the map of spring 3 — and of spring 10 — without walking the machine one year at a time.

Task: feed the point \((3,0)\) three years running, by hand, in school numbers. One feed is the column walk B06 taught; one point per year is all the office asks. Year by year, on paper:

\[M(3,0) \;=\; 3\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 0\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (9,6),\]

\[M(9,6) \;=\; 9\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 6\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (27+6,\; 18+12) \;=\; (33,30),\]

\[M(33,30) \;=\; 33\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 30\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (99+30,\; 66+60) \;=\; (129,126).\]

Three feeds, six grown multiplications, and the numbers couple at every step: both entries of each answer hear from both entries of the question. Now put the pencil down and read the four points again: \((3,0)\), \((9,6)\), \((33,30)\), \((129,126)\). Something never moved. Subtract within each point: \(3-0\), \(9-6\), \(33-30\), \(129-126\) — the answer is \(3\), four times running. The tangle has a spine: some one-number story the machine is protecting while it scrambles everything else.

Now the same three years, done the rails way. Split the starting point on the machine's two rails — find the step-counts \(a, b\) with \((3,0) = a\cdot(1,-2) + b\cdot(1,1)\). Two little equations: \(a + b = 3\) along the first coordinate, \(-2a + b = 0\) along the second, so \(b = 2a\) and \(3a = 3\). Whole numbers: \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\). The point is one blue step plus two red steps,

\[(3,0) \;=\; 1\cdot(1,-2) \;+\; 2\cdot(1,1).\]

And a machine that only stretches rail arrows cannot scramble rail step-counts — that is what B07's rails are. The blue step sits on the \(\times 1\) rail: returned as itself, this year and every year. The red content \(2\cdot(1,1)\) sits on the \(\times 4\) rail: one year makes it \(8\cdot(1,1)\), two years \(32\cdot(1,1)\), three years \(128\cdot(1,1)\). Reassemble spring 3:

\[1\cdot(1,-2) \;+\; 128\cdot(1,1) \;=\; (129,126).\]

The same answer — and this route was barely arithmetic: multiply 2 by 4, three times. The protected \(x - y = 3\) is no longer mysterious either: it is the standing-still blue piece, whose \(x - y\) is \(1 - (-2) = 3\), wearing school clothes. Fig B08.1 draws the whole story.

What the pencil just showed. A coupled computation — both coordinates mixed at every step — became, in the right reading, two uncoupled one-number stories. That is the whole phenomenon, and it wants its law. When can a machine be read this way at all? B07 answered half of that: it must own two rails, and the discriminant says whether it does. What exactly does the rail reading buy for the machine itself — spring 10, spring 100, a formula for every spring at once? And what honest price does the relabelling charge, on the machines the door refuses? The next section names the move; the name is the block's last new word.

2 · The idea, named

First the floor, restated from B07 in one breath: the hunt finds the stretch factors by solving \(\det(M - \lambda I) = 0\), then the rails by solving \((M - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0\); two distinct factors give non-parallel rails, hence a scheme (B07's Claim 3); and the eigen-scheme's dictionary \(P\) — rails as columns — was health-checked, reversed, and run through the round trip on this very machine, where \(P^{-1}MP\) came out the stretch table \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). That one computed line is the new idea; what remains is to name it, and to reap what it buys.

Box B08 — diagonalization (decoupling by relabelling). A machine \(M\) is diagonalizable when some healthy dictionary \(P\) relabels it into a diagonal table:

\[P^{-1} M P \;=\; D \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix},\]

the stretch table: first reading stretched by \(\lambda_1\), second by \(\lambda_2\), no mixing. In words: to diagonalize a machine is to re-measure the plane in the machine's own directions, where the machine's coupled action reads as two independent one-number stories. The coupling never disappears — it lives in the dictionary and is paid once at each end of the trip; inside the scheme, the action itself is pure stretching.

Box B08.A — the harvest law. For a diagonalizable machine, for every whole number \(n\) of feeds:

\[M^n \;=\; P\,D^n\,P^{-1}, \qquad D^n \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^n & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^n \end{bmatrix}.\]

In words: \(n\) feeds cost one read, \(n\) one-number multiplications per reading, and one translate-home — the interior dictionaries annihilate each other pairwise (\(P^{-1}P = I\) at every seam), so a hundred coupled feeds collapse into two uncoupled stretch-counts. The law is also a closed form: the answer to "where after \(n\) feeds" written directly in \(n\), with no ladder of intermediate feeds to climb.

Four words get defined here, at first use:

  • New word: diagonal (table). A table whose off-corner entries are zero: it answers each unit-step question with a multiple of the same step, so it never mixes the two readings. \(\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}\) is the shape, and the name is plain description — the nonzero entries run down the corner-to-corner line, the diagonal. A diagonal table is two one-number stories filed side by side.
  • New word: diagonalizable. A machine is diagonalizable when some healthy relabelling makes it diagonal. For a real two-by-two machine that happens exactly when it owns two non-parallel rails — or already is a pure stretch \(\lambda I\) (section 4's Claim 3 pins the "exactly"). The adjective carries its own health warning: not every machine qualifies, and the failures are named below, not hidden.
  • New word: power of a machine. \(M^n\) is \(n\) feeds composed — the machine applied \(n\) times in a row. As a definition that is the end of the story; as a computation it is a tangle that grows with every year, until Box B08.A collapses it.
  • New word: closed form. A formula that answers a "what after \(n\) steps" question directly in \(n\). Lecture 0's year-by-year walks were the ladder; the harvest law is the closed form standing at the top. Closed forms are what the relabelling buys: not just cheaper arithmetic, but the whole future in one line.

Examples and non-examples, one per line, each checkable against the boxes:

  • The distortion machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\): diagonalizable, with \(D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\); section 3 prints its tenth power both ways, and a general formula for every power at once.
  • Identity and triple, \(I\) and \(3I\): diagonal already, in every scheme at once — for any healthy \(P\), \(P^{-1}(\lambda I)P = \lambda P^{-1}P = \lambda I\). The machines that need no relabelling; the tool honestly declines work that is already done.
  • The shear \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\): not diagonalizable — one rail, so no two-column dictionary exists to file. Its year-after-year story is still honest, and section 3(c) tells it without any dictionary.
  • The quarter-turn \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\): no real rails at all — nothing to relabel into. The complex completion stays exactly where B07 left it: named, and deferred.
  • The Fibonacci machine \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\): diagonalizable with irrational stretch factors — the harvest travels past integer machines, and Exercise 3 reaps from it the most famous closed form in the book.

The names carry a history worth one paragraph, none of it load-bearing. The most famous reaping predates matrices by six centuries: Fibonacci — Leonardo of Pisa — posed the rabbit problem in the Liber Abaci of 1202, and its monthly counts \(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \ldots\) are Exercise 3's machine in disguise. The closed form for those counts is called Binet's formula, after Jacques Binet, who published a proof in 1843 — but the formula was old news even then: Daniel Bernoulli had proved essentially the same result in 1726, Abraham de Moivre had it by 1730 in the first systematic treatment of linear recurrences (Donald Knuth traces the trail), and Euler published his own account in 1765 — an attribution late enough to star in any telling of Stigler's law of eponymy. And Binet deserves better than a borrowed name: he was one of the quiet founders of the very matrix algebra this page runs on, before Cayley — B06's law-office lawyer — wrote its rules. None of this carries the page's weight; the harvest works whether or not its history is flattering.

3 · Worked by hand

Three workings, simplest first, each carried to the end. Under all of them run Boxes B08 and B08.A, and every claim takes the return trip P78 teaches: computed one way, fed back the other.

(a) The harvest on the block's machine: \(M^{10}\), two ways

The harbourmaster's spring-10 map, without walking the machine year by year. The scheme route is three moves. Stretch table to the tenth: diagonal tables power corner-wise (section 4, Claim 2, proves it), so \(D^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4^{10} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1048576 \end{bmatrix}\). Assemble the round trip with letters first — the letters cost nothing extra and print every year at once:

\[M^{n} \;=\; P\,D^{n}\,P^{-1} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4^n \end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4^n \\ -2 & 4^n \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 + 2\cdot 4^n & -1 + 4^n \\ -2 + 2\cdot 4^n & 2 + 4^n \end{bmatrix}.\]

That is the machine's whole future in one line: \(M^n\) for every \(n\) at once, the answer to spring 3, spring 10, spring 100. Evaluate at the office's year:

\[M^{10} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2097153 & 1048575 \\ 2097150 & 1048578 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 699051 & 349525 \\ 699050 & 349526 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Three divisions by 3, all exact — and the exactness is no accident: \(4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\), so \(4^{10} \equiv 1\), and \(1 + 2\cdot 4^{10} \equiv 3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) — B01's arithmetic one floor down, certifying in advance that every division lands. The school route, for contrast and confirmation: square honestly, twice — \(M^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 11 & 5 \\ 10 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\), \(M^4 = \begin{bmatrix} 171 & 85 \\ 170 & 86 \end{bmatrix}\), \(M^8 = \begin{bmatrix} 43691 & 21845 \\ 43690 & 21846 \end{bmatrix}\) — then one last product:

\[M^{10} \;=\; M^8 M^2 \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 43691 & 21845 \\ 43690 & 21846 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 11 & 5 \\ 10 & 6 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 699051 & 349525 \\ 699050 & 349526 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Same answer. One route: four matrix multiplications with entries swelling toward a million. The other: three moves on the two small numbers \(1\) and \(4\). Both are honest; only one stays honest cheaply as the years grow — that is what "decoupling" buys, and the price of the ticket was one change of dictionary.

Two closing glances, both free with the letters. The trace: \(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl((1 + 2\cdot 4^n) + (2 + 4^n)\bigr) = 1 + 4^n\) — the trace of the \(n\)-th power is \(\lambda_1^n + \lambda_2^n\), Vieta's sum reading one floor up; at \(n = 10\): \(699051 + 349526 = 1048577 = 1 + 4^{10}\). The determinant: B06's multiplication law on the \(n\)-feed composition gives \(\det(M^n) = (\det M)^n = 4^n\) outright, and the closed form confirms it by letters — \(\tfrac{1}{9}\bigl((1 + 2\cdot 4^n)(2 + 4^n) - (4^n - 1)(2\cdot 4^n - 2)\bigr) = \tfrac{1}{9}\cdot 9\cdot 4^n = 4^n\) — and the printed table agrees at \(n = 10\): \(699051 \cdot 349526 - 349525 \cdot 699050 = 1048576 = 4^{10}\). The relabelled machine agrees with the machine on every number the block has learned to ask of it.

(b) One point's whole future: the orbit closed form, and the tilt

Section (a) answered for the machine; the office asks the same question for a single point. Take the reading of \((2,1)\) on the rails: \(P^{-1}(2,1) = \frac{1}{3}(2 - 1,\; 4 + 1) = \left(\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{5}{3}\right)\) — fractional step-counts, Elena's honesty from B06, and no less exact for being fractions. The harvest law says each step-count stretches by its own factor, every year, with no mixing (Claim 4 proves it; the pencil may anticipate):

\[(x_n, y_n) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{3}\,(1,-2) \;+\; \tfrac{5}{3}\cdot 4^n \,(1,1).\]

Check it against the year-by-year ladder — the return trip, every clause. At \(n = 0\): \(\left(\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{5}{3},\; -\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{5}{3}\right) = (2,1)\). At \(n = 1\) the formula gives \(\left(\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{20}{3},\; -\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{20}{3}\right) = (7,6)\), and one honest feed agrees: \(M(2,1) = (7,6)\). At \(n = 2\): \((27,26)\), and \(M(7,6) = (21+6,\; 14+12) = (27,26)\). At \(n = 3\): \((107,106)\), and \(M(27,26) = (81+26,\; 54+52) = (107,106)\). Four years, four agreements — and \(x - y = 1\) in every one, the school route's little mystery. It is accounted for now: the blue step-count \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) contributes \(\tfrac{1}{3}\cdot(1-(-2)) = 1\) to \(x - y\), the red rail contributes \(1 - 1 = 0\), and the blue count never changes. That is P09's find-the-invariant, arrived at by relabelling: the invariant was always the frozen rail's signature.

Two more orbits finish the census of the phenomenon. The point \((3,0)\) — Lecture 0's — reads \((1, 2)\), whole step-counts, and its formula \((1,-2) + 2\cdot 4^n (1,1)\) is the one the pencil already walked. The point \((-1, 2)\) reads \(\left(\frac{-1-2}{3}, \frac{-2+2}{3}\right) = (-1, 0)\): pure blue, zero red content, and the formula says it never moves at all — the grid census's two fixed arrows are exactly the blue rail's \(\pm(1,-2)\), now explained rather than counted. Every other point has red content, and red content quadruples. The tilt: divide the closed form by \(4^n\) and the blue share shrinks by 4 every year,

\[(x_n, y_n) \;=\; 4^n\left(\; \frac{1}{3\cdot 4^n}(1,-2) \;+\; \tfrac{5}{3}(1,1) \;\right),\]

so the orbit's direction closes in on the diagonal: the blue term \(\frac{1}{3 \cdot 4^n}\) can be made smaller than any fraction you name (Claim 4 makes that precise), and after twelve years every grid arrow with red content has its blue share below one part in two million. The island's long-run map, every arrow on it but the blue pair, points within a hair of the red rail. Fig B08.2 draws the march: each orbit runs up its own private road \(x - y = c\), one blue step off the diagonal — parallel to the red rail, because the blue piece is a constant.

(c) The door that sticks: the shear's honest years

Not every machine can be relabelled — the block has said so twice, and the shear \(S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) is the standing proof. Its hunt (B07's section 3(b)) returned one doubled factor \(\lambda = 1\) and one rail, east \((1,0)\); a dictionary needs two columns and the machine owns one direction, so there is nothing to file. The relabelling tool declines the job, and no honest page repairs that in secret. But the year-after-year question still deserves its answer, and here a direct look beats machinery. Feed once and read:

\[S(x, y) \;=\; (x + y,\; y).\]

The height never changes; each year adds the height to the east position. So \(n\) years add \(n\) times the height, and the machine's every future is one line:

\[S^n \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix},\]

proved the way such formulas always are — a one-line induction, the climbing move with its own page later in the book: \(S^{n+1} = S^n S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & n \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & n+1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Fig B08.3 draws the years: each point marches east along its own horizontal road, at speed equal to its height.

Put the two machines' futures side by side and read the price structure. Relabelled, \(M\)'s orbits grow by multiplication — \(\times 4\) a year, the harvest's signature — and everything has a closed form. The shear's grow by addition — \(+\,y\) a year, arithmetic progressions, linear and honest. Neither future is wrong; but the tangle-riddle of Lecture 0 has a one-line answer for \(M\) and no dictionary at all for \(S\), and the discriminant says so beforehand: two distinct real factors, or the door sticks. The relabelling is a tool, not a ritual — it serves exactly the machines that own two rails, and the next section pins that boundary as a claim with a proof.

4 · Why it is true

Four claims carry this page: the diagonal form is honest (a relabelling really does turn the machine into its stretch table), the harvest law holds for every whole number of feeds, the machines that can be relabelled are exactly the ones the block has been pointing at — and the orbit closed form, with the tilt, is a theorem and not a hope. All four proofs are complete; each leans on the block's earlier claims by citation, not by repetition.

Claim 1 — the diagonal form is honest. Let \(M\) own two non-parallel rails \(\vec v_1\) with factor \(\lambda_1\) and \(\vec v_2\) with \(\lambda_2\), and let \(P = [\,\vec v_1 \;\; \vec v_2\,]\) be the dictionary whose columns are the rails. Then \(MP = PD\), where \(D = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}\) — and consequently \(P^{-1}MP = D\). Proof. Two tables agree exactly when their columns agree — a table is its two column-answers (B06). Compare first columns: the first column of \(MP\) is \(M\vec v_1\), the machine's answer to the first column of \(P\); the first column of \(PD\) is \(P(\lambda_1, 0)\), the dictionary walked with reading \((\lambda_1, 0)\), which is \(\lambda_1 \vec v_1\). These are equal, because \(\vec v_1\) is a rail: \(M\vec v_1 = \lambda_1 \vec v_1\) is Box B07 verbatim. Second columns likewise: \(M\vec v_2 = \lambda_2 \vec v_2\). So \(MP = PD\). The rails are non-parallel (B07's Claim 3), so \(P\) passes B05's health test, \(\det P \ne 0\), and the reverse dictionary \(P^{-1}\) exists (Box B06.A). Multiply \(MP = PD\) on the left by \(P^{-1}\): \(P^{-1}MP = P^{-1}PD = D\). Complete. The pure stretches need no relabelling at all, which is the same argument read backwards: for any healthy \(P\), \(P^{-1}(\lambda I)P = \lambda P^{-1}P = \lambda I\) — already diagonal, in every scheme at once.

Claim 2 — the harvest law. For every whole \(n\): \(D^n = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^n & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^n \end{bmatrix}\) and \(M^n = P D^n P^{-1}\). Proof. Both halves by induction — the climbing move, with its own page later in this book. The table first. At \(n = 1\) the claim is \(D\) itself. If \(D^k\) is diagonal with corners \(\lambda_1^k, \lambda_2^k\), then \(D^{k+1} = D^k D\) multiplies corner-wise — a diagonal table never mixes readings, so its powers never mix either — giving corners \(\lambda_1^k \lambda_1 = \lambda_1^{k+1}\) and \(\lambda_2^{k+1}\). Then the machine. At \(n = 1\), Claim 1 read right-to-left gives \(M = PDP^{-1}\). If \(M^k = P D^k P^{-1}\), then

\[M^{k+1} \;=\; M^k M \;=\; P D^k P^{-1} \, P D P^{-1} \;=\; P D^k \bigl(P^{-1}P\bigr) D P^{-1} \;=\; P D^{k+1} P^{-1},\]

the interior \(P^{-1}P = I\) annihilating pairwise at every seam — the telescoping that makes a hundred feeds cost no more than one. The anchor \(n = 0\) holds too: \(M^0 = I = P P^{-1} = P D^0 P^{-1}\). Complete. This is the law that printed \(M^n\) for every \(n\) at once in section 3(a), and it is exact — no limits, no approximations, honest for the first feed and the millionth alike.

Claim 3 — who can be relabelled. A real two-by-two machine is diagonalizable exactly when its characteristic polynomial has two distinct real roots, or the machine is a pure stretch \(\lambda I\). Proof, both directions. (When \(\Rightarrow\) relabelled.) Two distinct real roots \(\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2\): step two of the hunt exhibits rails \(\vec v_1, \vec v_2\) (Box B07.A), B07's Claim 3 makes them non-parallel, and Claim 1 diagonalizes. Or \(M = \lambda I\): already diagonal, as above. (Relabelled \(\Rightarrow\) when.) Suppose \(P^{-1}MP = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha & 0 \\ 0 & \beta \end{bmatrix}\) for some healthy \(P\). Read Claim 1 backwards: \(MP = PD\), so \(M\) times the first column of \(P\) equals \(\alpha\) times that column — a rail with factor \(\alpha\) — and the second column is a rail with factor \(\beta\). If \(\alpha \ne \beta\), the characteristic equation has the two distinct real roots \(\alpha, \beta\) (they satisfy it, by B07's Claim 1, and a degree-two equation has no room for a third). If \(\alpha = \beta\), then \(M = P(\alpha I)P^{-1} = \alpha I\), a pure stretch. Nothing else can happen. Complete — and the census from B07 rereads at a glance: of the 625 machines with entries from \(-2\) to \(2\), the 416 two-rail machines and the 5 pure stretches relabel — \(421\) in all, just over two in three — while the 56 shears and the 148 empty-handed machines stand outside, honestly, until the complex completion is earned.

Claim 4 — the orbit closed form, and the tilt. Fix any starting point \((x_0, y_0)\), read it in the eigen-scheme, and call the reading \((a, b)\) — so \((x_0, y_0) = P(a,b) = a\vec v_1 + b\vec v_2\). Then after \(n\) feeds,

\[M^n(x_0, y_0) \;=\; a\,\lambda_1^n\,\vec v_1 \;+\; b\,\lambda_2^n\,\vec v_2.\]

Proof. \(M^n (x_0, y_0) = M^n P (a,b) = P D^n (a,b)\) — the middle step is Claim 2 right-multiplied by \(P\), the last is the dictionary walked with the reading \(D^n(a,b) = (\lambda_1^n a,\; \lambda_2^n b)\) — and walking that reading home is \(a\lambda_1^n \vec v_1 + b \lambda_2^n \vec v_2\). Complete. For the block's machine, \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\lambda_2 = 4\): the blue count \(a\) never changes, which is the invariant \(x - y = 3a\) (P09, arrived at by relabelling), and the red count quadruples. The tilt, made precise. Suppose \(b \ne 0\). Divide by \(4^n\): the orbit, scaled to year-zero size, is \(\frac{a}{4^n}\vec v_1 + b\vec v_2\). Name any fraction \(\frac{1}{m}\); the number \(\frac{|a|}{4^n}\) drops below it once \(4^n > |a|\, m\), and such an \(n\) exists because \(4^n \ge n + 1\) for every whole \(n\) (a one-line induction: \(4^0 = 1\), and multiplying by \(4\) adds at least \(1\)), so any whole \(n\) beyond \(|a|\,m\) does the job. The squeeze habit (P15) at its plainest: a fixed quantity divided by something that grows past every bound shrinks to nothing. So the scaled orbit closes in on the direction \(b\vec v_2\), the red rail: after twelve years every grid arrow with red content has its blue share below one part in two million, and the island's long-run map is the diagonal's map. Complete.

Scope, honestly. Three boundaries. (i) This page's machines are two-by-two with real entries; bigger tables run the same hunt and the same harvest with more rails wanted — a three-by-three machine needs three — and that floor is named, not built. (ii) The sticking doors stand: the shear owns one direction and the turn owns none, and over the reals no relabelling repairs them — 204 of the census's 625 machines; the complex completion that gives the turn its rails stays deferred, exactly where B07 left it. (iii) The relabelling answers the machine's own story — powers, orbits, growth; a question that needs the directions as well as the factors travels with \(P\) and \(D\) together (the stretch table alone forgets which way its rails point), and the harvest law as stated is for whole numbers of feeds — fractional and negative powers are later floors, named here. What the page closes, it closes completely; what it defers, it names.

The symmetric machine \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) — the machine of B07's first exercise, rails already hunted: \((1,1)\) with \(\lambda = 3\) and \((1,-1)\) with \(\lambda = 1\). (a) File the eigen-scheme's dictionary \(P\) — smaller stretch factor's rail first — health-check it, and write the reverse dictionary by Box B06.A. (b) Verify the diagonal claim: compute \(P^{-1}AP\). (c) Reap: \(A^5\) by the harvest law. (d) The return trip: feed \((1,0)\) five times, one feed per rung, and check the last rung against the closed form's first column.

Hint

(a) Columns are the rails in school numbers; the determinant is the health check; the adjugate over the determinant is the reverse. (b) Multiply in the order written. (c) The stretch table to the fifth is corner-wise; assemble \(P D^5 P^{-1}\) and let the factor \(\frac{1}{2}\) ride along. (d) Five feeds, five points, one column of the answer each.

Worked answer

(a) First column the \(\lambda = 1\) rail \((1,-1)\), second the \(\lambda = 3\) rail \((1,1)\): \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det P = 1 + 1 = 2 \ne 0\), healthy; \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) by Box B06.A. (b) \(P^{-1}AP = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\): a pure stretch, \(\times 1\) and \(\times 3\), no mixing. (c) \(A^5 = P \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3^5 \end{bmatrix} P^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 243 \\ -1 & 243 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 244 & 242 \\ 242 & 244 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 122 & 121 \\ 121 & 122 \end{bmatrix}\). (d) The ladder: \(A(1,0) = (2,1)\); \(A(2,1) = (5,4)\); \(A(5,4) = (14,13)\); \(A(14,13) = (41,40)\); \(A(41,40) = (122,121)\). The fifth feed's answer is \(A^5\)'s first column — and the closed form's first column is \(\left(\frac{1 + 3^5}{2}, \frac{-1 + 3^5}{2}\right) = (122, 121)\): three routes, one answer. The trace glance agrees: \(122 + 122 = 244 = 1 + 3^5\).

Exercise 2 ●●○

A second storm machine washes up, \(C = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\). (a) Hunt: characteristic equation, roots, both rails — one line each. (b) File the dictionary (smaller factor first), health-check it, reverse it, and verify \(P^{-1}CP\) is the stretch table. (c) Reap: \(C^5\) by the harvest law. (d) Confirm two ways: the trace glance against \(2^5 + 7^5\), and the school route \(C^2\), \(C^4\), then \(C^5 = C^4 C\).

Hint

(a) Trace 9, determinant 14 — the equation assembles itself, and both roots are whole. (b) Columns are the rails; \(\det P\) comes out 5. (c) \(2^5 = 32\), \(7^5 = 16807\); the two divisions by 5 both land. (d) The trace of a power is the sum of the powers of the factors; the school route is three honest multiplications.

Worked answer

(a) \(\operatorname{tr} C = 9\), \(\det C = 18 - 4 = 14\): \(\lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 14 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 7)\). For \(\lambda = 2\): \(C - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(a + 2b = 0\), rail \((2,-1)\) — check: \(C(2,-1) = (6-2,\; 4-6) = (4,-4) = 2(2,-1)\). For \(\lambda = 7\): \(C - 7I = \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(b = 2a\), rail \((1,2)\) — check: \(C(1,2) = (3+4,\; 2+12) = (7,14) = 7(1,2)\). (b) \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\det P = 4 + 1 = 5\), healthy; \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). \(P^{-1}CP = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 7 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 35 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\). (c) \(C^5 = P \begin{bmatrix} 32 & 0 \\ 0 & 16807 \end{bmatrix} P^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 64 & 16807 \\ -32 & 33614 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 16935 & 33550 \\ 33550 & 67260 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3387 & 6710 \\ 6710 & 13452 \end{bmatrix}\). (d) Trace: \(3387 + 13452 = 16839 = 32 + 16807 = 2^5 + 7^5\) — Vieta's sum reading one floor up, holding. School route: \(C^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 13 & 18 \\ 18 & 40 \end{bmatrix}\); \(C^4 = (C^2)^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 493 & 954 \\ 954 & 1924 \end{bmatrix}\); \(C^5 = C^4 C = \begin{bmatrix} 3387 & 6710 \\ 6710 & 13452 \end{bmatrix}\). Both routes, one table.

Exercise 3 ●●●

The Fibonacci machine \(F = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\), end to end — the harvest run on irrational rails, reaping the most famous closed form in the book. The counts first: \(F(0) = 0\), \(F(1) = 1\), and each month adds the last two, \(F(n+2) = F(n+1) + F(n)\) — the rabbit counts of section 2's history note. (a) Full hunt: trace, determinant, characteristic equation, discriminant, both roots — and the Vieta glance. (b) The rails: show \((\varphi, 1)\) and \((\psi, 1)\) are the machine's directions, where \(\varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2}\) and \(\psi = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt 5}{2}\). (c) File the dictionary \(P\), health-check it — the check comes out irrational, and the page owes you a sentence on whether that is legal — and write the reverse dictionary. (d) Reap: prove \(F^n = \begin{bmatrix} F(n+1) & F(n) \\ F(n) & F(n-1) \end{bmatrix}\) by induction, harvest \(F^n = P D^n P^{-1}\), and read off the \((1,2)\) entry: Binet's formula. (e) Three routes to \(F(10) = 55\): the integer ladder, the Binet float, and an exact irrational-free route that squares the surds away.

Hint

(a) Box B07.A, two steps, new numbers; the discriminant is not a perfect square, and the hunt does not mind. (b) Feed the machine and use what \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) satisfy — each is a root of the equation from (a). (c) Columns are the rails; the determinant is the health check, and B05's test asks only nonzero, never whole. (d) Prove the table shape by induction first — the recurrence does the climbing — then multiply \(P D^n P^{-1}\) and remember Vieta's product \(\varphi\psi = -1\) at the crucial cancellation. (e) Route 1 is ten feeds of \((1,0)\); route 2 is a calculator; route 3 writes \(\varphi^n\) as \(\tfrac{a_n + b_n\sqrt 5}{2}\) and climbs the pairs.

Worked answer

(a) \(\operatorname{tr} F = 1 + 0 = 1\), \(\det F = 0 - 1 = -1\), so the equation is \(\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0\). Discriminant \((\operatorname{tr})^2 - 4\det = 1 + 4 = 5\), not a square — the roots are irrational, \(\varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2} \approx 1.618\) and \(\psi = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt 5}{2} \approx -0.618\). Vieta's two readings hold exactly as B07's Claim 2 promises: \(\varphi + \psi = 1 = \operatorname{tr} F\) and \(\varphi\psi = \tfrac{1 - 5}{4} = -1 = \det F\) — the product reading is the cancellation (d) will spend. (b) Feed the machine: \(F(\varphi, 1) = (\varphi + 1,\; \varphi)\). But \(\varphi\) satisfies \(\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0\), that is, \(\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1\) — so \(F(\varphi, 1) = (\varphi^2, \varphi) = \varphi(\varphi, 1)\): a rail with factor \(\varphi\). Identically \(F(\psi, 1) = (\psi^2, \psi) = \psi(\psi, 1)\). Two distinct factors, so the rails are non-parallel (B07's Claim 3) and a scheme exists. (c) Columns the rails, golden first: \(P = \begin{bmatrix} \varphi & \psi \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), and \(\det P = \varphi - \psi = \sqrt 5 \ne 0\) — healthy. The health check coming out irrational is legal because the test only ever asked nonzero: B05's determinant certifies that the two directions are independent, and it prices areas, not arithmetic — the dictionary may carry surds, exactly as Elena's fractional readings carried fractions. The reverse dictionary, adjugate over determinant (Box B06.A): \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -\psi \\ -1 & \varphi \end{bmatrix}\). (d) The table shape, by induction. At \(n = 1\): \(F^1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} F(2) & F(1) \\ F(1) & F(0) \end{bmatrix}\). If \(F^n = \begin{bmatrix} F(n+1) & F(n) \\ F(n) & F(n-1) \end{bmatrix}\), then

\[F^{n+1} \;=\; F^n F \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} F(n+1) + F(n) & F(n+1) \\ F(n) + F(n-1) & F(n) \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} F(n+2) & F(n+1) \\ F(n+1) & F(n) \end{bmatrix},\]

the recurrence \(F(n+2) = F(n+1) + F(n)\) doing the climbing. So the \((1,2)\) entry of \(F^n\) is \(F(n)\) for every whole \(n\) — the school route. The harvest. Claim 2 gives \(F^n = P D^n P^{-1}\) with \(D = \begin{bmatrix} \varphi & 0 \\ 0 & \psi \end{bmatrix}\). The first row of \(P D^n\) is \((\varphi^{n+1},\; \psi^{n+1})\); the second column of \(P^{-1}\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt 5}\begin{bmatrix} -\psi \\ \varphi \end{bmatrix}\). So the \((1,2)\) entry is

\[\frac{-\varphi^{n+1}\psi \;+\; \psi^{n+1}\varphi}{\sqrt 5} \;=\; \frac{\varphi^n \, (-\varphi\psi) \;+\; \psi^n \, (\varphi\psi)}{\sqrt 5} \;=\; \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt 5},\]

because \(\varphi\psi = -1\): the \(-\varphi\psi\) turns the first term into \(+\varphi^n\) and the \(\varphi\psi\) turns the second into \(-\psi^n\). Equate the two routes and Vieta's product reading has bought a theorem:

\[F(n) \;=\; \frac{\varphi^n - \psi^n}{\sqrt 5} \qquad \text{for every whole } n \text{ at once}.\]

Binet's formula — the closed form the whole page was built to reap, and it is exact, not a float: the surds cancel to whole numbers for every \(n\), because the two routes computed the same entry of the same table. (e) Route 1, the ladder. Ten feeds of \((1,0)\): \((1,0) \to (1,1) \to (2,1) \to (3,2) \to (5,3) \to (8,5) \to (13,8) \to (21,13) \to (34,21) \to (55,34) \to (89,55)\). The tenth feed lands on the first column of \(F^{10}\), which by the table shape is \((F(11), F(10)) = (89, 55)\): \(F(10) = 55\), school arithmetic, no surd in sight. Route 2, the Binet float. \((\varphi^{10} - \psi^{10})/\sqrt 5\) on a calculator: \(55.000000000000014\) — fourteen right answers and then float noise; the formula is exact and the float is merely the float. Route 3, exact pairs. Write \(\varphi^n = \tfrac{a_n + b_n\sqrt 5}{2}\): \(\varphi^1 = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt 5}{2}\), and multiplying by \(\varphi\) again gives \(\varphi^{n+1} = \tfrac{(a_n + 5b_n) + (a_n + b_n)\sqrt 5}{4}\), so the pairs climb by \((a, b) \to \left(\tfrac{a + 5b}{2},\; \tfrac{a + b}{2}\right)\): \((1,1) \to (3,1) \to (4,2) \to (7,3) \to (11,5) \to (18,8) \to (29,13) \to (47,21) \to (76,34) \to (123,55)\). So \(\varphi^{10} = \tfrac{123 + 55\sqrt 5}{2}\), and \(\psi\) is the same expression with \(\sqrt 5\) negated — the conjugate — so \(\psi^{10} = \tfrac{123 - 55\sqrt 5}{2}\). The difference is \(55\sqrt 5\), and Binet divides by \(\sqrt 5\): exactly \(55\), with every surd gone. Three routes, one answer — and a closing glance the tilt taught you to take: \(\psi \approx -0.618\) has size below \(1\), so \(\psi^n\) dies out and \(F(n)\) grows like \(\varphi^n/\sqrt 5\) — the counts are the golden rail's shadow, the red rail's tilt worn as a number sequence.

Open exploration ◐

The growth detector, island edition. The harbourmaster pins B07's census grid to the desk — the 48 nonzero arrows with entries from \(-3\) to \(3\) — and asks a question with a brute-force answer and a closed-form proof. (a) Feed every arrow to \(M\) ten times over: which arrows are still inside the grid at the end? Brute-force it if you like — 48 arrows, ten feeds, honest bookkeeping — then prove the answer from Claim 4, in terms of red content. (b) What does the detector detect: say precisely what "stays in the window" measures about an arrow. (c) The twist: double the machine to \(2M = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 2 \\ 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\), hunt it in one line, and show that now nothing survives — every one of the 48 arrows escapes within a single feed.

Hint

(a) An arrow's red content is the second coordinate of its reading, \(\tfrac{2a + b}{3}\) — a fraction whose denominator is fixed, so a nonzero red content has a size it cannot shrink below. Claim 4 says the red piece multiplies by 4 every feed. (b) Compare the two rails' factors. (c) In-grid means both coordinates answer to \(-3\) to \(3\); \(4(a + b)\) and \(2(3a + b)\) are even numbers in that window — count what can fit.

Worked answer

(a) Exactly two arrows survive: the blue pair \(\pm(1,-2)\). Proof. Read an arrow \((a,b)\) on the rails: \(P^{-1}(a,b) = \left(\tfrac{a - b}{3},\; \tfrac{2a + b}{3}\right)\) — blue content \(\tfrac{a-b}{3}\), red content \(\tfrac{2a+b}{3}\). Claim 4 says the orbit is \(\text{blue} \cdot (1,-2) + 4^n \cdot \text{red} \cdot (1,1)\). If the red content is nonzero, then \(2a + b\) is a nonzero whole number, so \(|\text{red}| \ge \tfrac{1}{3}\); and on the grid \(|\text{blue}| = \tfrac{|a-b|}{3} \le 2\). The first coordinate after \(n\) feeds is \(\text{blue} + 4^n\,\text{red}\), so its size is at least \(4^n \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} - 2\). Staying in the window demands \(|x_n| \le 3\), so \(4^n \le 15\): true at \(n = 0, 1\) and false from \(n = 2\) on — every arrow with red content has left the grid by its second feed at the latest, and ten feeds is far past that. If the red content is zero then \(b = -2a\): the arrow sits on the blue rail, and the grid holds exactly \(\pm(1,-2)\) of those. The blue factor is \(1\), so \(M\) returns them unchanged, this feed and every feed. Survivors: exactly \(\pm(1,-2)\) — B07's two fixed arrows, now detected by growth instead of counted by hand. (b) The detector detects red content. "Stays in the window for ten feeds" and "red content zero" name the same arrows — bounded inside the grid exactly on the frozen rail — and the reason is the pair of factors: one factor equal to \(1\) keeps its content; any other factor, even \(4\), marches its content through every window in a couple of feeds. The detector is a growth meter, and it reads off the machine nothing but the stretch factors. (c) The hunt: \(\operatorname{tr}(2M) = 10\), \(\det(2M) = 16\), so \(\lambda^2 - 10\lambda + 16 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 8)\) — the same two rails (doubling a machine doubles every answer, so every factor doubles: \(1 \to 2\), \(4 \to 8\)), and now both factors are off \(1\): the detector predicts an empty grid, survivors or not, blue rail included. And the prediction holds faster than promised — one feed suffices. \(2M(a,b) = (6a + 2b,\; 4a + 4b)\); to stay in the window the second coordinate must satisfy \(|4(a+b)| \le 3\), and \(a + b\) is a whole number, so \(a + b = 0\). Then the first coordinate is \(6a + 2b = 4a\), and \(|4a| \le 3\) forces \(a = 0\), so \(b = 0\) as well: the only arrow whose feed stays in the grid is the zero arrow the census never counted. All 48 escape in one feed — the blue pair first of all, \(2M(1,-2) = (2,-4)\), one bound out of the window. A machine with every factor off \(1\) empties any bounded grid, and how fast it empties is the factors' own sizes — the growth detector, reading the stretch table off nothing but survival.
