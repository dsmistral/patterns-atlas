The Patterns Atlas · P16 · the sixteenth move of the spine

Same problem, different clothes

Two problems can share nothing — no number, no object, no word — and still be the same problem. A card game about sums is a board game about lines. A tower of discs moved under a monk's rule is a counter rolling through its paces. A machine that scrambles a whole island map is two one-number stories filed side by side. The sixteenth move is the wardrobe that makes the sameness usable. Build a dictionary — one name for each object, this side and that — and make it keep the only promise that matters: that the relations the question runs on, the wins and the feeds and the sits-on, are carried by the pairing, in both directions. A dictionary that keeps the promise has an old Greek name — isomorphism, equal shape — and it licenses the biggest short-cut in mathematics: stop solving, translate, let the solved sibling answer, and carry the answer home. P05 promised to prove things once and translate everywhere; this page names the license under which whole problems — not just objects — change clothes. The block B05–B08 built one dictionary in full public view; here the move is named in the wide — and the forgeries get a gallery.

New word: dictionary. A dictionary between two problems is a one-for-one translation rule: it pairs every object of one problem with exactly one object of the other — card with cell, disc move with numeral, plane reading with rail step-count — nobody nameless, nobody named twice. The word rhymes with B06's change-of-basis dictionary on purpose: it is that idea at full width. New word: structure. The structure of a problem is the list of relations the question runs on — which combinations win, which positions feed which, which points sit on which lines. Structure is what a good translation carries; the dress — the names, the material, the costume — is what it leaves behind. New word: isomorphism. Two problems are isomorphic — Greek for "of equal shape" — when some dictionary between them keeps the whole promise: every relation that matters is carried, exact, in both directions. The verdict is what this page teaches you to check before you trust; the forgeries are what it teaches you to catch.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on, P13 convicted whole families by manufacturing ever-smaller criminals, P14 built the one object no list can contain, and P15 trapped the unwritable between two computable walls. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P05, because the canonical-form promise — normalize the object, prove it once, translate back — is the promise this page cashes at full width: the uniform becomes a whole wardrobe, and "translate back" becomes a licensed act with checked conditions. P03, because a dictionary is a claim and claims are attacked, not admired — §5's gallery is three forgeries caught exactly where the checks jam. And the coordinates block just built: B05 the measuring scheme, B06 the translation, B07 the machine's own directions, B08 the harvest — a dictionary built for the plane in full public view, which §4 re-runs at pattern speed as this page's worked machine example.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Mathematicians say "the same problem" about problems that share no material at all — and the sentence is not a metaphor but a claim with a proof obligation: exhibit the dictionary, check that the pairing is one-for-one, check that the relations are carried, check that the check runs both ways. The good news: nothing new needs building. The checks are P01's pinning plus honest bookkeeping, and §2's card game gives the whole move in one square. The bad news, paid up front: the license is exact and unforgiving. A dictionary that carries seven of eight relations carries nothing safely; a pairing that names an object twice squashes the problem into a shape it never had; a translation without its return ticket delivers right answers in the wrong clothes. Each failure gets its exhibit, and each exhibit gets its price.

2 · Meet the problem

A game with no board. Nine cards lie face up between the players, numbered 1 to 9. They take turns claiming one card each, and the first to hold three cards summing to fifteen wins. That is the whole game — a parlour classic the puzzle literature calls Number Scrabble. No grid, no marks, nothing to look at: just the numbers, and the sums.

Play one out. A claims 5; B claims 9; A claims 2; B claims 8; A claims 7; B claims 3; A claims 6 — and A lays down 2, 6, 7: fifteen. If you have never met the game, replay the picks and notice what playing it feels like. Every turn is a scan. B, holding 9 and 8, checks A's pairs for a reaching third: A holds 5, 2, 7, and 5 + 2 + 7 = 14, safe for now — but the next card will decide. A checks B the same way. The arithmetic is trivial and endless. B's claim of 8 was in fact a block — A held 5 and 2, and 5 + 2 + 8 = 15, so B took A's completion card away — and A's reply, the claim of 7, opened two threats at once (5 + 7 + 3 and 2 + 7 + 6), a fork that B, with one move, could not cover: blocking at 3 conceded the win at 6. None of this was visible. The game is all bookkeeping — threats hide inside three-term sums, forks look like ordinary claims, and a hundred plays in you are no closer to the game's shape than on play one. It does not compress.

Now draw a square, and write the cards into it like this:

— the ancient arrangement §7 calls the Luoshu square — and read the dictionary card → cell. Reread the whole game through it. A's 5 is the center. B's 9 the top-middle. A's 2 the top-right. B's 8 the bottom-left — the anti-diagonal block, drawn as such. A's 7 the middle-right — the fork, two lines open at once, plain on the board. B's 3 the middle-left, blocking one. A's 6 the bottom-right, completing the right column: 2, 7, 6 — fifteen. The game that felt like arithmetic was tic-tac-toe the whole time — nine cells, three in a row, the board game every schoolyard knows — wearing a costume of numbers. And the tailoring is exact, not lucky: every three cards that sum to fifteen sit on a straight line of this square, and every straight line holds three cards summing to fifteen.

Count both sides of the tailoring, because the counts are the first evidence. The triples: three distinct cards summing to fifteen — there are exactly eight:

The card game's win-structure and tic-tac-toe's win-structure are not merely the same size; under the card → cell pairing they are the same relations, one wearing digits and the other wearing cells.

A skeptic — and this move trains skeptics — asks exactly the right question: is the square a coincidence of one playthrough, or is the card game tic-tac-toe? What would license the strong sentence, the games are the same, rather than the weak one, the games feel alike? The answer is this page's whole subject, and it is a demand structure, not a feeling. The pairing must be one-for-one — nine cards, nine cells, each named once, checkable at a glance. The relations that matter must be carried — three cards win exactly when their cells line up: all eight winning triples, not seven of them. And the check must run both ways — every line must be a winning triple too, so that nothing on the board side is left over, no eleventh-hour line living in the cells but not the cards. Those three demands — one-for-one, relations carried, both directions — are the promise a dictionary must keep before the verdict isomorphic is licensed, and they are named, boxed, and priced in the next section. But first see what the promise buys, because it buys more than a riddle's solution: it buys every fact about tic-tac-toe as a fact about the card game, free of new analysis. What the promise costs — and how dictionaries lie — is §5's gallery.

3 · Name the move

P16 · Same problem, different clothes — solve the problem it is a copy of

Shape of the claim. "I have solved this before — haven't I?" The problem arrives in unfamiliar dress: cards instead of cells, discs instead of digits, a scrambling machine instead of two stretches. But its question has the shape of a solved one. The claim to check is strong: the two problems share not a look but their entire structure, so that the solved sibling's answers are answers here.

The move. Do not solve. Build a dictionary: pair each object of the problem with its twin in the solved sibling. Check the three promises — the pairing is one-for-one; the relations the question runs on are carried by it; the reverse pairing carries them too. If all three clear, translate the question over, let the sibling answer, and translate the answer home. If any promise jams, stop: §5 is the gallery of what each broken promise costs.

When to reach for it. When a problem resembles a solved one but its dress resists — the sums will not scan, the tangle will not decouple, the rule will not compress. When a solved sibling's verdict, strategy, or formula would settle the question outright. When re-deriving would repeat work a dictionary could carry. And in the negative direction: when no dictionary exists, that failure is itself a finding — P12's obstruction, hunted by this move.

The move in words a page long: a problem is its structure plus its dress; the solved sibling wears the same structure in different dress; the dictionary is the tailoring pattern between the two, and its three promises are the fitting. When the fitting holds, nothing about the structure needs re-deriving — verdicts, strategies, formulas, whole futures cross the wardrobe freely, and the dress stays behind. That inversion — knowledge about labels becoming knowledge about relations — is the isomorphism's signature, and every example below runs on it.

What a dictionary must promise

A dictionary is legitimate only if all three gates clear, and each gate names a promise in words before words get mathematical:

  • The pairing, one-for-one. Every object on each side is named, and nobody is named twice: card to cell, disc move to numeral, reading to step-count. A pairing that names an object twice is not a translation but a squash — two problems' objects forced to share a name — and §5's second exhibit prices it. For the plane, this promise is B05's health test: a dictionary with determinant zero squashes, and the test refuses it.
  • The relations, carried. List the relations the question runs on — P01's job — and check each against the pairing: three cards win exactly when their cells lie on a line; a feed of the machine is a year of the story; a move of the puzzle is a roll of the counter. The relations to carry are the ones the question asks about, and no others; carrying extras is impossible, and §5's first exhibit prices carrying only some.
  • Both directions. The check must survive reversal: the dictionary's own twin — read right to left — carries the sibling's structure back to the problem. A one-way translation carries answers over but never home, and §5's third exhibit prices a ticket bought one way. This is P78's reversible chain, worn as a license: the pairing must work as a round trip before it works at all.

Checked, the three promises license the verdict: the problems are isomorphic — same structure, different labels — and everything structural about one is a fact about the other: verdicts, strategies, formulas, futures. Unchecked, the sentence "same problem" is a metaphor, and metaphors do not carry proofs.

Where the move appears, and its three-key signature

Appearances. Games and puzzles: Number Scrabble is tic-tac-toe (§2), and every "new" pastime that is an old one relabelled — the audits below say how common the forgeries are. Procedures: the tower of Hanoi's move log is a binary counter rolling (§4's second example). Machines: the coordinates block's change of clothes, \(A = PDP^{-1}\), is this page's move wearing work clothes, and §4's third example runs it at pattern speed. Arithmetic itself: B02's same-remainder classes — adding clock hours is adding remainders — a dictionary the bridge track already built and cashed. And refutations: to show a problem cannot be solved, show its solved sibling would have to break — the obstruction travels a dictionary exactly as well as a strategy does.

Three keys. The move applies exactly when all three turn:

  1. A solved sibling in sight. The pattern-matching instinct comes first: "what does this resemble?" The book's whole spine is a wardrobe of siblings — the question is which garment the problem already fits.
  2. Objects that pair one-for-one. A size match you can count: nine cards and nine cells; \(2^n\) move-log entries and \(2^n\) counter states; the plane's points and their rail step-count readings. Where sizes differ, a cousin move may still serve — divide the extras away by symmetry (P08) or lift to a roomier floor (P18) — but the dictionary itself demands the count.
  3. Relations you can list and check. Structure must be nameable before it can be carried: pin the win-sets, the feed-rules, the line-memberships — then check the pairing against the list, relation by relation, both ways. A structure too diffuse to list is a structure the dictionary cannot promise.

When the first key jams, the sibling must be built before it can be worn — P07's constructions. When the second jams, the sizes are telling you something: hunt the symmetry or the dimension that reconciles them, or accept the obstruction and price it. When the third jams, the resemblance is real but not structural — a cousinship (P03's discipline applies: check before trusting) — and §8's closing exercise counts exactly how badly such cousinships fail.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per family in Box P16.B's catalogue: a game, a procedure, a machine. Each runs the same spine — pin the relations (P01), build the dictionary, check the three promises, translate, harvest — and each ends with the last step that is easy to forget: the answer is not the answer until it is back in the question's own clothes.

Now collect what the certificate buys. The verdict. Tic-tac-toe with best play is a draw — an elementary, well-known fact. Suppose the fifteen-game were not a draw: say the second player has a winning strategy. Translate that strategy move by move through the dictionary — it pairs moves with moves (each claimable card is a markable cell), legal positions with legal positions, and wins with wins (the eight-pair table) — and it becomes a winning strategy for the second player in tic-tac-toe, which cannot exist. The contradiction transfers as cleanly as the strategy does, because both are made of the relations the dictionary carries. So the fifteen-game is a draw with best play — and the page has spent not one line of card analysis to learn it. The strategy. Everything the schoolyard knows about tic-tac-toe now plays the card game: open center, answer the corners' forks, block the line that is two-thirds built. §2's game rereads as a lesson: A's 5 was the center opening, B's 8 the forced anti-diagonal block, A's 7 the fork that wins against any single reply. The honesty. The dictionary imports; it does not manufacture. The draw arrived free because tic-tac-toe's draw was already banked; had the sibling's verdict been unknown, the certificate would license the transfer of a strategy but not conjure one — the hunting still has to happen somewhere, and the move's genius is making it happen where the problem is easiest.

Example 2 · The tower, re-dressed as a counter — strategy by odometer

Moves used: P02 tabulating the small cases until they rhyme · P07 building the counter the puzzle forgot to give you · P16 wearing the odometer's paces.

The puzzle: three pegs stand in a row — call them A, B, C — and \(n\) discs of graded sizes sit on A as a tower, largest at the bottom. A move takes the top disc of one peg and places it on another, never onto a smaller disc; the task is to carry the whole tower from A to C. The standard solution is famous and famously recursive: to move \(n\) discs, carry \(n-1\) aside, move the big disc, rebuild. What plagues the player is not the plan but the mid-game question — which disc moves on move \(m\), and can I know without replaying the whole plan?

Tabulate the smallest case that is not trivial (P02): \(n = 3\), played out in full. Seven moves, each one legal, the tower arriving complete on C:

Read the disc column: \(1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1\). Something in that column is not about discs at all — it is the rhythm of counting. Write each move number in binary and count the trailing zeros: move 1 is \(001\) — zero trailing zeros — and moves disc 1; move 2 is \(010\) — one — and moves disc 2; move 4 is \(100\) — two — and moves disc 3. The dictionary stands up out of the table: the move-log of the puzzle pairs with a binary counter rolling from 1 to \(2^n - 1\), and the disc that moves on count \(m\) is the disc of the topmost bit that turns over when the odometer rolls from \(m-1\) to \(m\): disc \(\mathrm{tz}(m) + 1\), where \(\mathrm{tz}(m)\) counts the trailing zeros of \(m\).

Check the promises, because the rhythm might be a small-case rhyme (P03's discipline: three cases prove nothing). The pairing is one-for-one: the standard solution runs exactly \(2^n - 1\) moves — one per count, none skipped, none doubled — and the positions it visits are all distinct, so the counter names each one once. The relations are carried: the table's seven rows check the rule on \(n = 3\), and the check extends by machine — for every tower size up to \(n = 10\), every move the rule names is legal, the tower arrives complete, and no position repeats. And both directions hold: the rule reads \(m\) to disc, and disc to \(m\) — the disc \(d\) moves exactly on the counts \(m\) divisible by \(2^{d-1}\) but not by \(2^d\), which is to say \(2^{n-d}\) times in the whole game: \(4, 2, 1\) moves for discs \(1, 2, 3\) when \(n = 3\) — and the paces sum to \(4 + 2 + 1 = 7 = 2^3 - 1\), the total move count, as they must.

Now the harvest, and it is the puzzle's whole hard question answered in the head. Which disc moves on move \(m\)? Count the trailing zeros of \(m\) and add one. The billionth move of a large tower — say thirty discs or more, so the game runs past a billion counts — is \(10^9 = 2^9 \cdot 1953125\): nine trailing zeros, disc 10. And the legend's arithmetic closes the same way: a tower of 64 discs takes \(2^{64} - 1 = 18{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}615\) moves — twenty digits — which at one move per second runs about 584.5 billion years; the temple is in no danger of finishing. The mid-game question that plagued the player — which disc, and where does it go — has been re-dressed as reading a counter, and the counter never hesitates.

One boundary, named honestly. This dictionary pairs the counts with the moves of the standard solution — the shortest route, the recursive plan. The fuller wardrobe — every legal position of the puzzle, reached or not, named systematically, with the whole graph of legal moves carrying to a matching graph — is a richer structure and a later floor; this page names it and does not build it. What is certified here is what the examples used: the count names the move, the disc, and the pace.

Example 3 · The storm office's spring 100 — the block's dictionary, cashed at pattern speed

Moves used: B07 hunting the rails · B08 harvesting the future in rail clothes · P09 watching the invariant ride through · P16 the re-dressing, named at last.

The coordinates block ended with the island's machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), its two rails — the red diagonal \((1,1)\) stretched \(\times 4\), the blue cove arrow \((1,-2)\) stretched \(\times 1\) and so standing still — and the harvest law: relabel the plane by its rails and the machine's every future is two one-number stories. B08 walked three springs by hand and proved the law; the office now asks for spring 100, and the school route is unthinkable: a hundred coupled feeds, each mixing both coordinates, the entries swollen past sixty digits. This example is the block's whole apparatus wearing this page's formal clothes — the worked machine application \(A = PDP^{-1}\) that B08 pointed here — and it runs the pattern's spine exactly.

Pin the relations (P01): the question asks where a point is after 100 feeds, so the structure that matters is the feed-relation — one feed, one image — plus the arithmetic that counts feeds. Build the dictionary: it is the block's own \(P\), the rails filed as columns, blue first:

\[P \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det P \;=\; 3 \;\ne\; 0 \;\;\text{— healthy, and one-for-one: no reading is squashed.}\]

Check the three promises. The pairing is one-for-one — the health test passed (B05), the reverse dictionary exists (B06). The relations are carried — one feed of \(M\), read in rail step-counts, is "blue count unchanged, red count times four": that is B08's Claim 4, proved there. Both directions — the round trip \(P^{-1}MP = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\) was computed there too, on this very machine. The verdict was licensed a bridge page ago: in rail clothes the machine is two one-number stories, and the block certified it.

Now translate the question. The office's point is \((3,0)\) — B08's Lecture 0 walk — and its rail reading is \((1, 2)\): one blue step, two red steps, the two little equations \(a + b = 3\) and \(-2a + b = 0\) solving to whole numbers. One hundred feeds in rail clothes: the blue count \(1\) is stretched by \(1\) — frozen, this year and every year — and the red count \(2\) is stretched by \(4^{100}\). The reading after spring 100 is \((1,\; 2 \cdot 4^{100})\). That is the sibling's answer: two one-number stories, each told in a single multiplication.

The return ticket — pay it, because the office asked for a map point, not a reading. Walk the reading home through the dictionary:

\[1 \cdot (1,-2) \;+\; 2 \cdot 4^{100} \cdot (1,1) \;=\; \bigl(1 + 2 \cdot 4^{100},\;\; -2 + 2 \cdot 4^{100}\bigr).\]

Check it against the walks the block already banked, because a translation is checked, not trusted: spring 1 gives \((1 + 8,\, -2 + 8) = (9,6)\) — the Lecture 0 walk's first feed, in exact agreement; spring 2 gives \((33,30)\), agreement again; and the difference \(x - y\) is exactly \(3\) in every spring, P09's invariant riding the dictionary untouched — the blue piece's signature, \(1 - (-2) = 3\), with the red rail contributing nothing, \(1 - 1 = 0\). The answer's size is a fact worth one sentence: \(4^{100} = 2^{200}\) runs to sixty-one digits and opens \(1606938044\ldots\), so both coordinates of spring 100 are sixty-one-digit numbers opening \(321387608851\ldots\) — and they differ by exactly \(3\). Two immense numbers, three apart: the tiniest frozen blue step inside the loudest red roar.

Read the price structure, because this is the move's signature trade. The school route: a hundred feeds, each two multiplications against growing entries — hundreds of big multiplications, and a new walk for every new spring asked. The re-dressed route: one read (two small equations), six squarings and two multiplications for \(4^{100}\) itself (\(4^1, 4^2, 4^4, \ldots, 4^{64}\) — six squarings — then \(4^{100} = 4^{64} \cdot 4^{32} \cdot 4^4\) — two more), one doubling, two additions — eight big multiplications, once, for every spring at once: the closed form is spring \(n\) for every \(n\), P05's prove-once-wear-everywhere promise, kept at the level of whole problems. And the honest boundary: the dictionary carried the feed-relation and the counting arithmetic — it did not carry the school coordinates themselves. A question about the map's own grid ("is spring 100 inside the old harbour?") must be answered in translated clothes too — the answer above, not its reading \((1, 2\cdot4^{100})\); §5's third exhibit prices that ticket left unpaid.

5 · When the move fails

The three promises are not decoration; each fails in a characteristic way, and the forgeries are as instructive as the certificates. Three exhibits, then the boundary.

Exhibit 1 · The half-carried wardrobe — a structure failure

Take the nine cards and write them into the board in reading order: 1, 2, 3 across the top, 4, 5, 6 across the middle, 7, 8, 9 across the bottom. This is a dictionary — a perfectly good pairing, one-for-one, nine cards to nine cells, nobody named twice. Now check the relations, which is the exhibit's whole point: of the eight winning triples, how many sit on lines? The middle row holds 4, 5, 6 — a line, fifteen. The middle column holds 2, 5, 8 — a line. The two diagonals, 1-5-9 and 3-5-7 — lines. And the other four triples land nowhere: the top row sums to 6, the bottom row to 24, the outer columns to 12 and 18 — not wins, not lines. Four of eight carried: a bijection that is not an isomorphism.

Play the card game through this dictionary and the failure is not subtle. A strategy imported from tic-tac-toe — block the two-thirds-built line — blocks the wrong cards: the translated board shows lines where the cards have no wins, and hides wins where the cards do. And there is a small detective story in the wreckage, worth the telling: the four surviving lines are exactly the four lines through the center. Cells opposite each other through the center hold numbers summing to ten — 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, 4 and 6, because reading order is built that way — so every line through the center carries a triple \(\{d, 5, 10 - d\}\) summing to fifteen, while every line avoiding the center carries numbers too small or too large. The surviving structure is not random; it is the residue of an accident of the arrangement. But an accident it remains: four of eight carries nothing safely, and the verdict "the games are the same" is a forgery. Fig P16.3 draws the wreckage. The census in §8's closing exercise counts the whole wardrobe: of the 362,880 ways to dress the board, exactly 8 carry all eight — and 2,824, this exhibit's family, carry exactly four.

Exhibit 2 · The collapsing dictionary — a pairing failure

A dictionary must name each object once. A pairing that gives two objects one name is not a translation but a squash: information the problem needs has nowhere to live. The card game has a toy version — pair the cards to only the eight outer cells and the center is nameless, so every win through 5 becomes unreadable — but the block already built the real one. When B08 looked for a dictionary that would decouple the shear \(S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), the hunt returned a single rail, east \((1,0)\), stretched by \(1\): the machine owns one direction, and a two-column dictionary needs two. Fill the second column with anything parallel and the two plane readings share a name — the determinant lands on zero, B05's health test refuses the scheme, and the reverse dictionary does not exist: not a difficult translation but an impossible one, because the pairing was never one-for-one. This is the deepest of the three failures, and the quietest: a squashed dictionary does not produce wrong answers with a wrongness you can catch by re-reading — it fails to mean anything at all, and the health test is the guard that says so before the damage. The bijection promise is not pedantry; it is the promise that the two problems have the same amount of stuff.

Exhibit 3 · The one-way ticket — a return-trip failure

The dictionary checked, the promises certified, the question translated, the sibling's answer exact — and the last step skipped: the answer reported in the sibling's clothes. The storm office's junior clerk, freshly taught the block, is asked for spring 100 of the point \((3,0)\). The clerk reads the point on the rails — \((1, 2)\) — computes the rail reading after one hundred feeds — \((1, 2 \cdot 4^{100})\) — and proudly reports those two numbers as the map point. The arithmetic is flawless and the answer is wrong: \((1, 2\cdot 4^{100})\) is a pair of step-counts, blue and red, not a pair of map coordinates. The right answer sits one dictionary-walk away — \(\bigl(1 + 2\cdot 4^{100},\, -2 + 2\cdot 4^{100}\bigr)\), two numbers that differ from the clerk's report the way a longitude differs from a step-count — and the ticket home was never paid. P78 built the habit for chains of steps; here it prices a translation: the answer's clothes are part of the answer. A one-way ticket does not just risk a wrong report; it loses the thread, because the next question — "is spring 100 inside the old harbour?" — cannot even be asked of a step-count.

Scope, honestly. What P16 gives: verdicts, strategies, formulas, and whole futures carried between problems whose dictionaries keep the three promises — work done once and worn everywhere, which is P05's promise at its widest. What it does not give: (i) exactness is the whole license — a dictionary that carries seven of eight relations carries nothing safely (Exhibit 1; and §8's census shows that near-misses are a thousandfold more common than certificates, which is why the checks are the move and the resemblance is only the lead); weaker cousinships — same counts, same sizes, same look — each have honest names and honest prices, and none of them is this move. (ii) The dictionary carries the listed relations and nothing else: questions about the un-carried dress — are the cards even, is 2 less than 4, what does the winning column weigh — have no standing on the far side, and importing their answers is a smuggle, not a proof (P03's hostile discipline applies to dictionaries most of all). (iii) Finding the dictionary is the hard half — the move names the destination, not the road; the roads are built by other pages' tools (P07's constructions, P02's tabulated rhymes), and when no road exists the obstruction is a finding, priced by P12. What the page closes, it closes; what it defers, it names.

6 · What to try next

Three destinations elsewhere in the book, and a default habit.

  • Flip to the dual (P17). The sibling move, one step wider: instead of relabelling this problem's objects into a solved problem's objects, stand the whole problem on its head — solve the mirrored problem and translate the answer back. The dictionary runs between problems rather than within them, and the two moves share the same ticket-home discipline: Exhibit 3 prices forgetting it in either currency.
  • Lift to a higher dimension (P18). A re-dressing that changes floors: the stubborn two-dimensional problem as the shadow of an easy three-dimensional one. The dictionary pairs objects across dimensions, and the move's three promises apply unchanged — one-for-one, relations carried, both ways.
  • The universal property argument (P31). The profession this page apprentices you for, some tiers up the ladder: describe an object by its job, not its material, and anything with the same job is the same object, up to a dictionary — structure over labels made law. The ladder to it runs through this page, honestly marked.

And the default habit, in one sentence: when a problem resists, ask what it is a copy of — pin the relations the question needs (P01), name a solved sibling, and hunt the dictionary: one-for-one, relations carried, both directions; if the dictionary exists, stop solving — translate, harvest, come home; and if it does not, say so out loud, because a named obstruction is a finding and a squashed one is a forgery.

7 · Lineage

The oldest exhibit on this page is a square, not a theorem. The Luoshu — the "Nine Halls" diagram, named for the Luo River near Luoyang in China — arranges the numbers 1 to 9 so that rows, columns, and diagonals all sum to fifteen, and its legend has been told for well over two thousand years of a turtle rising from the Luo flood with the pattern on its shell. Whatever the turtle's role, the placement is a genuine find, and in this page's language it is exactly a dictionary: the card game's entire win-structure — the eight triples of §2 — filed as a picture, with the tailoring so exact that the puzzle tradition built a parlour game on it (Number Scrabble) and the schoolyard built a board game on its twin. The first magic square in the world was, in modern terms, an isomorphism certificate.

The word is younger and louder. In 1819 the German chemist Eilhard Mitscherlich, studying crystals, noticed that compounds of similar chemical constitution tend to crystallise in the same form, and gave the phenomenon the name isomorphism — equal shape — which is this page's promise, discovered in minerals before mathematics. The name crossed over within decades: Arthur Cayley's 1854 paper in the Philosophical Magazine — On the theory of groups, as depending on the symbolic equation \(\theta^n = 1\) — defined a group by its laws alone, labels stripped, making it possible for the first time to prove that two differently dressed objects are the same object. And in 1872 Felix Klein's Erlangen program — the Vergleichende Betrachtungen — ran the idea at full width: a geometry is the study of what a chosen group of transformations leaves invariant, so which structure you keep decides which geometry you are in, and the dictionaries between geometries became a classification of the geometries themselves. The modern profession — P31's universal properties, the working mathematician's daily "up to isomorphism" — is the same sentence with the particular objects left out. None of this history carries the page's weight: the three promises and the paid return ticket carry it. But the lineage explains why the word is Greek and the wardrobe is stocked: mathematicians have been buying clothes through this exact license for two centuries, and the receipts are all in order.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — The dictionary, built and checked. (a) Show that exactly eight triples of distinct cards from 1 to 9 sum to fifteen, and list them. (b) Verify §2's square both ways: every winning triple sits on a line, every line carries a winning triple, and nothing is left over on either side. (c) Translate the played game 5, 9, 2, 8, 7, 3, 6 cell by cell, name the winning line, and check that no earlier position is a win for either player.

Hint

(a) Enumerate by smallest card: fix the first card, and the second leaves at most one choice for the third. (b) Eight lines — three rows, three columns, two diagonals — against the eight triples; count the pairings. (c) Check every partial hand of the leader after each pick, both players.

Worked

(a) Smallest card 1: pairs (5, 9) and (6, 8) — {1,5,9}, {1,6,8}. Smallest 2: (4, 9), (5, 8), (6, 7) — {2,4,9}, {2,5,8}, {2,6,7}. Smallest 3: (4, 8), (5, 7) — {3,4,8}, {3,5,7}. Smallest 4: (5, 6) — {4,5,6}. Smallest 5 or more: the third card would be at most as large as the second, no new triples. Eight exactly, and the count is forced, not sampled. (b) The pairing table of Example 1 lists all eight rows; reading it left to right, each triple is a line; right to left, each of the eight lines appears exactly once; the game's census (eight triples) and the board's census (eight lines) close with nothing unmatched. (c) A's hands grow {5}; {5,2}; {5,2,7} — 5 + 2 + 7 = 14, not a win, and no earlier hand holds a triple either; the win arrives only at the last pick: {5,2,7,6} contains {2,6,7} = 15. B's hands along the way: {9}; {9,8}; {9,8,3} — pairs sum 17, 12, 11, the triple 20: no win. And the two-threat fork is visible in the threat ledger: after A's 7, A threatens 3 (5+7+3) and 6 (2+7+6) simultaneously — the fork B could not cover, exactly as the board showed.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The tower's paces, verified. (a) Play the three-disc tower out by hand: give the seven-move schedule with pegs, and check every move is legal. (b) Verify the trailing-zero rule on the schedule, and each disc's pace \(2^{n-d}\). (c) The billionth move of a tower of thirty discs: which disc moves? And the legend's arithmetic: how many moves does a 64-disc tower take, how many digits, and how many years at one move per second?

Hint

(a) To move three discs: carry two aside, move the big one, rebuild. (b) Write the counts 1 to 7 in binary. (c) \(10^9 = 2^9 \cdot 1953125\); \(2^{64}\) by squaring: \(2^{16}\), then square twice more; a Julian year is 31,557,600 seconds.

Worked

(a) The schedule, moves 1 to 7: disc 1 A to C; disc 2 A to B; disc 1 C to B; disc 3 A to C; disc 1 B to A; disc 2 B to C; disc 1 A to C. Legality, move by move: disc 1 is on top whenever it moves; disc 2 lands on an empty B at move 2 and on an empty C at move 6 (disc 1 sits on A after move 5); disc 3 lands on an empty C at move 4. No move ever places a larger disc on a smaller, and the tower lands complete on C. (b) The counts in binary: 001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110, 111 — trailing zeros 0, 1, 0, 2, 0, 1, 0 — discs 1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 1: the schedule's column, exactly. Paces: disc 1 moves at counts 1, 3, 5, 7 — four moves, \(2^{3-1}\); disc 2 at counts 2, 6 — two moves, \(2^{3-2}\); disc 3 at count 4 — one move, \(2^{3-3}\); total \(4 + 2 + 1 = 7 = 2^3 - 1\). (c) \(\mathrm{tz}(10^9)\): \(10^9 = 2^9 \cdot 1953125\) and 1953125 is odd, so nine trailing zeros — disc 10 moves, in the head. The full 64-disc count: \(2^{64} - 1\). Build \(2^{64}\) by squaring: \(2^{16} = 65536\); \(65536^2 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296 = 2^{32}\); \(4{,}294{,}967{,}296^2 = 18{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}616 = 2^{64}\). So the count is \(18{,}446{,}744{,}073{,}709{,}551{,}615\) — twenty digits — and at one move per second, dividing by 31,557,600 seconds per Julian year, the game runs 584,542,046,090 years and change: about 584.5 billion years. The temple is safe.

Exercise 3 ●●● — What a dictionary carries — and what it leaves behind. (a) Prove the strategy-transfer claim in full: if the second player had a winning strategy in the fifteen-game, translate it through §2's dictionary and derive the contradiction with tic-tac-toe's draw. State exactly what the dictionary does to moves, to wins, and to whole strategies. (b) The even-number trap: a player reasons "2, 4, 6 are all even — a natural set — so their cells should form a line." Refute it: locate the three cells, check the claim, and name the relation that was smuggled in. (c) List the relations the dictionary actually carries for the fifteen-game, and give one question about the cards the board cannot hear and one about the cells the cards cannot hear.

Hint

(a) The dictionary pairs moves with moves and wins with wins; a strategy is a rule for choosing moves, and rules about paired objects pair too. (b) Evenness is a fact about the dress. (c) §2 pinned the win-relations; nothing else was ever checked, so nothing else is licensed.

Worked

(a) The dictionary is a function — card to cell — and it pairs: each unclaimed card with an unmarked cell (so a legal move translates to a legal move); each partial hand with the matching partial board position; and each winning triple with a winning line (Example 1's eight-row table, all eight, both ways). A strategy is a rule that reads a position and names a move; composing the three pairings with the rule translates it into a rule that reads a board position and names a cell — a tic-tac-toe strategy for the same player. If the card game's second player could force a win, the composed strategy would force a win for the second player in tic-tac-toe; tic-tac-toe with best play is a draw, so no player can force a win; contradiction. The same argument with the players swapped kills the first player's supposed win. The draw verdict imports — and note what did the proving: not card analysis but the pairing's three promises. (b) The cells: card 2 is top-right, card 4 top-left, card 6 bottom-right — two top corners and the bottom-right corner: not a line; and 2 + 4 + 6 = 12, not fifteen, so the cards are not even a winning set. "All even" is a relation of the dress — parity — which the win-structure never asked the dictionary to carry; the instinct dressed a costume-fact in structure's clothing. That is the smuggle §5's scope note prices. (c) Carried: claimability (each card once, each cell once), turn order, and the win-relation (the eight triples are exactly the eight lines). Not carried, in both directions: the cards cannot hear "these two cells share an edge" (cells 4 and 9 are adjacent; the cards 4 and 9 have no relation beyond winning together); the board cannot hear "is this card even", "which card is larger", "what do the two cards sum to" — the sums exist in the card world but only the eight fifteen-sums have board meaning, which is the whole point: the dictionary carries the listed relations and nothing else.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Referee three students. Student A: "The fifteen-game and tic-tac-toe both have nine positions and eight winning sets — the counts agree, so the games are the same." Student B: "The dictionary is just renaming — nothing can be proved by it that we could not have proved directly." Student C: "Isomorphic problems are the same problem — so any fact about the cards must be a fact about the cells." For each: verdict, the section that settles it, and the confusion named.

Hint

A stopped one promise short. B has never watched a verdict arrive without analysis. C has never asked a question the dictionary cannot hear.

Worked

A — reject: matching counts are necessary, not sufficient; the verdict requires a dictionary that carries all eight win-relations, and §5's first exhibit is a bijection with matching counts that carries only four (§8's census adds that the reading-order family runs 2,824 deep — count-agreement is cheap, certificates are rare). The confusion: equinumerosity mistaken for isomorphism — sizes pair, structures need not. B — reject: the draw verdict arrived for the fifteen-game with zero lines of card analysis (Example 1), and the harvest law — a hundred coupled feeds collapsed to a formula — is Example 3 arriving the same way; renaming carries proofs precisely because the promises carry the relations the proofs run on. The confusion: translation mistaken for typography — the labels are empty, the relations are load-bearing. C — reject: isomorphic means same structure, not same objects; the dictionary carries exactly the listed relations, so questions about the un-carried dress — evenness, order, adjacency — have no standing on the far side, and Exercise 3(b)'s even-number trap prices the error in one line. The confusion: the verdict "same problem" heard as "same things". All three stumble on one root: the license is the checked promise, not the word "isomorphism" — the word is cheap, the receipt is the move.

Exercise 5 ◐ — The wardrobe audit, by machine. §2's dictionary is one of how many? (a) Prove that any dictionary carrying all eight win-relations must place 5 in the center, the even cards in the corners, and the odd cards on the edges. (b) Count the full dictionaries by hand, then verify the count by brute force: enumerate all \(9! = 362{,}880\) arrangements and tally how many lines each carries. (c) Report the census — how many arrangements carry exactly \(k\) lines, for each \(k\) from 0 to 8 — and check the average against the double-counting prediction of P70: 8 lines times 8 triples times the probability a fixed line carries a fixed triple, \(3! \cdot 6!/9! = 1/84\), giving \(64/84 = 16/21\). (d) Exhibit 1 claimed that a four-of-eight dictionary carries nothing safely — what does the census say about near-misses, and what is the honest status of a seven-of-eight dictionary?

Hint

(a) Count memberships: how many triples contain each card, how many lines contain each cell. (b) Fix 5, put 2 in a corner, and chase what the diagonal forces. (c) 362,880 arrangements, eight lines each to check — a minute of machine time. (d) Compare 8 with 32 and 2,824.

Worked

(a) Membership counts on the card side: 5 lies in four triples ({1,5,9}, {2,5,8}, {3,5,7}, {4,5,6}); each even card in three (2: {2,4,9}, {2,5,8}, {2,6,7}; and likewise 4, 6, 8); each odd corner-card in two. On the cell side: the center lies in four lines, each corner in three, each edge in two. A dictionary carrying all eight matches line-memberships to triple-memberships, so the multisets must agree: \(\{4, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2\}\) against \(\{4, 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2, 2, 2\}\) — forcing 5 to the center, the evens to the corners, the odds to the edges. (b) Place 5, then place 2 in any corner — four ways — and the diagonal through 2 forces the opposite corner: its line with the center must be a triple, and the only triple containing 2 and 5 is {2,5,8}, so 8 sits opposite. The two remaining corners take 4 and 6 in either order — two ways — and then every edge is forced: the edge between two corners completes a unique triple ({2,4,9} puts 9 between the 2-corner and the 4-corner, and so on), and consistency holds to the end. Count: \(4 \times 2 = 8\), and the brute force agrees — exactly 8 of the 362,880 arrangements carry all eight lines; they are §2's square under its eight rotations and reflections. (c) The census:

(d) Near-misses are common: 2,824 arrangements carry four (Exhibit 1's family), 32 carry seven. But the honest status of a seven-of-eight dictionary is the same as a four-of-eight one: a lead, not a certificate — one win-relation is untranslated, and any play that needs it (a block, a fork, a win) breaks exactly there. The license is all eight or nothing; the census's lesson is that the wardrobe is mostly forgeries, which is why this page's move is the checking and not the noticing.
