Example 3 (geometry) — the triangle that cannot close

The claim: a triangle has at most one right angle. The negation hands us a figure: a triangle \(ABC\) with right angles at \(A\) and at \(B\). Draw it — the assumption is a picture now, and pictures can be squeezed.

Two crashes, one external and one internal, and watching both is the point. The external: the angles sum to \(90^\circ + 90^\circ + C = 180^\circ + C\), which exceeds \(180^\circ\) for every possible \(C\) — and the angle-sum theorem of triangles is already on the ladder, proven long before this page. The assumed figure collides with a standing theorem. The internal, worse: draw it honestly. Base \(AB\); the third vertex \(C\) sits above it; the side from \(A\) toward \(C\) and the side from \(B\) toward \(C\). A right angle at \(A\) makes \(AC\) perpendicular to \(AB\); a right angle at \(B\) makes \(BC\) perpendicular to \(AB\) as well — and two lines perpendicular to the same line are parallel, another standing fact. Parallel lines never meet. But \(AC\) and \(BC\) are supposed to meet at \(C\). The assumed figure defeats itself: the vertex \(C\) that both sides must share is a point that perpendiculars refuse to produce.

Sharpness — the boundary is exactly one. A triangle with one right angle is the healthiest object in school geometry: the \(3\)-\(4\)-\(5\) triangle carries angles of \(90^\circ\), \(36.87^\circ\) and \(53.13^\circ\), and no theorem complains. The census agrees over three hundred random integer triangles: none carries two angles of \(90^\circ\) or more — for the blunt reason that two such angles already sum to at least \(180^\circ\), leaving the third angle nothing to be. One right angle: life. Two: contradiction. The claim's edge is exact.
