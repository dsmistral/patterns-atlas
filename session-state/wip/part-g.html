7 · Where the move came from

Non-load-bearing, and honest about it: the page above works if this section is skipped. But the move has a two-thousand-year paper trail, and its early chapters are the best warning label the pattern has.

The struggle before the device. Proclus, writing in the fifth century, already records attempts to purge Euclid's fifth postulate from the Elements — it reads like a theorem, and geometers treated it as one that had merely misplaced its proof. John Playfair's 1795 edition gave the postulate the form this page used for \\(S\\). Girolamo Saccheri's 1733 Euclides ab omni naevo vindicatus mounted the refutation hunt in earnest — assume the rival, find the contradiction — and found, theorem after theorem, a geometry that behaved strangely and contradicted nothing; he published a verdict of "repugnant" that his own lemmas did not support. Johann Lambert's 1766 Theorie der Parallellinien, published posthumously, went further: the rival triangles' angle-shortfall behaved exactly like an area, and Lambert saw what that meant — a world with its own scale — and shelved the manuscript. The device was missing, and without it, the finest geometers could only search harder, which is the one thing this pattern says never settles the question.

The creators, and the missing certificate. Nikolai Lobachevsky built the rival geometry deliberately and published it as "O nachalakh geometrii" — On the principles of geometry — in the Kazanskii vestnik across 1829–1830. János Bolyai, twenty-one, wrote his father in late 1823 with the sentence the whole subject keeps: aus dem Nichts habe ich eine neue, andere Welt erschaffen — out of nothing I have created a strange new world; the world appeared in print in 1832, as an appendix to a book of his father's. Gauss had the same geometry in his notebooks and advised silence. All three had the theorems. None had the certificate — the proof that the strange world could exist — and without it, their geometry stood one undiscovered contradiction from vanishing.

The device, made rigorous. Eugenio Beltrami's 1868 Saggio di interpretazione della geometria non-euclidea — Giornale di Matematiche 6, 284–312 — supplied it: the rival world built inside a region of the ordinary plane, so that its consistency became Euclid's problem, transferred and honest. Felix Klein recast the construction projectively in 1871 (Über die sogenannte Nicht-Euklidische Geometrie, in the Mathematische Annalen), giving the disk its cross-ratio wardrobe. Henri Poincaré's 1882 Théorie des groupes fuchsiens (Acta Mathematica 1, 1–62, December) gave the disk of Example 3 its angles — conformal, census-friendly, and since the most-built world in mathematics. In one decade, independence stopped being a scandal and became a certificate.

The logician's century. Thoralf Skolem in 1934 built the first nonstandard models of arithmetic — worlds satisfying arithmetic's first-order axioms with elements past every counting number, Example 2's idea at arithmetic scale. Abraham Robinson's 1961 paper (Non-standard analysis, KNAW Proceedings A 64, 432–440) made the infinitesimal world a working calculus — Leibniz's \\(dx\\), cleared after two centuries of Berkeley's complaints, by the two-worlds device. And the device went to its highest court: Kurt Gödel announced in December 1938 (PNAS 24, 556–557) the constructible universe \\(L\\), a world of set theory where the continuum hypothesis holds; Paul Cohen, in two 1963–64 papers (PNAS 50, 1143–1148, then Part II in 51), built worlds where it fails — forcing, the two-worlds move industrialized, recognized with the Fields Medal in 1966, the only one logic has received. From Proclus's marginalia to a medal in Paris: the pattern's history is the story of mathematics learning that some silences are theorems, and that the way to prove one is to build.

Exercise 1 ●○○ · Worlds by hand

(a) Check \\(\\mathcal W_2\\)'s identity law by hand, and exhibit the pair of products that witnesses noncommutativity. Hint

For (a): the identity law has two halves — \\(e \\cdot u\\) and \\(u \\cdot e\\); check both columns against both rows. For (b): the only elements are \\(e\\) and \\(a\\); what can \\(a \\cdot a\\) be, and in each case, which pairs \\(u \\cdot v\\) with \\(u \\neq v\\) even exist to commute? For (c): rename \\(e \\mapsto 0\\), \\(a \\mapsto 1\\), \\(b \\mapsto 2\\) and read the table as a sum table.

Worked answer

(a) \\(e\\)'s row reads \\((e, a, b)\\) and every row's \\(e\\)-entry reads the row's own element: identity holds both ways. The witness pair: \\(a \\cdot b = b\\) but \\(b \\cdot a = a\\) — one table, two unequal products, the disagreement at \\(S\\) exactly. (b) In a two-element monoid the only unordered pair of distinct elements is \\(\\{e, a\\}\\), and \\(e \\cdot a = a = a \\cdot e\\) by the identity law itself — commutativity has nothing left to fail on. The deeper reading is the funnel's: order \\(2\\) cannot refute \\(S\\), though it also cannot prove it — silence both ways, which is why Exhibit A insists the census is not the certificate. (c) With \\(e \\mapsto 0\\), \\(a \\mapsto 1\\), \\(b \\mapsto 2\\), the table reads \\(1 + 1 = 2\\), \\(1 + 2 = 0\\), \\(2 + 2 = 1\\) — addition mod \\(3\\), abelian since \\(\\mathbb{Z}/3\\) is; in the table's own terms \\(a \\cdot b = e = b \\cdot a\\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ · The infinite element by hand

(a) Using only the sign rule, prove \\(x > n\\) for every natural number \\(n\\). (b) Prove \\(0 < \\varepsilon = \\frac{1}{x} < \\frac{1}{n}\\) and \\(n \\cdot \\varepsilon < 1\\) for every \\(n\\). (c) Verify the exact receipt \\(\\frac{1}{1-\\varepsilon} - (1 + \\varepsilon + \\varepsilon^2 + \\varepsilon^3 + \\varepsilon^4) = \\frac{\\varepsilon^5 x}{x-1}\\), and say why the school reflex \\(\\frac{1}{1-\\varepsilon} \\approx 1\\) is, in this world, exactly as wrong as it looks.

Hint

The sign rule: \\(\\frac{p}{q} > 0\\) when leading coefficients of \\(p\\) and \\(q\\) share sign; compare two elements by applying the rule to their difference. For (c): put everything over the common denominator \\((x-1)\\) or work with \\(\\varepsilon = \\frac{1}{x}\\) directly — and remember the finite geometric sum is an identity in any field, no limits invited.

Worked answer

(a) \\(x - n\\) has numerator with leading coefficient \\(1\\), denominator \\(1\\): positive by the rule, so \\(x > n\\), uniformly in \\(n\\). (b) \\(\\varepsilon = \\frac{1}{x} > 0\\) (leading \\(1\\) over \\(1\\)); \\(\\varepsilon - \\frac{1}{n} = \\frac{n - x}{nx}\\), leading numerator coefficient \\(-1\\), so negative — \\(\\varepsilon < \\frac{1}{n}\\). And \\(n\\varepsilon - 1 = \\frac{n - x}{x}\\), negative likewise. An element bigger than every \\(n\\), one smaller than every \\(\\frac{1}{n}\\), neither zero nor infinitesimal in the axioms' eyes — both certified by the rule. (c) In any field, \\((1-\\varepsilon)(1 + \\varepsilon + \\cdots + \\varepsilon^4) = 1 - \\varepsilon^5\\), so the difference \\(\\frac{1}{1-\\varepsilon} - (1 + \\cdots + \\varepsilon^4) = \\frac{\\varepsilon^5}{1-\\varepsilon}\\); here \\(1 - \\varepsilon = \\frac{x-1}{x}\\), so the residue is \\(\\frac{\\varepsilon^5 x}{x-1}\\), exactly the machine's receipt. The reflex says "small denominator perturbation, answer near \\(1\\)" — and \\(\\frac{1}{1-\\varepsilon}\\) is indeed within \\(\\varepsilon^5\\)-scale of the partial sum — but the partial sum itself differs from \\(1\\) by \\(\\varepsilon + \\varepsilon^2 + \\varepsilon^3 + \\varepsilon^4\\), which no natural-number multiple of anything makes small in this world. The school intuition assumed an Archimedean ruler; the world declines to provide one, which is precisely the independence.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The disk by hand

(a) Derive the geodesic equations \\(2P \\cdot C = |P|^2 + 1\\) from the orthogonality demand. (b) Compute the hypotenuse-geodesic of \\(T(0.5)\\) — center, radius — and verify the vertex angle \\(30.9638°\\) at \\((0.5, 0)\\). (c) The defect ratio law: verify the ratios \\(0.2548\\), \\(0.0408\\), \\(0.0004\\) against the census, and explain the \\(s^2\\) in "halve the triangle, quarter the defect".

Hint

For (a): a circle centered \\(C\\) through \\(P\\) is orthogonal to the unit circle exactly when \\(|C|^2 = r^2 + 1\\), and \\(r^2 = |C - P|^2\\) — expand and subtract. For (b): the tangent to the arc at \\((0.5,0)\\) is perpendicular to the radius vector from \\(C\\); the angle sought is between that tangent and the diameter. (a) Orthogonality to the unit circle: \\(|C|^2 = r^2 + 1\\). Center \\((1.25, 1.25)\\), radius \\(\\sqrt{1.25^2 + 1.25^2 - 1} = \\sqrt{2.125} = 1.4577\\), orthogonal since \\(|C|^2 - r^2 = 1\\). At \\((0.5, 0)\\) the radius vector is \\((-0.75, -1.25)\\); a tangent vector along the arc toward \\((0, 0.5)\\) is \\((-1.25, 0.75)\\), and the diameter direction back toward the origin is \\((-1, 0)\\). The cosine of the angle between them is \\(\\frac{1.25}{\\sqrt{1.25^2 + 0.75^2}} = \\frac{1.25}{1.4577}\\), and the angle is \\(\\arccos 0.8575 = 30.9638°\\). (c) The census: defects \\(T(0.25): 7.1527°\\), \\(T(0.5): 28.0725°\\), \\(T(0.1): 1.1459°\\), \\(T(0.01): 0.0115°\\) — ratios \\(0.2548\\), \\(0.0408\\), \\(0.0004\\), against the squared side ratios \\(0.25\\), \\(0.04\\), \\(0.0004\\). The disk is conformal — angles true, so no first-order distortion — and its metric inflates by \\(1 + \\mathcal{O}(|z|^2)\\): the curvature is a second-order correction, so area effects scale like \\(s^2\\). Exercise 4 ◆ · The diagnosis — cancellation

A colleague claims: "cancellation — \\(a \\cdot b = a \\cdot c\\) implies \\(b = c\\) — follows from associativity and identity; the left side pins the right." You own this page's move. Diagnose the claim: is it provable, refutable, or independent? Produce whatever worlds the verdict needs, at the smallest order where they exist, and say what the diagnosis teaches that Example 1's funnel does not.

Hint

Reach for the two worlds before searching for any proof. What does cancellation need to fail — which triple of elements, and how small can a table be and still contain one? And on the other side: which small monoids cancel perfectly? Re-examine the order-\\(2\\) census before assuming it is silent about everything.

Worked answer

Independent — and the smallest worlds suffice, which is the exercise's point. \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash\\) cancellation: take the two-element monoid \\(\\{e, a\\}\\) with \\(a \\cdot a = a\\) (identity and all \\(8\\) associativity triples hold); then \\(a \\cdot e = a = a \\cdot a\\) with \\(e \\neq a\\) — cancellation fails, witnessed at order \\(2\\). \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash \\neg\\) cancellation: \\(\\mathbb{Z}/2\\), the other two-element monoid, is a group and cancels perfectly; so does \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) of Example 1. Soundness closes both directions, and the verdict is in. The lesson Example 1's funnel under-teaches: the order-\\(2\\) census was silent about commutativity but is decisive about cancellation — the two-element monoids separate it, with \\(a \\cdot a = a\\) on one side and \\(a \\cdot a = e\\) on the other. Separating power is a property of the pair (axioms, statement), not of the pool size: a census is silent or eloquent depending on what you ask it, and the diagnosis skill is knowing which questions a given pond can answer. That is why the pattern builds worlds on demand instead of enlarging censuses on faith.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The other failure — the sphere

Playfair's statement fails in two directions: too many parallels (the disk), or none at all. Explore the sphere: points on the surface, "lines" as great circles. (a) Show the angle sum of a spherical triangle exceeds \\(180°\\), and compute one. (b) What breaks when you try to use the sphere as the second world for \\(S\\) — which axioms of neutral geometry does it refuse? (c) What does the trichotomy — disk, plane, sphere — suggest that no single two-worlds argument can prove?

Hint

For (a): a great-circle triangle, say with a vertex at the north pole and two right angles there, is already enough. For (b): how many great circles join the north pole to the south pole? And can betweenness survive on a closed curve? For (c): the three angle-sums \\(<\\, 180\\), \\(=\\, 180\\), \\(>\\, 180\\) — what would a classification of all worlds need, that two worlds never supply?

Worked answer

(a) Take the triangle with vertices: north pole, \\((1, 0, 0)\\), \\((0, 1, 0)\\) — two meridians from the pole meeting the equator, plus a quarter of the equator. Both pole angles are \\(90°\\), and the equator corner is \\(90°\\): the sum is \\(270°\\), excess \\(90°\\), and the excess is the area — the sphere runs the disk's defect law with the sign flipped, one more letter in Lambert's drawer. (b) Two things break, at different depths. The naive sphere fails uniqueness of the line through two points: antipodal pairs — pole to pole, say — are joined by every meridian at once, so "exactly one line" fails before parallels are even reached. The repair — identify antipodal points, the elliptic plane — restores uniqueness but at the cost of the order axioms: a "line" becomes a closed loop, betweenness loses its meaning, and the honest certificate is that the elliptic plane refutes Playfair for a different, smaller axiom list than the disk refutes it for. So the sphere is a witness for the curvature phenomenon, not a clean second world against \\(S\\) — the diagnosis of which axioms a world obeys is itself part of the work, and sloppiness here is how independence claims get retracted. (c) The three worlds sort the angle-sums \\(<\\), \\(=\\), \\(>\\) \\(180°\\), paralleling "many, one, none" — and the open end is exactly the pattern's boundary: a trichotomy over all worlds is a classification theorem, not a two-worlds certificate, and it needs the machinery of the far doors (P49's ultraproducts, or the full force of the geometry) to even state honestly.
