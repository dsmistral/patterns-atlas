The Patterns Atlas · P35 · the first move of tier 4

Build two worlds

To show that no proof will ever come, build two worlds that obey every axiom and disagree about the one statement — and the axioms' silence becomes a theorem, witnessed.

New word: independence. A statement \\(S\\) is independent of an axiom list \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) when the axioms prove neither \\(S\\) nor its negation: the question is real, and the axioms are silent. Silence is hard to prove — no census can count every proof that will ever be written. The device that proves it is a pair of worlds: two structures that interpret the same language, each certified to obey every axiom, one making \\(S\\) true and the other making it false. A proof from the axioms is blind to everything the axioms do not say, so it cannot choose between the worlds; if a proof of \\(S\\) existed, it would hold in every world that obeys the axioms, and one of ours does not. This page builds three such pairs — rival multiplication tables, an ordered field with an infinite element, a curved disk where triangles bulge — certifies each world by machine census, and watches a claim about all proofs ever fall to two finite, checkable objects.

1 · Before we begin

P34 ended with a debt. Its machine found a sentence \\(G\\) — true, in the honest reading, and provable in no consistent system containing arithmetic — and the finding was conditional twice over: if the axioms are consistent, and if "unprovable so far" could be promoted to "unprovable ever". The first condition is not dischargeable from inside (that is the second incompleteness theorem, the deeper of P34's two silences). The second is this page's business, and it is harder than it looks: "no proof exists" is a claim about an ocean of finite objects — every proof that could ever be written — and P25's censuses only ever drained ponds. P11 taught refutation: assume the opponent's claim, run the machine, watch it break. But contradiction needs the opponent to show its face, and "a proof exists" wears a thousand masks.

Other pages hold the tools this page assembles. P01: before arguing about independence, say exactly what the axioms are, what the statement says, and what a world formally is — three pins this page drives before any construction. P34: the codebook — axioms, statements, and proofs are finite syntax, so the question "which proofs exist" is a question about listable objects, and the answer can be a construction. P25: when the possibilities are finite, the machine counts them — every world below is certified by a census, not by plausibility. P03: stress-testing with special cases, upgraded — the special case is here promoted to a full counterworld, the stress-test that proves the theorem it was hired to explore. P07: the world nobody asked for is the auxiliary object par excellence, invented purely to carry a witness no given problem supplied. P11: the closing step is a proof by contradiction in miniature — assume a proof of \\(S\\), and the two worlds must agree, but they do not. And P66: the bridge from "these axioms are obeyed" to "no proof exists" is an induction over proof length — the soundness ladder — climbed once here and then trusted, like every ladder this book builds.

Here is the tension this page resolves. A universal negative about proofs seems to demand an infinite inspection; a construction is finite. The resolution is the axioms' own blindness: a proof can use nothing but the axioms and the rules, so whatever difference two worlds carry beyond the axioms is invisible to every proof, forever. The page's three receipts make that blindness concrete at census size — and the honest fine print is printed up front: the worlds are certified, the soundness ladder is cited, and the one thing this page never claims is that either world is the "real" one. That reserve is not modesty; it is a theorem's worth of discipline, and Exhibit C pays it out.
