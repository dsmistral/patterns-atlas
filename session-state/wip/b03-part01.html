The Euclidean algorithm

To find the largest number dividing two others, keep dividing the bigger by the smaller and keep only the leftovers — the leftovers shrink until the answer is all that remains.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it directly extends B01 (the division algorithm) and B02 (congruence classes). B02 ended with two honest debts. Its scope box promised that the precise condition for dividing a congruence "involves the greatest common divisor" — a tool school never builds. And its Exercise 4 conjectured, without proof, that the invertible classes mod \(n\) are exactly the numbers sharing no factor with \(n\) beyond 1. Both debts route through one missing machine: a fast, factoring-free way to find greatest common divisors. Take a pencil and, in spirit, some scissors; you will cut paper before you read.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Task one. Take a rectangle 1071 units wide and 462 units tall. Cut off the biggest squares you can — as many \(462 \times 462\) squares as fit — then from what remains, again the biggest squares you can, and keep going until nothing is left. How big is the last square you cut?

Do it. Two \(462 \times 462\) squares come off first (using \(2 \times 462 = 924\) of the 1071 width), leaving a strip 147 wide by 462 tall. Three \(147 \times 147\) squares come off that strip (using \(3 \times 147 = 441\) of its 462 height), leaving a corner 147 by 21. And \(147 = 7 \times 21\), so seven \(21 \times 21\) squares finish the rectangle exactly — nothing left over. The last square is 21 units wide, and that number deserves a second look: 21 divides both sides of the original rectangle (\(1071 = 51 \times 21\) and \(462 = 22 \times 21\)), and — as this page will prove — no larger number does.

Task two. In B02's Exercise 4 you censused the invertible classes: mod 10 they are \(\{1, 3, 7, 9\}\), and the conjecture was that invertibility means sharing no factor with the modulus beyond 1. Checking that for a small modulus is easy. But now try a big one: do 1491 and 2026 share a factor? The obvious route is factoring — and factoring by trial division means testing candidate divisors one by one up to the square root: for 2026 that is every candidate up to 45 (since \(45^2 = 2025 < 2026\)), for 1491 up to 38 (since \(38^2 = 1444 < 1491 < 39^2 = 1521\)). There is a faster road, and you have already walked it: cut squares, or rather strips. A \(2026 \times 2026\) square leaves a strip 535 wide against 1491 (\(2026 - 1491 = 535\)); two \(535 \times 535\) squares leave a strip 421 wide (\(1491 - 2 \times 535 = 421\)); then the strip widths fall: \(114, 79, 35, 9, 8\), and finally 1. A strip of width 1 measures everything — and nothing bigger survived all the cuts, so 1491 and 2026 share no factor beyond 1. Nine cuts decided it. No factoring anywhere.

Notice what the two tasks share. At every step, the question "what divides both?" was handed from a pair of numbers down to a smaller pair, with the answer unchanged, until it stood alone at the bottom. That hand-me-down is the entire algorithm — one of the oldest in mathematics, and still the workhorse it was twenty-three centuries ago. We now name it.
