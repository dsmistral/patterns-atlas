Example 2 · Vandermonde bought with four small sums

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim), P05 (both sides normalized as polynomials in \(m\) — the canonical form is the price of the degree cap), P02 (the four small counts that feed the certificate).

Pin the claim: Vandermonde's convolution — choosing \(3\) objects from a pile of \(m\) red and \(4\) blue means choosing some \(k\) red and \(3 - k\) blue, over all splits:

\[\binom{m+4}{3} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{3} \binom{m}{k}\binom{4}{3-k} \;=\; 4 + 6m + 4\binom{m}{2} + \binom{m}{3} \qquad \text{for every } m.\]

As a statement about numbers it is proved by a double count — P70's department, planned ahead. But the claim is also, structurally, an identity: both sides are polynomials in \(m\) of degree at most \(3\) (the left side is \(\frac{(m+4)(m+3)(m+2)}{6}\); on the right, \(\binom{m}{3}\) carries the degree \(3\)). So the certificate applies, and the price is four evaluations — each one an honest small count:

\(m\)\(\binom{m+4}{3}\)the four-term sumagreement
0\(4\)\(4 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 4\)\(\checkmark\)
1\(10\)\(4 + 6 + 0 + 0 = 10\)\(\checkmark\)
2\(20\)\(4 + 12 + 4 + 0 = 20\)\(\checkmark\)
3\(35\)\(4 + 18 + 12 + 1 = 35\)\(\checkmark\)

Four agreements against a budget of \(3\): the difference is the zero polynomial, and Vandermonde's convolution holds for every \(m\) — \(m = 10^6\) included, never tested, already settled. Four small additions bought a theorem about infinitely many piles.

The same claim with the \(4\) replaced by an arbitrary \(n\) is an identity in two variables, and it exposes the staircase this page climbs next. In several variables the certificate theorem needs a structural upgrade — a pile of agreeing points is no longer enough (\S5, Exhibit C shows why), but a grid of them is. The grid lemma. If a polynomial \(P(x, y)\) has degree at most \(d_x\) in \(x\) and \(d_y\) in \(y\), and it vanishes on all of \(A \times B\) — every combination of a point of \(A\) with a point of \(B\) — where \(A\) and \(B\) have \(d_x + 1\) and \(d_y + 1\) points respectively, then \(P\) is the zero polynomial. Proof: the one-variable certificate, run twice. Fix any \(a\) in \(A\) and look down the vertical line \(x = a\): the slice \(P(a, y)\) is a one-variable polynomial in \(y\) of degree at most \(d_y\), and it vanishes at all \(d_y + 1\) points of \(B\), so it is the zero polynomial — \(P\) vanishes on the entire line \(x = a\), not just at the tested points. Now collect the vertical lines: write \(P(x, y) = \sum_{i \le d_x} c_i(y)\, x^i\), where the coefficients \(c_i\) are polynomials in \(y\). Each \(a \in A\) gives \(\sum_i c_i(y) a^i = 0\) as a polynomial in \(y\), so each power \(y^j\) separately satisfies \(\sum_i a^i c_{i,j} = 0\), where \(c_{i,j}\) is the coefficient of \(y^j\) in \(c_i\). For each fixed \(j\), that is a one-variable polynomial in \(a\) of degree at most \(d_x\) vanishing at all \(d_x + 1\) points of \(A\): zero again, so every coefficient \(c_{i,j}\) is zero, and \(P\) is the zero polynomial. Down the columns once, across the rows once — the budget spent in two directions.

Applied to the full two-variable Vandermonde \(\binom{m+n}{3} = \sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{3-k}\): both sides have degree \(3\) in each variable, so a \(4 \times 4\) grid of \((m, n)\) values certifies it — sixteen small counts instead of an expansion in two variables. The page's lab runs the full two-variable check exhaustively for all \(m, n \le 8\), and it runs the grid lemma's own census: for every grid with \(|A| = d_x + 1\), \(|B| = d_y + 1\) over the small integers, no nonzero polynomial of the capped degrees vanishes on the whole grid — the lemma is a theorem, and the machine confirms the boundary is exactly where the lemma says (\S8, Ex3 walks the proof by hand).

Moves used, one level up: the claim was normalized into polynomial form (P05's canonical habit), the budget was counted per variable, and the grid — not the pile — was the shape the test set needed; the price is that two-variable certificates cost \((d_x+1)(d_y+1)\) tests, and \(n\) variables cost the product of all of them. That exponential is the crack \S4's last example walks through.
