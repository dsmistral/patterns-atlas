3 · Name the move

P15 · Squeeze it — trap the answer between two computable walls

Shape of the claim. "How much is it?" — asked about a quantity that is well-defined but not directly computable: no formula, no closed form, no finite spelling. The question is quantitative, and it expects a number, not a description.

The move. Do not chase the quantity. Build two families of computable approximations — one proved to sit always below the answer, one always above — and a crank: a parameter (sides \(n\), terms \(k\), steps \(m\)) whose turning provably shrinks the gap between the walls. Then match the crank to the demand: a gap below \(10^{-2}\) certifies two decimals; a gap below every named size at once — the ε-trap — forces the answer exactly.

When to reach for it. When the answer exists but the formula does not — or the formula is beyond your current floor, or too expensive to evaluate. When someone demands a certified value, not an estimate. And when a conjectured value needs walls strong enough to rule out every rival.

The move in words a page long: the quantity is known exactly when it is known to arbitrary precision — because "arbitrary precision" is a statement about the walls, not the prisoner. The two families do all the work; the prisoner never testifies. That inversion — proof without exhibition, knowledge without capture — is the squeeze's signature, and every example below runs on it.

The three demands

A squeeze is legitimate only if all three gates clear, and §5 is a gallery of failures at each:

  • Computable walls. Each wall must be something you can actually evaluate — with rationals, radicals, or an algorithm — and the enclosure must be a theorem. A "wall" that costs more to evaluate than the answer you want is a joke, and a bound with no partner is worse: §5's first exhibit is the one-armed version.
  • Proven enclosure. The inequalities must be proved, not observed. A million agreeing decimals enclose nothing; a wall is a proof, and §5's second exhibit prices the difference between evidence and enclosure.
  • A drivable gap. There must be a crank: a step that provably shrinks the gap, without limit — for every demanded size, some number of cranks gets below it. A trap whose gap has a floor (§5's frozen exhibit) certifies one digit forever, and no amount of cranking changes that.

Where the move appears, and its three-key signature

Appearances. Perimeters and areas cornered by polygons (§2, and §4's first example). Constants certified by their series (§4's second example). Roots cornered by interval halving, and limits pinned exactly by the ε-trap (§4's third example). Every definite integral ever evaluated by sums from below and above. And — least obviously — refutations: an integer trapped strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) cannot exist, and §8's third exercise uses that squeeze to prove \(e\) is not a fraction.

Three keys. The move applies exactly when all three turn:

  1. A quantitative unknown. The target is a number — a length, an area, a value — not a structure or a classification. If the question is not "how much," the trap has nothing to enclose.
  2. Walls in reach. Some computation you already own must sit provably on each side: inscribed and circumscribed polygons, partial sums and tail bounds, test intervals that flip sign.
  3. A crank with no floor. One honest parameter must drive the gap down without limit: doubling, adding terms, halving, refining.

When the first key jams, the question is structural — hand it back to P01 and ask what is actually being demanded. When the second jams, you are not squeezing; you are conjecturing, and §5's second exhibit prices the difference. When the third jams, the walls are real but the trap only closes partway: welcome to one-sided country, where single bounds are theorems in their own right — the frontier where P38 and P56 work.
