Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

The claim on trial: the descent machinery of this page, pointed at new targets, gives correct verdicts. Three students hand in verdicts on new targets. Student A: "\(\sqrt 4\) is irrational. I ran the fraction machine \((4b-a)/(a-b)\) on \(2/1\), and the machine never jams — it runs forever, always descending. Impossible: no fraction equals \(\sqrt 4\)." Student B: "\(x^2 + y^2 = 2z^2\) has no positive solutions. Mod \(2\) forces \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) all even; strip the common \(2\) and the solutions shrink forever — impossible." Student C: "Descent proves no fraction equals \(\sqrt 2\) — but my calculator says \(\sqrt 2 = 1.41421356\), close enough that I'll call it a fraction and be done." Referee: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

For A: what does the machine actually do at \(2/1\) — descend, or stand still? Compute the output. For B: hunt a small witness, the way P03 taught. For C: what is the claim, exactly — equality, or closeness? Which one did the descent prove anything about?

Worked answer

A: rejected — the machine idles, and idling is not descending. At \(2/1\) the \(n = 4\) machine returns \((4 - 2)/(2 - 1) = 2/1\): a fixed point, exactly §5's Exhibit 3. The decrease needs the output's denominator below the input's, which costs \(a < 2b\), which for exact fractions means \(n < 4\); at \(n = 4\) the machine stands perfectly still — "runs forever" with nothing shrinking, which convicts nobody. And the target is a fraction, \(\sqrt 4 = 2/1\), so no working descent could exist: the idle machine is the boundary of Exercise 2 telling the truth. B: rejected — the step is a phantom. The honest census says \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) forces \(x\) and \(y\) to share parity — both odd is legal, and \(z\) is unconstrained. The witness: \((1,1,1)\), since \(1 + 1 = 2 = 2 \cdot 1\); also \((1,7,5)\), since \(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\). Solutions exist, the all-even step was sampled necessity, and the strip refuted nothing — Exhibit 2 word for word. C: half upheld, half corrected — the verdict stands, the sentence fails. The \(\sqrt 2\) descent is exactly §2's, and it is sound. But "close enough to call it a fraction" trades on an unpinned claim: \(141421356/100000000\) approximates \(\sqrt 2\) — within about \(2.4 \times 10^{-9}\), the machine confirms — without equaling it, and equality is the only thing anyone ever denied. Feed the approximating fraction to §2's machine and it walks the same staircase — a dozen shrinking floors — until its nearness runs out and the walk collapses; Exercise 1's fractions end that journey at the jam, and every fraction that is not exactly \(\sqrt 2\) ends it somewhere. None of them lands. Closeness was never the crime; being a fraction is, and no calculator digit settles that question either way. P01's discipline, enforced one last time: pin the claim before the machinery runs.

Exercise 5 ◐ — run the machine backwards

§2's machine walks near-misses down to the jam; run it backwards and it builds them. Starting from \((1,1)\), repeatedly apply \((p, q) \mapsto (p + 2q,\, p + q)\), and you generate \((3, 2), (7, 5), (17, 12), (41, 29), (99, 70), (239, 169), (577, 408)\) — the page's entire supply of near-misses. (a) Verify that \(p^2 - 2q^2\) alternates \(-1, +1\) along the family, and say what the sign says about which side of \(\sqrt 2\) the fraction sits on. (b) Prove that the backwards step is genuinely the machine's inverse: show that the forward machine \((2b - a, a - b)\) sends each family member to the previous one. (c) Watch the errors \(|p/q - \sqrt 2|\): compute the first eight and check they shrink strictly. (d) The family brackets \(\sqrt 2\), tightening like a vice and never landing — the equation \(p^2 - 2q^2 = \pm 1\) has a name and a full theory, and this book defers it, by name. Where?

Hint

For (a): compute the eight values \(1 - 2\), \(9 - 8\), \(49 - 50\), and so on — the alternation is exact, not approximate, and comparing \(p/q\) with \(\sqrt 2\) is comparing \(p^2\) with \(2q^2\). For (b): feed \((3,2)\) to the forward machine; then \((7, 5)\); then generalize with one line of algebra.

Worked answer

(a) The values: \(1 - 2 = -1\), \(9 - 8 = +1\), \(49 - 50 = -1\), \(289 - 288 = +1\), \(1681 - 1682 = -1\), \(9801 - 9800 = +1\), \(57121 - 57122 = -1\), \(332929 - 332928 = +1\) — perfect alternation, starting and ending on the machine's checks. The sign: \(p^2 - 2q^2 < 0\) means \(p^2 < 2q^2\), i.e. \(p/q < \sqrt 2\) — the \(-1\) members sit below, the \(+1\) members above: \(1/1 = 1\) below, \(3/2 = 1.5\) above, \(7/5 = 1.4\) below, \(17/12 \approx 1.4167\) above. The family brackets its target, hop by hop. (b) The forward machine on \((3,2)\): \((2 \cdot 2 - 3, 3 - 2) = (1,1)\) — the previous member. On \((7,5)\): \((10 - 7, 7 - 5) = (3, 2)\). In general, the successor map \((p, q) \mapsto (p + 2q, p + q)\) has forward image \((2(p+q) - (p + 2q), (p + 2q) - (p + q)) = (p, q)\) — one line of algebra, checked on all eight members: the backwards step and the forward machine are exact inverses, so the family is precisely the machine's orbit, run in reverse. (c) The errors: \(|1 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.4142\), \(|3/2 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0858\), \(|7/5 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0142\), \(|17/12 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.00245\), \(|41/29 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.000420\), \(|99/70 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.000072\), \(|239/169 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0000124\), \(|577/408 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0000021\) — strictly shrinking, roughly a factor of \(5\) to \(6\) per hop, the machine confirms on all eight. (d) The equation is Pell's equation, and its theory — why the near-misses exist, why they crowd as they do, and what orbits like this family really are — is deferred, by name, to M01, where \(\sqrt 2\) returns with three proofs and its family in tow. The descent proved no member ever lands; the backwards machine shows the misses marching in anyway, forever — the two facts do not fight; together they are the whole story of how densely the fractions crowd the line.
