5 · When it fails

Three exhibits, one per working part. Each is a place the certificate genuinely breaks — not a hard identity it merely fails to settle, but a way its own logic goes wrong.

Exhibit A · The five-hour clock — a complete census that certifies nothing

Take the difference \(x^5 - x\) into the arithmetic of remainders mod \(5\) — the five-hour clock of B01 and B02, where \(5\) wraps to \(0\). Evaluate the difference at every single residue, an honest and complete census of the room:

\(a\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)\(4\)
\(a^5 - a\)\(0\)\(0\)\(30\)\(240\)\(1020\)
mod \(5\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)

All five residues vanish. The difference agrees with zero at every point of the room — a stronger test than any certificate of \S4 ever ran — and yet \(x^5 - x\) is blatantly not the zero polynomial: its \(x^5\) coefficient is \(1\). The certificate theorem has not failed; it was never licensed. Its budget argument needs \(d + 1 = 6\) distinct test points, and this room has only \(5\) — the degree does not fit in the room, and every conclusion drawn from the census is a type error. What the census actually proved is a different, honest statement: \(a^5 \equiv a \pmod 5\) for every residue \(a\) — Fermat's little theorem in its smallest case, a true fact about the function the clock sees. Over a finite room, two different forms can be the same function: \(x^5 - x\) and the zero polynomial are indistinguishable to the five-hour clock while being different objects, and the repair is always the same — certify in a room that outruns the degree. Over the integers the same polynomial shows its honest budget: \(x^5 - x = x(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)\) has exactly three real roots, three of its five allowed, a vanishing set nowhere near big enough to fake an identity over any room with six points in it.
