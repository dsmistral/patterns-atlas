Exercise 1 ●○○

Charlie's scheme, both ways. His directions are \((1,1)\) — the same road-step Ben walks — and \((2,3)\). (a) His clerk's report of the treasure is \((11,-2)\); recover the school point by walking the columns. (b) Build the reverse dictionary \(M_{\text{school} \to \text{Charlie}}\) by Box B06.A, and check it against the clerk's report. (c) The wreck reads \((6,-1)\) in Charlie's book. Find its school point two ways — by the reverse dictionary and by walking — and then confirm the round trip: treasure, wreck, and origin all come home.

Hint

(a) A reading \((a,b)\) means the point is \(a\) steps of the first direction plus \(b\) of the second — walk it. (b) First write Charlie's own dictionary (columns are his directions), take its \(D\), then apply Box B06.A. (c) The same two tools, run in the other direction; the origin needs no computation at all.

Worked answer

(a) \(11(1,1) - 2(2,3) = (11 - 4,\; 11 - 6) = (7, 5)\) — the treasure, of course. (b) Charlie's dictionary is \(M_{\text{Charlie} \to \text{school}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\) (columns \((1,1)\), \((2,3)\)), with \(D = 1 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 1 = 1\) — healthy, and \(D = 1\) means no fractions anywhere. Box B06.A: the reverse is \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). Check: \((3 \cdot 7 - 2 \cdot 5,\; -7 + 5) = (11, -2)\) — the clerk's report, recovered without re-solving anything. (c) Walk Charlie's \((6,-1)\) first: \(6(1,1) - 1(2,3) = (4, 3)\) — the wreck's school point. Now the reverse dictionary on \((4,3)\): \((12 - 6,\; -4 + 3) = (6, -1)\), Charlie's book entry, reproduced. Round trips: the treasure \((7,5) \to (11,-2) \to (7,5)\); the wreck \((4,3) \to (6,-1) \to (4,3)\); and the origin \((0,0) \to (0,0)\), because the empty journey reads empty in every scheme — B05's origin test, holding quietly at the bottom of every dictionary.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Two notes washed ashore, each claiming to be a surveyor's dictionary. Note 1: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), claiming "the well reads \((2,1)\)". Note 2: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), claiming "the well reads \((2,1)\)" as well. (a) Diagnose both notes, each by one number. (b) For the healthy note, find the well's school point. (c) Build its reverse dictionary and send the school point back through it — the round trip. (d) For the broken note, exhibit a nonzero reading that translates to the school origin — the witness — and say which of B05's two demands the note fails: one, or both?

Hint

Health is still one number: \(D = ps - qr\), read off the claimed columns. For (d), you want a pair of step-counts whose combined walk returns to the start — the two columns are nearly parallel, so a small combination cancels.

Worked answer

(a) Note 1: columns \((2,1)\) and \((1,3)\), \(D = 2 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5 \ne 0\), healthy. Note 2: columns \((2,4)\) and \((1,2)\), \(D = 2 \cdot 2 - 4 \cdot 1 = 0\), broken — and visibly so: \((2,4) = 2 \times (1,2)\), parallel directions. (b) Walk the reading: \(2(2,1) + 1(1,3) = (5, 5)\). (c) Box B06.A with \(D = 5\): the reverse is \(\frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). Send the school point through: \(\frac{1}{5}(3 \cdot 5 - 5,\; -5 + 2 \cdot 5) = \frac{1}{5}(10, 5) = (2,1)\) — home, the note vindicated. (d) Take the reading \((1,-2)\): \(1 \times (2,4) - 2 \times (1,2) = (0,0)\). A genuine journey — one step out, two back — that translates to nowhere. Note 2 fails both demands: its span is the single line through \((1,2)\), not the whole plane (demand one), and the origin has two readings, \((0,0)\) and \((1,-2)\) (demand two) — the two failures always arrive together, as Claim 3 proved.

Exercise 3 ●●●

A route of your own: Elena to Charlie. (a) Compose the through-dictionary \(M_{\text{Elena} \to \text{Charlie}}\) by translating columns: where do Elena's two unit-steps land in Charlie's numbers? (b) Compose it again as the product \(M_{\text{school} \to \text{Charlie}} M_{\text{Elena} \to \text{school}}\). (c) Verify the table on four points: Elena's two unit-steps, her \((1,1)\), and her \((2,1)\) — the treasure's Elena reading from section 3(b). (d) Check the determinant law: the through-table's \(D\) against the legs'. (e) Now compose the reverse route, Charlie to Elena, and watch the Ben–Charlie "coincidence" fail to repeat; verify the reverse route sends the treasure home, and explain why the two tables' determinants are reciprocals.

Hint

For (a), remember what a unit-step is: Elena's \((1,0)\) is the school point \((3,1)\); now read that school point in Charlie's scheme. For (e), the reverse route composes the other pair of legs — school to Elena, then Charlie to school — and the treasure's Charlie reading \((11,-2)\) is sitting in Exercise 1, waiting to be sent home.

Worked answer

(a) Elena's \((1,0)\) is the school point \((3,1)\), which Charlie reads as \((3 \cdot 3 - 2 \cdot 1,\; -3 + 1) = (7, -2)\). Elena's \((0,1)\) is the school point \((1,3)\), reading \((3 - 6,\; -1 + 3) = (-3, 2)\). The columns give \(M_{\text{Elena} \to \text{Charlie}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). (b) The product: \(\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 - 2 & 3 - 6 \\ -3 + 1 & -1 + 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\) — the same table, built from the other honest source. (c) The unit-steps match by construction: \((1,0) \to (7,-2)\), \((0,1) \to (-3,2)\). Elena's \((1,1)\): through-table \((7 - 3,\; -2 + 2) = (4, 0)\); by legs, school \((4,4)\), Charlie \((12 - 8,\; -4 + 4) = (4,0)\) ✓. Elena's \((2,1)\) — the treasure: through-table \((14 - 3,\; -4 + 2) = (11, -2)\), Charlie's own report from Exercise 1, reached from Elena's side of the island. (d) Through-table: \(D = 7 \cdot 2 - (-3)(-2) = 14 - 6 = 8\). Legs: \(D = 8\) for Elena to school, \(D = 1\) for school to Charlie; \(8 \cdot 1 = 8\) — Claim 4, holding. (e) The reverse route: \(M_{\text{school} \to \text{Elena}} M_{\text{Charlie} \to \text{school}} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 3 - 1 & 6 - 3 \\ -1 + 3 & -2 + 9 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\) — plainly not \(\begin{bmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). No coincidence this time; the Ben–Charlie sameness was the involution, a rare guest, and this route is honest enough to differ. Send the treasure home: \(\frac{1}{8}(2 \cdot 11 + 3 \cdot (-2),\; 2 \cdot 11 + 7 \cdot (-2)) = \frac{1}{8}(16, 8) = (2, 1)\) — Elena's treasure reading from section 3(b) ✓. The determinants: \(8\) through, \(\frac{14 - 6}{64} = \frac{1}{8}\) back — reciprocals, exactly as inverse tables must be: composed, the two routes run Elena to Elena, the identity, whose \(D = 1 = 8 \cdot \frac{1}{8}\). The multiplication law, read backwards, is the reciprocal law.
