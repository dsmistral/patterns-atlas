6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P18: Desargues's theorem is self-dual, Exhibit C's mirror that gives nothing back, and its natural proof lifts the whole configuration out of the plane into three dimensions — the projective plane's own repair pointing at a dimension above, which is the next move of the spine. The second door is P24: a function and its Fourier transform are a dictionary pair, and every fact about time-limited signals carries to a fact about band-limited ones, backwards — the same machine, running on functions instead of points, with the same fine print and far bigger harvest. The farthest is P31: at the frontier, "product" and "coproduct" are defined as mirror-image universal properties, two paired kinds of object whose duality is an axiom of the subject rather than a theorem about it — the flip, promoted to a load-bearing wall.

The default habit

When a subject's objects come in two paired kinds — points and lines, vertices and faces, unions and intersections, "for all" and "there exists" — write the dictionary explicitly, one line, no hand-waving. Check the double flip comes home. Check each relation on the certificate list, and note which ones come out reversed. Then price the twin before paying for the problem: if the twin is cheaper, or already solved, or half of a census, collect the harvest. If the twin is the same problem wearing your own face, close the notebook — the flip told you something true anyway: that the problem stands alone.

7 · Where it came from

The flip is old, and its history is a sequence of mathematicians noticing that their subject's bookkeeping ran twice. Girard Desargues, an architect from Lyon, self-published a pamphlet in Paris in 1639 — the Brouillon projet — that introduced points at infinity and treated points and lines as trading partners; the profession was not ready, the pamphlet vanished, and the flip was rediscovered a century and a half later. Blaise Pascal printed a single page in 1639 or 1640 about a hexagon inscribed in a conic; its dual — six lines tangent to a conic meeting three at a time on one line — waited until 1806, when Charles Brianchon, a student at the École Polytechnique, published it, 167 years after the original. Giovanni Ceva's 1678 book De lineis rectis announced his concurrency theorem and, in the same covers, republished the collinearity theorem of Menelaus of Alexandria, second century, from the Sphaerica — the dual pair reunited in one binding without either author knowing they were twins. Euler's 1750 letter to Goldbach (printed in 1758) recorded \(V - E + F = 2\), a formula that under the vertex-face flip becomes \(F - E + V = 2\): the equation is its own dual, which is why it survives the flip untouched. Adrien-Marie Legendre, in a 1787 memoir on minimal surfaces, used the transform that trades points for tangent lines — the grown-up version of this page's dictionary, with \((f^*)^* = f\) as its Promise 1. The 19th-century logician Augustus De Morgan gave the complement laws their modern form. Joseph Gergonne stated the principle of duality as a general policy in 1825 (Jean-Victor Poncelet reached it independently), turning two centuries of paired theorems into a printing press. And in the fall of 1947, John von Neumann's typescript Discussion of a Maximization Problem — the linear-programming dual, where every optimization problem arrives with a mirror problem whose optimum must agree — put the flip to work in the age of machines; the first published version is due to Gale, Kuhn, and Tucker, 1951. Every one of these was the same discovery: the dictionary was checkable, the checks were cheap, and the theorems came in pairs.
