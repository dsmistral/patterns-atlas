Exercise 4 (◆). Diagnose the classmate. A student reports, triumphantly: "\(x^5 - x\) is zero — I checked all five residues mod \(5\) and every single one gave \(0\)." (a) State precisely what is true in the report: what fact did the census actually establish, about what kind of object? (b) State the error: name the room, the degree, the budget, and the exact type confusion the conclusion commits. (c) Prescribe the repair from the book's own shelf: which bridge pages own the room's arithmetic, what check must run before any finite-room census is trusted, and what tool certifies identities in rooms too small for testing — plus the promotion that turns this room's smallness from a bug into a method.

Hint

(a) Fermat's little theorem, smallest case — a statement about every residue in the room. (b) The certificate needs \(d + 1\) points; count the room's size against \(5 + 1\). What two different things can "zero" mean — a function's readings, or a form's coefficients? (c) B01 and B02 own the clock; this page owns the pre-check; P05 owns the tool that never counts roots at all; and \S6's far doors own the promotion.

Worked

(a) True, and honestly earned: for every residue \(a\) mod \(5\), \(a^5 - a \equiv 0\). The census is complete — five residues, five vanishing readings — and the fact is Fermat's little theorem in its smallest case, a true statement about the function the room can see. (b) False: "\(x^5 - x\) is zero" as a claim about the polynomial. The form has an \(x^5\) coefficient of \(1\), so it is not the zero object. The error is a type confusion between two questions: where does the difference vanish? — an equation, and the census answered it: everywhere in this room — and is the difference the zero polynomial? — an identity, whose certificate needs \(d + 1 = 6\) distinct roots in a room holding only \(5\) residues: void. On a finite room, two different forms (\(x^5\) and \(x\)) can be the same function, so no census of the room — complete or not — can ever distinguish them; "void" is not "confirmed". (c) The repairs, in order: B01 and B02 own the clock's arithmetic, so the room is never mysterious; this page's pre-check — does the room outrun the degree? \(6 > 5\), so no — is what should have stopped the conclusion before it started; P05's canonical comparison certifies identities in rooms too small for testing, because coefficient comparison consults the form and never counts roots at all; and testing in a bigger room confirms the budget honestly — over the reals, \(x^5 - x = x(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)\) has exactly three roots, three of its five allowed. The promotion, named in \S6: P30 makes small rooms a method — work one prime at a time, in rooms where the census is complete — but for statements about residues, never for identities of forms.

Exercise 5 (◐). Build both forgeries yourself. (a) A rival announces that his formula \(F(n)\) — the fifth-power sum formula of Example 1 — is "verified by direct check": he summed at \(n = 1\) through \(10\), ten matches. Build in closed form a rival formula that also passes all ten of his checks and is wrong at \(n = 11\); compute its error there. (b) The parabola's grids: prove that no \(2 \times 2\) grid of integer points lies inside \(\{(n, n^2)\}\), and exhibit the largest grid that does lie inside. (c) Guard the book: one sentence each — which single quantity, fixed before any testing, defeats the forgery of (a), and which structural demand on the test set defeats the pile of (b)?

Hint

(a) Add to \(F\) a correction factor that vanishes at each of his ten points — one factor per point — and evaluate the correction at \(n = 11\). (b) A grid \(A \times B\) on the curve forces every \(a^2\) for \(a \in A\) to equal every \(b \in B\); count what that leaves in \(B\). (c) Exhibit B's moral and Exhibit C's moral, each in one clause.

Worked

(a) Take \(G(n) = F(n) + (n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-10)\). At each of his ten test points one of the correction's factors is zero, so \(G\) agrees with \(F\) there — ten for ten, "verified" by his own standard. At \(n = 11\) the correction is \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\), against the true fifth-power sum \(381{,}876\): his method certifies a formula wrong by a factor of nearly ten, and the same construction kills any fixed test set of size \(k\) — the correction is just the product of \((n - t)\) over his points \(t\). Direct check without a degree cap is not weak evidence; it is no evidence, in exactly the sense Exhibit B priced. (b) A \(2 \times 2\) grid \(A \times B\) on the curve needs every \((a, b)\) with \(a \in A\), \(b \in B\) to satisfy \(b = a^2\): then both \(a_1^2\) and \(a_2^2\) must equal both \(b_1\) and \(b_2\), forcing \(b_1 = b_2\) — but \(B\) was required to hold two distinct values. No \(2 \times 2\) grid exists. The largest grid inside is \(2 \times 1\): \(A = \{-1, 1\}\), \(B = \{1\}\), since the two square roots of a shared square supply the two columns and the square itself the single row. The points are a one-dimensional thread, and a thread can dodge every rectangular grid while accumulating points without end. (c) Against (a): the degree cap, fixed from the shape of the claim before any test runs — with the cap, his ten tests were one more than needed and the forgery is impossible to build under it; without it, no run of tests ever suffices. Against (b): the demand that the test set be a grid — or a coin with priced confidence — never a pile: the parabola agrees infinitely often and certifies nothing at any size.
