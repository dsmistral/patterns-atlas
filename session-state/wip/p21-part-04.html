3 · Name the move

P21 · Catch the limit point — halve the room, keep the endless half

When an endless record of attempts is trapped in a closed, bounded room, halve the room: at least one half is hit infinitely often. Keep such a half, halve it again, and repeat forever. The chain of kept halves is a nest of intervals with lengths shrinking to zero, and the one point that survives the whole chain is a limit point of the record: every window around it is hit infinitely often. The machine never reads the whole record, never lists the hits, and at every step leaves a checkable trace — one bit per halving: which half stays endless. What the machine catches is what existence claims about endless records are made of: the floor under traps, the guarantee that maxima are attained and roots are real, the answer to every question of the form the attempts are endless and the room is finite, so where do they crowd?

In words: you cannot read an endless record, but you can interrogate it one bit at a time, and the question is always the same — which half of the room still holds infinitely many attempts? Each answer is provable from the record's rule without listing anything, and the bits trace a path down a binary tree whose branches have lengths halving to zero. At the bottom of that path — no finite depth reaches it — the room has collapsed to a point, and the point is forced, because the line has no holes for the trap to close on. The move has three working parts. First, name the room: bounded, so the trap has walls; closed, so the catch cannot leak out the door — two separate demands, and §5 prices them separately. Second, halve: an endless half always exists, because a finite pile of finite piles is finite — the pigeonhole, promoted from counting to existence. Third, catch: the nested intervals share exactly one point, and that point is a limit point by the most elementary argument on this page: a window that contains a kept interval is hit as often as that interval, which is infinitely often.

Box P21.A · The two halves of the certificate

The machine's guarantee splits into two independent statements, and each one is exactly where it is needed.

Half 1 — the endless half exists (the endless pigeonhole). If a room is split into finitely many parts and hit infinitely often, some part is hit infinitely often. Contrapositive: if every part were hit only finitely often, a finite pile of finite piles would be finite — and the record is not. This is P10 with the word more replaced by endless, and it is the only place the record's infinity enters the machine.

Half 2 — the chain catches exactly one point (the line has no holes). Nested closed intervals \(I_1 \supseteq I_2 \supseteq \cdots\) with lengths tending to zero share at least one point, and at most one point: two survivors would sit no more than \(2^{-n}\) apart for every \(n\), and no two distinct numbers are closer than every positive distance. The at least half is the single assumption this whole engine spends — the no-holes property of the number line, worn here in its nested-interval costume; the least-upper-wall costume is the same coin, spent in §4's second example. A page that hides this is selling you a machine with an unpaid bill.

Neither half alone catches anything: the first without the second is an endless chase, the second without the first an empty trap. The machine is the pair, and the pair is why the move feels like closing a hand — the fingers close, and something is forced to be in the fist.

Box P21.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Convergence itself — a subsequence walking toward a point is the crowd caught in the act, and §4's first example certifies one in public. The extreme value theorem — the loudest point of a continuous signal exists, §4's second example. The intermediate value theorem — a sign change hides a root, §4's third. The fixed points of contractions — P22's Babylonian iteration is an endless record in a room that shrinks, and the shrinking makes the catch unique and computable. The grown-up word for closed-and-bounded — compact — and its deep descendants, among them P54's deliberately completed families. The covering sibling — every open cover of a closed bounded room has a finite subcover, Heine's theorem, named here and built later. One shape under all of them: endless attempts, bounded room, forced crowd.

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the attempts are genuinely endless: a finite card licenses nothing, and §5 shows a record where no window holds more than one hit. Second, the attempts are trapped in a room with walls: a bound, so the trap can close; §5's runaway record shows the wall-free wreckage. Third, the room halves cleanly: each half a room of the same kind, the halves of a closed room staying closed; §5's open room shows the leak. If all three keys turn, the crowd exists, and the only remaining questions — which spot, and is it unique — are questions the machine answers only by running, never by promising.
