The Patterns Atlas · P12 · the twelfth move of the spine

Name the obstruction

A proof that ends at "contradiction, done" has certified that the door is locked — and thrown away the key. Every impossibility leans on a wall: a class no candidate can reach, a count that cannot balance, a parity that refuses to flip. Name the wall, prove it holds for every route at once, and it answers a whole family of questions before you ask them.

New word: obstruction. An obstruction is a named property that blocks a whole family of attempts at once: every candidate that could possibly succeed must satisfy it, and the target violates it. "Cannot be a sum of two squares because it leaves remainder \(3\) on division by \(4\)" is an obstruction — the class \(3 \pmod 4\) is the wall's name. New word: necessity. The wall's first half — "every candidate must satisfy it" — is its necessity, and it is a theorem, never a sample: a property that merely happens to hold for the attempts you happened to try is a coincidence wearing an obstruction's coat. New word: census. The instrument that finds walls: list which classes the reachable objects can occupy — remainders, colours, parities — and the classes nobody can reach stand out by themselves. A census is a count taken over classes, not over candidates; it is what turns one dead target into a sealed family.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, and P11 exploded the opposite of the claim. P11's closing pointer promised exactly this page: a successful contradiction often runs on a quantity that refuses to change; when the refusing quantity blocks a whole family of claims at once, promote it from a proof device to a named obstruction — that promotion is P12's first move, and P11 is its front door.

Here is the tension this page resolves. The good news: you have already built walls without naming them. P09's colour class sealed the chameleon meadow — not "this sequence fails" but "no sequence exists"; P11's parity census cornered \(\sqrt{2}\) — not "these fractions miss" but "every fraction misses"; the mutilated chessboard died on a colour count, not on a search through coverings. Each of those proofs used a wall and threw it away. Name the wall instead — prove it necessary, check the target against it, state the family it seals — and the same one-page argument answers "is \(2027\) a sum of two squares?" and every question shaped like it, instantly, with no search at all. The bad news, paid up front: the move is one-way — a wall rules out, it never rules in; the necessity must be proven for every route, not sampled from the routes you tried — a wall some legitimate route simply walks around is the move's most seductive wrong answer; and walls are family-specific — the census that seals one family may say nothing at all about its next-door neighbour, which is exactly the asymmetry P11's Exercise 3 left open and this page's Example 1 closes.

2 · Meet the problem

Here is a hunt you can run with pencil and patience. The question: can \(2027\) be written as a sum of two squares — whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(a^2 + b^2 = 2027\)? Try it. The squares up to \(2027\) are \(1, 4, 9, \ldots, 44^2 = 1936\), and \(45^2 = 2025\), a whisker away. Work down: \(2027 - 45^2 = 2\), not a square. \(2027 - 44^2 = 91\), no. \(2027 - 43^2 = 178\), no. \(2027 - 42^2 = 263\), no. \(2027 - 41^2 = 346\), no. \(2027 - 40^2 = 427\), no — \(20^2 = 400\), \(21^2 = 441\), and \(427\) sits between them. Ten minutes of this will convince you the answer is no; ten minutes will prove nothing. The candidates never run out until the squares do, and a hunt that finishes is exactly what P02 taught you to distrust: a detector, not a proof.

Now take the census — the hunt, but over classes instead of candidates. Every square leaves remainder \(0\) or \(1\) when divided by \(4\): even squares are \((2k)^2 = 4k^2\), a multiple of \(4\), and odd squares are \((2k+1)^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1\), one more than a multiple of \(4\) — B02's classes, doing their silent work. So a sum of two squares leaves remainder \(0\), \(1\), or \(2\) — never \(3\). And \(2027 = 4 \cdot 506 + 3\): the class \(3 \pmod 4\), dead centre in the wall. The hunt is over before it starts, and so is the hunt for \(3\), for \(7\), for \(11\), for every number that leaves remainder \(3\): one census, infinitely many questions sealed. That is the promotion this page trains — and here is the wall, named:

\[\text{if } n \equiv 3 \pmod 4, \quad n \text{ is not a sum of two squares.}\]

Read the wall backwards, and it becomes a compass. \(2029 = 4 \cdot 507 + 1\) passes the class test — the census is silent about it, which means a search is worth trying — and the very first try lands: \(2029 = 45^2 + 2^2 = 2025 + 4\). The wall did not prove that; it only pointed where the search could not be doomed. And the pointing is honest in the other direction too: \(21 = 4 \cdot 5 + 1\) also passes the class test, and \(21\) is still not a sum of two squares — the hunt for it is short and genuinely exhaustive: with \(b \geq a\), only \(a = 0, 1, 2, 3\) need checking, and \(21\), \(20\), \(17\), \(12\) — none of the four — is a square. But notice what sealed \(21\): not a named family wall — a finite hunt that happened to be small. The full truth about which numbers are sums of two squares is a deeper theorem with its own page (M02), and this page will say exactly what it defers and why.

Wait — catch the move doing its own work in that last paragraph. "Every pair misses" is a detector's sentence, and detectors never seal; the honest census that seals \(21\) belongs to §5, where the wall's one-way nature gets its full price tag. The fine print, paid up front: a named obstruction rules out, never rules in; its necessity must be proven for every candidate, not sampled; and it dies at the family boundary — the mod-\(4\) census says nothing about numbers like \(21\) that live in the passable classes but carry their obstruction somewhere else. The wall you can name is the proof; the wall you cannot name yet is the next page's business.

3 · Name the move

What the census taught, stated once and for all.

Pattern P12 — Name the obstruction. When a hunt stalls, or a proof of impossibility succeeds, do not pocket the verdict and move on: interrogate the argument for the wall it leaned on — the property every candidate must satisfy and the target violates. Then promote that wall to a named obstruction, in three acts. (1) Prove the necessity: show that every candidate the rules could possibly produce satisfies the property — a theorem over all routes, never a sample of the ones you tried; the instrument of choice is a census over classes (remainders, colours, parities, degrees), because a census covers every candidate at once. (2) Check the violation: one computation on the target — \(n \bmod 4 = 3\), the board's colour counts, the graph's odd degrees — and the target is sealed without a single search. (3) State the family: name the whole class the wall seals — "no number \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\)", not "not \(2027\)" — so every future instance answers at the same instant, and the boundary of the family is part of the statement: where the wall ends, the question reopens. Then read the wall backwards: the boundary is a compass, telling you exactly which modified problems are no longer doomed — the eighth bridge, the opposite-colour mutilation, the number that passes the class test.

Or, in words: when something cannot happen, find out which class of somethings cannot happen and say so in a form you can check in one line. A proof that ends "so \(2027\) is not a sum of two squares" is one dead target; a proof that ends "no number leaving remainder \(3\) on division by \(4\) is a sum of two squares" is a wall with a name, and it works forever, on questions you have not met yet. But the wall is a one-way instrument — it rules families out, it never rules a single target in — and its necessity must be proven, not presumed: a wall that some legal route walks around is a phantom, and phantoms are indistinguishable from walls until you check the routes.

Box P12.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The property must be necessary — every route, proven. "Every square leaves remainder \(0\) or \(1\) on division by \(4\)" is a theorem about all squares: even, odd, enormous, imaginary — none exempt, none sampled. A property that holds only for the candidates you happened to try is a coincidence, and a proof that leans on it seals nothing. What breaks: the phantom wall — §5's first exhibit watches a student seal a family with a wall that two legal candidates simply walk around.
  2. The target must violate it — checked, not felt. One honest computation on the target: \(2027 = 4 \cdot 506 + 3\); the mutilated board's colour census; the walk's odd-degree count. If the target satisfies the property, the wall is silent — silence is not a verdict, and §5's second exhibit charges the full price for reading silence as yes. What breaks: the one-way filter billed as two-way — the move's most common misuse.
  3. The family must be stated with its boundary. The wall's power is that it answers questions in advance — but only inside its family. "No \(n \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)" is exactly as wide as the census proved, and not one residue wider; the numbers that pass the test but fail anyway (\(21\)) mark the boundary honestly. What breaks: the family overreach — billing a census for targets it never covered; §5's fifth exhibit catches a proof charging a neighbour for a debt the family never owed.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: an impossibility or a "cannot" — no solution, no route, no arrangement — especially one that will recur as a family ("is this number a sum of two squares?" arriving again and again). Problem shape: the direct hunt is endless (candidates never run out), but the candidates all pass through a few common checkpoints — remainders, colours, parities — so a census can watch all of them at once. Tools at hand: an invariant from P09 or a crash from P11 whose working part is checkable on the target in one line. If the obstruction resists naming — if the proof works but no reusable property falls out — say so and keep the proof; a named wall is a bonus, never a requirement. And if the target passes the check, remember: passing means the wall is silent, and silence was never a verdict.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, three domains, one pose: the wall found, named, and read backwards to a compass. In each, the necessity is a one-paragraph theorem and the violation is a single count.

Example 1 (numbers) — the asymmetry, explained at last

P11's Exercise 3 proved there are infinitely many primes of the form \(4k+3\), then left an honest wound: the same machine fails for primes of the form \(4k+1\), "and obstructions are family-specific". This example names the wall that makes the machine work in one family and die in its neighbour — the cleanest demonstration of the move's third act that the spine can offer.

Why the \(4k+3\) machine worked. Suppose \(p_1, \ldots, p_k\) were all the \(4k+3\) primes and set \(N = 4(p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1\). Then \(N \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and \(N > 1\), so \(N\) has prime factors — and \(N\) is odd, so the prime \(2\), which belongs to no odd family, is not among them. Now the census — the multiplication table of B02's odd classes, confirmed by a check over all four pairs: \(1 \cdot 1 \equiv 1\), \(1 \cdot 3 \equiv 3\), \(3 \cdot 1 \equiv 3\), and \(3 \cdot 3 \equiv 9 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). Read it as a wall: a product of odd prime classes lands in class \(1\) or class \(3\), and it lands in class \(3\) exactly when it contains an odd number of class-\(3\) factors. So \(N \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) forces at least one prime factor \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\) — and no \(p_i\) divides \(N\) (each leaves remainder \(-1\)). The complete list was missing one. That is the obstruction, named: the class-multiplication census — necessity the class table proves for every product, violation one remainder computation on \(N\), family every number \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\) ever to appear.

Why the \(4k+1\) machine cannot work. Try the direct translation: suppose \(q_1, \ldots, q_k\) were all the \(4k+1\) primes, and set \(M = 4(q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k) + 1\). Then \(M \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — and the same class table now reads the other way: class \(1\) is reachable with zero class-\(1\) factors, because \(3 \cdot 3 \equiv 1\). The wall is silent. \(M\) could be prime, or a product of \(4k+1\) primes, or — the fatal case — a product of \(4k+3\) primes in pairs, and the last case supplies no new \(4k+1\) prime at all. Run it on the smallest pretend-list and watch the machine jam: let the list of \(4k+1\) primes be just \(\{5\}\); then \(M = 4 \cdot 5 + 1 = 21 = 3 \times 7\) — every prime factor is \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\), the list survives untouched, and the proof dies with nothing to contradict. The \(4k+1\) primes are still infinite — that truth is not in doubt — but this census cannot see it, and the wall's silence is the honest reason why: the obstruction that seals the \(4k+3\) family does not extend to the neighbours. Infinitely many \(4k+1\) primes needs an engine this ladder has not built yet; the Atlas records the deferral and moves on.

Moves used: P12 (the class-multiplication census promoted from proof device to named wall, then read backwards at the \(4k+1\) boundary), B02 (the class arithmetic), P11 (the Euclid-style machine whose asymmetry this is), P03 (the smallest pretend-list run as the hostile case that exhibits the jam).

Example 2 (the city walk) — the bridges that cannot all be crossed

The question, from 1736: the city of Königsberg sits on both banks of the Pregel and on two islands, joined by seven bridges. Can a Sunday walk cross every bridge exactly once and return home? Try it on paper and the tries blur together — the walks are too many to enumerate by hand, and a stubborn reader might suspect the answer without ever proving it. The machine can settle this particular city: a walk is a sequence of (landmass, bridges-already-used) situations, and there are only \(4 \times 2^7 = 512\) such situations — an exhaustive search of all 512 — an afternoon of patient bookkeeping — finds no walk that uses all seven bridges. Sealed, honestly, for Königsberg — as a detector seals, one city at a time.

Now name the wall that sealed every such city, past and future, in one paragraph. A landmass the walk passes through is entered on one bridge and left on another: each pass consumes bridges in pairs. So every landmass except possibly the walk's two endpoints must have even degree — an even number of bridge-ends touching it. Königsberg's four landmasses carry degrees \(5, 3, 3, 3\) — four odd. Four exceeds two, the pairing breaks, and the walk is impossible; not "we did not find it", but "no route exists at all", for Königsberg and for every city whose ground plan has more than two odd landmasses. The necessity is the pairing theorem; the violation is one degree count; the family is every multigraph ever drawn — and the wall reads backwards: add one bridge joining any two of the odd landmasses and exactly two odds remain, the family reopens, and a walk must exist — an honest enumeration finds one for every such addition. The obstruction was not the end of the story; it was the city's renovation manual.

Moves used: P12 (the pairing theorem as necessity, the degree count as violation, all multigraphs as the family), P09 (the enter-leave pairing is a conserved-quantity argument in disguise), P02 (the by-hand tries that showed the detector's honest limits), P10 (the degree count as a census over landmasses).

Example 3 (the sliding puzzle) — the swap that cannot be slid into place

The question, from the 1880 craze: the fifteen puzzle — a \(4 \times 4\) tray holding fifteen numbered tiles and one empty slot — starts with its tiles in order, and 1880 America went mad for it. Years later Sam Loyd — not the puzzle's inventor, whatever he claimed — offered a thousand dollars to anyone who could reach one particular arrangement: the same order, except tiles \(14\) and \(15\) exchanged, with the empty slot back in its corner. The prize was never claimed, and this example is the reason why — the wall that blocked it, named and checkable.

Small case first, P03's discipline — because the full puzzle is beyond any hunt. A \(2 \times 2\) tray holds three tiles and a blank; there are \(4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24\) arrangements. An honest enumeration of all of them (a small machine, a spare afternoon) splits the \(24\) cleanly in half: exactly \(12\) arrangements are reachable from the solved tray — and the unreachable \(12\) are precisely their mirror images with two tiles exchanged. Reach the same split with a parity count: every slide is a single swap — the blank trades places with one adjacent tile — so the parity of the four-token arrangement flips on every slide; and the blank, moving one square, flips the parity of its displacement from home just as often. The two flips cancel: the combined parity — arrangement parity plus blank-displacement parity — is the wall, never breached by any legal slide. The solved tray stamps it even; the two-tiles-swapped, blank-at-home arrangement stamps it odd. Unreachable — and the exhaustive \(12/24\) split confirms the census in miniature.

Now the full tray, where the hunt is not merely hard but impossible: \(16!\) arrangements is \(20{,}922{,}789{,}888{,}000\) — twenty trillion, at a million arrangements per second, roughly eight months without sleeping, and you would only be searching, not proving. The census does not grow with the tray. Every slide still swaps the blank with one tile; the parity pair still flips in unison; the combined stamp is still invariant. The solved tray stamps even; Loyd's target — \(14\) and \(15\) exchanged, blank home — stamps odd. No slide sequence exists, for the \(4 \times 4\), the \(5 \times 5\), or any size of tray, and the thousand dollars were safe from the moment the first applicant computed a parity. Read the wall backwards and it becomes a puzzle-designer's manual: the reachable arrangements (blank at home) are exactly the even permutations of the tiles — so swap two pairs, or cycle three tiles, and the challenge becomes solvable; exchange two single tiles and it never will be. The obstruction does not just block the prize — it draws the exact boundary between the puzzles that can be sold and the ones that cannot.

Moves used: P12 (the parity pair named as the obstruction, its boundary read as the puzzle-designer's manual), P09 (the combined parity is a conserved quantity — this page's parent move), P02 (the \(2 \times 2\) census that made the wall visible small before trusting it large), P03 (the boundary checks: which swaps are reachable and which are sealed).

5 · When the move fails

P03's discipline applies to the move itself: the wall jams, in five named shapes, and naming the shape is the diagnosis. The first three corrupt the wall itself. The fourth wastes it. The last overbills it. Every exhibit below was run, not asserted.

The phantom wall. A student stares at the sums of two squares — \(5, 13, 17, 29, 37, \ldots\), all \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — and announces the obstruction: "a sum of two squares must be \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\), and \(2027\) fails the test, so it is sealed." The conclusion happens to be right and the wall is a phantom: \(2 = 1 + 1\) is \(\equiv 2\), \(8 = 4 + 4\) is \(\equiv 0\), \(10 = 1 + 9\) is \(\equiv 2\), \(20 = 4 + 16\) is \(\equiv 0\) — legal candidates, walking around the wall in broad daylight. The student sampled a subfamily and billed the sample as necessity; §2's true census (\(0, 1, 2\) reachable, only \(3\) sealed) is a theorem over all squares, and the phantom was one honest enumeration away from dying. Mitigation: prove the necessity over every candidate the rules admit — the census is the proof, the sample is the trap. Price: a phantom wall reads exactly like a real one until the first legal candidate steps around it; \(2027\)'s verdict was saved by luck, not by the argument. Default: no proven necessity, no seal — the same demand as P09's, inherited honestly.

The one-way filter, billed as two-way. The mod-\(4\) census seals every \(n \equiv 3 \pmod 4\). A reader in a hurry reads the flip side into it: "so every \(n \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is a sum of two squares" — and \(21 = 4 \cdot 5 + 1\) quietly refuses (the exhaustive hunt of §2). The same shape on the board: a \(2 \times 2\) board loses two diagonal cells; two cells remain, one of each colour, the colour census balanced perfectly — and no domino covers them, because they touch only at a corner. Balanced census, even count, honest silence — silence is all a passed check ever returns. Mitigation: state the wall's direction out loud: violation seals; a pass is permission to hunt, never a promise of success. Price: the pass feels most like a promise exactly where the family is subtle — the two-squares question's full criterion lives up the ladder, at M02. Default: after a pass, run the hunt; do not bill the gate as a verdict.

The blunt census. A wall is only as sharp as its census. Take the two-squares question to the classes of \(3\): squares live in \(\{0, 1\} \pmod 3\), so sums live in \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) — every class reachable, nothing sealed, no matter how carefully the table is drawn. Worse, a census that is not even conserved: in P09's chameleon meadow, the parity of \(R - G\) tempts the amateur — but a meeting moves \(R - G\) by \(0\) or \(\pm 3\), and \(\pm 3\) flips parity, so the quantity is not preserved at all and can seal nothing. The sharp census for the meadow is the class of \(R - G\) modulo \(3\), and the sharp census for the squares is modulo \(4\) — each found, not guessed. Mitigation: before trusting a wall, verify it survives every legal move — P09's demand — and compute the full reachable-class table before claiming a gap. Price: a blunt census costs the same effort as a sharp one and seals nothing; a non-conserved census seals worse than nothing, because it feels strongest precisely when it is empty. Default: all classes reachable, or conservation failing — no wall here; go find the right census.

The un-promoted proof. The quietest failure: nothing is wrong. A page-perfect proof seals \(2027\) — "not a sum of two squares, contradiction, done" — and stops; next year the same mathematician grinds the same hunt for \(2031\), then \(2035\), each sealed by a fresh argument whose working part was, all three times, one remainder computation. The wall was there, load-bearing, un-named: the proof used it and threw it away, and the family's thousand instant verdicts went unclaimed. Mitigation: after any successful seal, interrogate the argument for its checkable working part and state the family — the one-sentence promotion of §3 is the entire move. Price: none, really — one extra sentence. That is the point: this failure shape is pure waste, and it is the most common of the five. Default: seal, then promote; if the working part resists a family statement, say so and keep the proof — the named wall is a bonus, never a requirement.

The dissolved wall, left unchecked. The compass misread as a guarantee. The wall's boundary says where the sealed family ends — it does not say the survivors succeed. \(2021 = 43 \times 47\) passes the mod-\(4\) gate (\(2021 = 4 \cdot 505 + 1\)), both prime factors \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and the honest hunt still finds no representation — the obstruction has not dissolved, it has moved house, to the odd primes. And the renovated Königsberg — one bridge added between two odd landmasses — passes its degree count, after which a crossing walk must exist and still wants exhibiting: the count proves the family is open, not that any particular route is short. A modified problem is a new problem, with its own hunts and its own proofs. Mitigation: after crossing a wall's boundary, run the fresh hunt honestly — the census bought you the right to search, not a result. Price: the renewed problem may hide a subtler obstruction (\(2021\)'s odd-prime wall) or none at all (\(2029 = 45^2 + 2^2\)); only the hunt tells them apart. Default: boundary crossed — new hunt, new proof, zero confidence carried over.

Scope, honestly. This move is the spine's negative-instrument specialist: it rules out, seals families, and points at boundaries — it never constructs, never certifies a survivor, and never measures how much something fails. Up the ladder, obstruction becomes quantitative: local answers that fail to glue get a number that measures the failure (P33, the tier-5 gateway this page is a named prerequisite of), and deformations get a dictionary where one group counts the moves and another counts the walls. Both need machinery this part of the ladder has not built. The Atlas says so here, once, and this page stays honest about what it owns: the named wall, the sealed family, the pointed boundary.

6 · What to try next

The wall is named — or the move jammed, and the jam itself is a route. Each ending has a door.

P13 — infinite descent. A named number-wall is often the front half of a full family theorem, and descent is the engine that finishes it: Fermat's two-squares theorem — the complete story of which numbers are sums of two squares, §2's deferral — is a descent proof wearing this page's census as its first move.

M02 — sums of two squares, all moves at once. The question this page kept meeting (\(2027\) sealed, \(2029\) landing, \(21\) and \(2021\) refusing from inside the passable classes) has a complete answer: a number is a sum of two squares exactly when every prime \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\) appears to an even power. The mod-\(4\) census is that theorem's visible tip; M02 climbs the rest.

P72 — monovariants. The wall's process-cousin: a quantity that only moves one way, which seals not a static target but a whole family of journeys — no route can end where the quantity forbids. If your obstruction refuses to sit still, it may be one of these.

P75 — coloring arguments. The colour census grown into its own move: paint the board, count the colours, and the mismatch is the proof — the mutilated chessboard's family, formalized.

Deferred honestly, with reasons. The deepest relative of this move measures obstructions instead of merely naming them: the sheaf cohomology bridge (P33) counts how local answers fail to glue into global ones — but it needs the universal-property argument (P31), telescoping (P69), and two bridge pages on graphs and potentials (B09–B10) first, none of which exist yet. And deformation cohomology (P53, tier 5) refines obstruction into a dictionary — one group counting the moves you can make, another counting the walls you cannot pass. Both are this page's descendants; neither is reachable yet, and pretending otherwise would violate the ladder's one law.

7 · Lineage

All three exhibits have a paper trail. Königsberg: Euler presented his solution to the St. Petersburg Academy on 26 August 1735, and it appeared as Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis — "the solution of a problem relating to the geometry of position" — in the Academy's journal, in a volume dated 1736. It is usually called the first theorem of graph theory, and its engine is this page's: the degree census \(5, 3, 3, 3\) — four odd landmasses where a walk can afford two. Euler stated that zero or two odd vertices is also sufficient for the walk; the careful proof of that direction came later, from Carl Hierholzer. The two squares: Fermat announced the criterion — a prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it leaves remainder \(1\) on division by \(4\) — in a letter to Marin Mersenne dated Christmas Day 1640, characteristically as an announcement without proof (Albert Girard had published the characterisation for all numbers, not just primes, fifteen years earlier); Euler supplied the proof, and the engine he built for it is the next page's own — infinite descent. The fifteen puzzle: a precursor is said to have been shown around 1874 by the postmaster Noyes Palmer Chapman, the craze swept the United States in 1880 — and in 1879, before it peaked, Johnson and Story proved by a parity invariant that half of all positions are unreachable: Example 3's census, found for a toy and stated as a theorem. Sam Loyd, who had nothing to do with the invention, claimed the puzzle from 1891 on and offered a 1,000-dollar prize for the 14–15 swap — a prize he could advertise in perfect safety, because the impossibility was already a published theorem more than a decade before his offer. (The bridge-by-bridge list in Example 2 is the standard reconstruction from Euler's paper; the degree census is Euler's own, and it is what the proof actually uses.) None of this history is load-bearing here — but every time a proof answers a whole family of questions by naming a wall, it is running the twelfth move of the spine, exactly as Euler ran it on the Pregel.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — seal or search

Four years are on trial: \(2027\), \(2029\), \(2033\), \(2039\). For each, decide whether it is a sum of two squares — with a rule of evidence: if a wall seals the year, name the wall and do not search at all; only a year no wall covers earns a hunt. Do them in the order the rule dictates.

Hint

Compute each year's remainder on division by \(4\) first. A remainder of \(3\) is a proof; a remainder of \(1\) is only a hunting licence — and §5 taught you that some licences lead nowhere.

Worked answer

\(2027 = 4 \cdot 506 + 3\): sealed by §2's census — no square leaves remainder \(3\), so no sum of two squares can. No search. \(2039 = 4 \cdot 509 + 3\): sealed the same way, in the same instant, by the same wall. \(2029 = 4 \cdot 507 + 1\): no wall at this modulus — hunt, and the hunt lands: \(2029 = 45^2 + 2^2 = 2025 + 4\). \(2033 = 4 \cdot 508 + 1\): no wall at this modulus — hunt, and the hunt fails at every \(a\) from \(0\) to \(45\), and the factorization says why: \(2033 = 19 \times 107\), both factors leaving remainder \(3\). §5's one-way wall, met again: passing the gate is permission to search, never a promise of success, and the deeper wall that seals \(2033\) — the class of every prime factor, counted with multiplicity — is named and proven on M02, one bridge past this page's charter. Two years answered with a single division each, one with a five-minute hunt, one refused after a full search: that spread is the move's whole value.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the eighth bridge

Königsberg has four odd landmasses; a walk using every bridge exactly once can serve at most two. The city builds an eighth bridge. (a) Prove that the walk stays impossible unless the new bridge joins two odd landmasses. (b) Exhibit the walk for the new bridge joining \(B\) to \(D\): list the eight crossings in order. (c) Prove the bookkeeping fact the whole argument leans on: the number of odd landmasses can never be odd.

Hint

For (a): adding a bridge changes the degree of exactly its two endpoints, so how can the count of odds move? For (b): after the \(B\)–\(D\) bridge, the degrees are \(A: 5\), \(B: 4\), \(C: 3\), \(D: 4\) — start where the odds say to. For (c): count bridge-ends.

Worked answer

(a) Every landmass the walk passes through is entered and left in pairs, so only the walk's two endpoints may have odd degree — §4's pairing theorem. A new bridge adds one to the degree of each of its two endpoints, and to nobody else: the parities of exactly two landmasses flip. If both endpoints were even, four odds become six — still more than two, and the walk stays impossible. If one was odd and one even, the count of odds does not move at all — four before, four after. Only two odd endpoints drop the count to exactly two, \(A\) and one other: the sole placement that can possibly work, exactly as §4 read the wall backwards. (b) After the bridge \(B\)–\(D\), the odds are \(A\) (degree \(5\)) and \(C\) (degree \(3\)), so the walk must run from \(A\) to \(C\): A to B, B to A, A to C, C to A, A to D, D to B, B to D over the new bridge, D to C — eight crossings, nine stops, every bridge used exactly once. Audit: consecutive crossings share a landmass; each of the seven old bridges and the new one appears exactly once; the walk starts where the census commands and ends where it commands. For the other five odd-odd placements the machine confirms a walk for each — §4 promised exactly that reading. (c) Each bridge has two ends, so the sum of all degrees is twice the number of bridges — even. Summing degrees pairs off the even ones and leaves the odd ones to cancel each other: the count of odd landmasses is even in every city, on every river, forever. A city cannot have exactly one odd landmass — which is why §4's "four odds" was a diagnosis with a remedy, not a mystery: the handshake count forbids three, the walk census forbids four, and one well-placed bridge cures it.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the class table, weaponized

§4 named the class-multiplication census — B02's four odd pairs — as a wall. Turn it into four working tools. (a) Prove that a product of numbers each leaving remainder \(1\) on division by \(4\) itself leaves remainder \(1\). (b) Prove that a product leaving remainder \(3\) must contain an odd number of factors leaving remainder \(3\). (c) Re-run P11's Exercise 3 — there are infinitely many primes of the form \(4k+3\) — naming the wall at each step: which line is necessity, which is the violation, and what family does the proof seal? (d) Explain Example 1's jam: with the pretend-list of \(4k+1\) primes \(\{5\}\), the machine's \(M = 4 \cdot 5 + 1 = 21 = 3 \times 7\) produced no \(4k+1\) factor at all. Which entry of the four-pair table is the machine's exact point of failure?

Hint

For (a), peel the product one factor at a time and consult the table after each peel. For (b), watch what a factor \(\equiv 3\) does to the running class: flip or hold? For (d), ask what the \(4k+3\) machine used that the \(4k+1\) translation needs.

Worked answer

(a) The table: \(1 \cdot 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). Peel: if a running product of \(1\)-class factors is \(\equiv 1\), multiplying by another \(1\)-class factor keeps it there — induction over the number of factors, with the four-pair table as base case and engine. (b) The four pairs: \(1 \cdot 1 \equiv 1\), \(1 \cdot 3 \equiv 3\), \(3 \cdot 1 \equiv 3\), \(3 \cdot 3 \equiv 1\). A \(1\)-class factor holds the running class; a \(3\)-class factor flips \(1 \leftrightarrow 3\). Starting from class \(1\) (the empty product), the final class is \(3\) exactly when the flips number odd — an odd count of \(3\)-class factors. Necessity by the table, for every product, not a sample; the machine confirms it on every odd number to \(200\). (c) P11's machine: suppose \(p_1, \ldots, p_k\) were all the \(4k+3\) primes; set \(N = 4(p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4\). Necessity: by (b), a product in class \(3\) must carry an odd count of class-\(3\) factors, so \(N\) has at least one prime factor \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\), whatever its other factors are and however long the pretend-list. Violation: no \(p_i\) divides \(N\) — each leaves remainder \(-1\), not \(0\). Contradiction: a class-\(3\) prime off the complete list. Family: every number \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\) ever encountered certifies, by the wall, an odd count of class-\(3\) prime factors — and the infinitude falls out: each such number demands a fresh witness. (d) The \(4k+3\) machine consumed (b): class \(3\) forces a class-\(3\) factor. The translation needs the mirrored statement — class \(1\) forces a class-\(1\) factor — and the table's entry \(3 \cdot 3 \equiv 1\) denies it: class \(1\) is reachable with zero class-\(1\) factors. At \(M = 21\): \(21 = 3 \times 7\), both factors class \(3\), landing in class \(1\) by exactly that entry — the machine's single point of failure, visible in a two-by-two table.

Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

The claim on trial: "every number that leaves remainder \(1\) on division by \(4\) is a sum of two squares." Three students hand in verdicts. Student A: "\(5 = 1 + 4\), \(13 = 4 + 9\), \(17 = 1 + 16\), \(29 = 4 + 25\), \(37 = 1 + 36\) — five for five, every one lands. Proven." Student B: "\(21 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), and \(21\) refuses: subtract \(0, 1, 4, 9, 16\) and no square is ever left. One counterexample: refuted." Student C: "The census seals the class \(3\) — that direction is a theorem. For the class \(1\) it is silent: it only licenses a search. Whether a class-\(1\) number lands is decided, number by number, by a different wall — every prime \(\equiv 3\) must appear to an even power — which is M02's theorem, and this page's census is its first shadow." Referee: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

For A: what does the onramp say a sample can never be? For B: what is the asymmetry between confirming and refuting an "every" claim? For C: is citing a later page's theorem as the full story more or less honest than pretending this page's wall settles it?

Worked answer

A: rejected — a sample is not necessity. The five landings are real — the receipts check: \(1 + 4 = 5\), \(4 + 9 = 13\), \(1 + 16 = 17\), \(4 + 25 = 29\), \(1 + 36 = 37\) — and they prove nothing about the family. Necessity is a theorem, never a sample, and the wall-naming discipline exists precisely for A's error: five successes out of an infinite family, with the family's refuser \(21\) sitting untested between \(17\) and \(29\). The same method "proves" every false claim that fails late. B: upheld — refutation is asymmetric. A "every \(n\)" claim dies by one honest counterexample, and B's is exhaustive: \(21 - 0 = 21\), \(-1 = 20\), \(-4 = 17\), \(-9 = 12\), \(-16 = 5\) — not one a square, and \(a = 5\) already overshoots since \(5^2 = 25 > 21\). The hunt is small, complete, and honest — a detector's negative result, reported without rounding up. C: upheld — the scope discipline is the verdict. C refuses to let the class-\(3\) wall pose as a class-\(1\) promise — §5's one-way filter — and refuses equally to pretend this page is the end of the story: the full criterion is deferred, by name, to the page that proves it. That double refusal — no overclaim, no underclaim — is what P12 adds to P11: not just finding the wall, but drawing its exact boundary on the ladder.

Exercise 5 ◐ — sharpen the census

The mod-\(4\) census seals the class \(3\) — but \(21\) passes that gate and still refuses, so the sieve cannot end there. Sharpen it. Compute the census of squares, then of sums of two squares, modulo \(8\); then modulo \(16\); then modulo \(32\). At each level, list the sealed classes and find which are new — sealed at this level while passing every coarser one. What does every new seal look like? Then find the smallest number that passes every power-of-two gate and still refuses, and read its factorization for what it says about where 2-sifting must end.

Hint

Squares modulo \(8\): compute \(n^2\) for \(n = 0, 1, \ldots, 7\) and keep the distinct classes; sums are all pairwise additions of square classes. Two facts carry the pattern: a number divisible by \(4\) that is a sum of two squares has both squares even — squares leave \(0\) or \(1\) on division by \(4\), and a sum leaving \(0\) forces both to leave \(0\); and an odd square always leaves \(1\) on division by \(8\).

Worked answer

Level \(8\): squares are \(\{0, 1, 4\}\); sums are \(\{0, 1, 2, 4, 5\}\); sealed: \(\{3, 6, 7\}\) — the lifts of the class \(3\) plus one new face, \(6 = 3 \cdot 2\). Level \(16\): squares \(\{0, 1, 4, 9\}\); sums \(\{0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 13\}\); sealed \(\{3, 6, 7, 11, 12, 14, 15\}\) — and \(12 = 3 \cdot 4\) is genuinely new: it passed level \(8\), since \(12 \equiv 4\), and \(4 = 4 + 0\). Level \(32\): squares \(\{0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 17, 25\}\); seventeen sum classes, fifteen sealed — of which \(24 = 3 \cdot 8\) is the new one, passing level \(16\) as \(8 = 4 + 4\). The pattern, three levels deep: each doubling of the modulus adds exactly one new sealed class, \(3 \cdot 2^{k-2}\) at modulus \(2^k\) — and both mechanisms are already on the table. A number divisible by \(4\) that is a sum of two squares has both squares even, so dividing by \(4\) preserves representations: old seals propagate upward through factors of \(4\), which is why the class \(3\) never stops sealing. And odd squares are \(1 \pmod 8\), which is why \(3 \cdot 2\) first seals at level \(8\). The refuser: \(21\) passes every gate — \(21 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(\equiv 5 \pmod 8\), \(\equiv 5 \pmod{16}\), \(\equiv 21 \pmod{32}\), verified by the machine through modulus \(128\) — and \(21 = 3 \times 7\) refuses all the same. Strip the powers of \(4\) from a refuser standing past every 2-gate and what remains is an odd number \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) carrying a class-\(3\) prime to an odd power: invisible to every 2-sieve, forever. The 2-censuses sharpen the detector but cannot become the wall — an infinite ladder of gates, all passed, is still not a landing — and the theorem the sifting ends at is a statement about prime factorizations: M02's, deferred by name. The wall's boundary, drawn one last time.
