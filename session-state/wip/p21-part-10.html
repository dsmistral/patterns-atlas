6 · What next

The doors this page opens

The nearest door: P22 · Iterate to a fixed point. The Babylonian iteration for \(\sqrt{2}\) — average your guess with the number divided by your guess, forever — is an endless record: hit after hit, each from the last. The whole apparatus of this page applies, and one extra clause changes everything: the map is a contraction, each hit pulled closer to the last by a fixed factor, so the room is not merely bounded but shrinking around its own catch. Where this page's record could crowd in many places, a contraction's crowd is a single point — the uniqueness this page never promised, arriving as a hypothesis. And where this page's machine finds the crowd one bit per level, the contraction's crowd is found digit per iteration, with a price tag on the error. P22 is this page's engine, turbocharged by an extra assumption.

The middle door: P41 · The mountain pass. Between your valley and the taller peak beyond lies a ridge, and somewhere on it a lowest crossing — the pass. Its existence is an extremal claim in a room with two dimensions, and the room above the line is where the machine generalizes: halve rectangles instead of intervals (Exercise 3 rehearses exactly this), keep the quadrant that stays hit, and close the trap on a point that must exist. The pass is that catch wearing a map: the minimax point that neither valley claims. The price carries over honestly — the room must be closed, bounded, and now the record needs more than crowding; it needs the terrain to be continuous, or the pass can fail to exist just as Exhibit C's maximizer did.

The far door: P54 · Compactify by stable degenerations. Exhibit A taught the repair — close the room, admit the boundary — as an emergency measure. P54 practises it deliberately: a family of objects that refuses to have a limit point is completed on purpose, the boundary points are added with ceremony, and what was a bug in a record becomes a compact room in which every endless family must crowd. The word compact, met here as the grown-up name for closed-and-bounded, becomes a design tool: build the room so that the machine is guaranteed to close. This page is P54's foundation layer, and Exhibit A is its motivation, preserved.

The default habit

When a claim says exists about anything endless or trapped, reach for this move first. Name the room, and check its clauses before touching the machine: bounded? closed? continuous too, if what you want is a maximizer? Then halve, keep the endless half, repeat, and state what the catch is — a crowd, a maximum, a root. Two questions must follow, every time, because the theorem answers neither: is the catch unique, and is the catch one of the hits? If the room fails a clause, repair first — bound the record, close the room — and price the repair out loud: the repaired claim is about the repaired room, not the original one. The discipline is the whole difference between a trap that closes and a trap that closes on nothing.

7 · Where it came from

The halving is the oldest move on this page — P15 walked past Archimedes using it — but the guarantee is young by mathematics' clock, and its story is one of the honest ones. Bolzano, 1817. The Prague priest-mathematician Bernard Bolzano, proving that a sign change forces a root, needed a lemma first: a bounded endless record must crowd. He built the nested-interval machine in essentially the form of §3, and his "purely analytic proof" was meant to purge the calculus of appeals to motion and continuity-as-picture. The honest gap, in plain sight of the modern eye: Bolzano assumed the no-holes property of the line — the completeness whose costume Box P21.A wears — without proving it, and by the strictest standard the proof did not fully succeed. The bill took fifty years to arrive, and when it did, it arrived three times in one year.

Weierstrass, the Berlin lectures. Karl Weierstrass rebuilt analysis on exact foundations half a century later, used the crowd-catching lemma as a load-bearing beam — the extreme value theorem of §4's second example is his — and for decades the result bore only his name. Priority was eventually sorted back to Bolzano, and the theorem now carries both: Bolzano–Weierstrass, the way a house carries its builder and its restorer.

1872, the debt paid three times. Richard Dedekind published his cuts — the no-holes property built out of nothing, the line completed by construction. Georg Cantor, the same year, named the concept this page centers on: the Grenzpunkt, the limit point, and studied the set of them a record carries — the derived set — turning §4's "audit the whole crowd" into a branch of mathematics. Eduard Heine, also 1872, published the covering theorem — every open cover of a closed bounded room has a finite subcover — crediting the Weierstrass lectures. Three papers, one door: the assumption Bolzano spent silently became an axiom, a construction, and a theorem.

The plastic constant, 1928. And the number §4's third example trapped — \(\rho \approx 1.3247179572\), the real root of \(x^3 = x + 1\) — earned its name from a monk. Hans van der Laan, a Dutch Benedictine architect, took monastic vows in his twenties and in 1928 published this ratio as the basis of his proportional system for buildings: the plastic number, named for the plastic arts — sculpture and architecture — with the property that a room's dimensions shrink along it the way the ear hears proportion. (The sequence of ratios converging to it is sometimes named for Richard Padovan, who wrote the architecture's later history; the number's own story is the monk's.) The number had existed since §4's sign change first held; the theorem that the trap under it has a floor is this page's, 1817's, and 1872's.
