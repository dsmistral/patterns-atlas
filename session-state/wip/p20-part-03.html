2 · Four integers, one square

Run the small-case machine of P02 on a pile of arithmetic. Multiply four consecutive integers and add one:

\(n\)\((n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n+2)\)plus onesquare?
3\(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 120\)\(121\)\(= 11^2\)
4\(3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 = 360\)\(361\)\(= 19^2\)
5\(4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 = 840\)\(841\)\(= 29^2\)
7\(6 \cdot 7 \cdot 8 \cdot 9 = 3024\)\(3025\)\(= 55^2\)

Four perfect squares, and the roots \(11, 19, 29, 55\) are not random: each is \(n^2 + n - 1\) — for \(n = 3\), \(9 + 3 - 1 = 11\); for \(n = 7\), \(49 + 7 - 1 = 55\). The conjecture assembles itself:

\[(n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n+2) \;+\; 1 \;=\; \left(n^2 + n - 1\right)^2 \qquad \text{for every } n.\]

A conjecture is not a proof, and "for every \(n\)" is exactly the demand that kills testing: no run of lucky cases ever reaches all integers. Two classical doors lead out. The clever one is P05's: spot that \((n-1)n(n+1)(n+2) = (n^2+n)(n^2+n-2)\), substitute \(m = n^2+n\), and read \(m(m-2)+1 = (m-1)^2\) — one line, one idea, done. It is beautiful, and it is ungeneralizable: the idea lives in this identity and nowhere else. The second door looks dumber and is the page. Call the left side minus the right side \(D(n)\) — the difference — and observe what shape it has before knowing anything else: a product of linear factors against a square, so \(D\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(4\), however it expands. Now the school census: if \(D\) were nonzero, it could vanish at at most \(4\) points — each root donates a factor \((n - a)\), and four of them exhaust the degree budget. So evaluate. Five values, chosen small:

\(n\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)
left side\(1\)\(1\)\(1\)\(25\)\(121\)
right side\(1\)\(1\)\(1\)\(25\)\(121\)

Five agreements. If \(D\) were nonzero it could afford four vanishings, and it has been caught at five — one over budget, contradiction, P11 in miniature. So \(D\) is the zero polynomial: every coefficient zero, the two sides the same object, the identity proved for every \(n\) at once — including the integers nobody will ever test. Count what each proof paid. P05's route paid one idea. This route paid zero ideas and five subtractions: it never looked at the structure, and it did not need to. That trade — insight for census — is the entire pattern, and it scales precisely where insight dries up.
