4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — the crossing that will not straighten linearly. The curved world this time is the node \\(xy = 0\\): two honest branches — the coordinate axes — meeting at the origin. First question, the invariant question: is the crossing a fact about the world or an artifact of coordinates? Linear charts are the cheapest to try, so the lab tries them all: every \\(2 \\times 2\\) integer matrix with entries in \\(\\{-1, 0, 1\\}\\) and determinant \\(\\pm 1\\) — \\(40\\) of them — and in all \\(40\\) the two branches map to two distinct lines through the origin. No invertible linear chart un-crosses a crossing; the tangent cone is an invariant of linear changes, and the crossing is a fact.

The blow-up is the chart that succeeds, and it is honest about needing two. Chart \\(U_1\\) remembers the horizontal coordinate and the slope: \\((x, y) = (u, uv)\\) — the dictionary says: keep \\(x\\), and keep \\(y/x\\) instead of \\(y\\). Chart \\(U_2\\) is the mirror: \\((x, y) = (st, s)\\), keeping \\(y\\) and \\(x/y\\). The chart’s own edge is visible immediately: the vertical branch \\(x = 0\\) has no slope to remember, and lives entirely in \\(U_2\\) — the same locality honesty as the circle’s anchor, now on the other branch. The arithmetic, machine-checked over a \\(31\\)-point rational grid in both charts (\\(1{,}922\\) chart identities, all exact): the total transform of \\(xy\\) in \\(U_1\\) is \\(u^2 v\\) — the exceptional line \\(u = 0\\) appearing with multiplicity two — and the proper transforms are the honest branches: the branch \\(y = 0\\) becomes \\(v = 0\\) in \\(U_1\\), meeting the exceptional line at slope \\(0\\); the branch \\(x = 0\\) becomes \\(t = 0\\) in \\(U_2\\), meeting it at slope \\(\\infty\\). The census that settles the encounter: \\(20\\) points of the first branch, all slope \\(0\\); \\(20\\) points of the second, all slope \\(\\infty\\); and the two meeting points \\([1:0]\\) and \\([0:1]\\) on the exceptional line are distinct. The crossing is gone: what crossed at one point now meets the exceptional line at two different points, each branch clean, each transverse. The multiplicity receipt is the conservation law of the move: the node’s multiplicity at the origin is \\(2\\) (the initial form \\(xy\\) is degree \\(2\\)), and after the blow-up the separated branches meet the exceptional line with intersection multiplicities \\(1 + 1\\) — the multiplicity was conserved and spread across the two separated points, machine-checked by the quotient-ring census (\\(k[[u,v]]/(u,v)\\) is one-dimensional, per branch).

Two contrast receipts close the encounter. The smooth curve \\(xy = 1\\) never passes through the origin, so the blow-up should change nothing for it — and does: through chart \\(U_1\\) the round trip is the identity on all \\(30\\) window points, and the curve never meets the exceptional line (\\(u = 0\\) forces \\(u^2 v = 1\\) impossible, all \\(11\\) probe points): straightening touches nothing where nothing is wrong. And the deepest receipt, the one that makes the blow-up worth its price: the node’s bookkeeping gap, measured only after the branches separate. The local records of the node are \\(R = \\mathbb{F}_p[x,y]/(xy)\\), truncated; its normalization — the records of the two branches apart — is \\(\\bar{R} = \\mathbb{F}_p[x] \\oplus \\mathbb{F}_p[y]\\), and the machine’s rank census over \\(\\mathbb{F}_5\\) and \\(\\mathbb{F}_7\\) both say the same thing: the normalization is exactly one dimension richer (\\(6 - 5\\)). That gap is the \\(\\delta\\)-invariant, \\(\\delta = 1\\): the price of the crossing, invisible in the plane, computed in the chart. The smooth curve \\(y - x^2 = 0\\) runs the same census and loses nothing: \\(\\delta = 0\\). The invariant was there all along; the straightening is what made it computable.

Encounter 2 — the form that keeps its signature. The algebra world’s curved objects are quadratic forms, and the straightening is the congruence: \\(A \\mapsto P^{T} A P\\) with \\(P\\) invertible — change the basis, keep the form. The lab runs the census over all \\(27\\) symmetric \\(2 \\times 2\\) forms with entries in \\(\\{-1, 0, 1\\}\\), and the signature — how many plus signs, how many minus signs in the straightened diagonal — is computed two independent ways: by congruence elimination (three honest cases: pivot on \\(a\\), pivot on \\(c\\), or the hyperbolic trick \\([0,b;b,0] \\mapsto [2b,0;0,-2b]\\) when both diagonal entries vanish), and by the eigenvalue census (trace and determinant sign logic, real eigenvalues forced by \\((a-c)^2 + 4b^2 \\geq 0\\)). The two routes agree on all \\(27\\). Then the round trips: seven unimodular congruence paths per form — \\(189\\) re-straightenings — and the signature survives every one, \\(189\\) of \\(189\\), with the transport receipt \\(\\det(P^{T} A P) = \\det(P)^2 \\det(A)\\) holding on all \\(189\\) as well. The must-fail receipt is the encounter’s teaching moment: the projection \\(E = [1,0;0,0]\\) looks like a straightening — it flattens every form to a single diagonal entry — but it is not invertible, and the collapsed “signature” disagrees with the true one on \\(20\\) of the \\(27\\) forms. The signature is an invariant of invertible transport, and the census of the survivors: \\(16\\) indefinite \\((1,1)\\), four of each one-sign class \\((1,0)\\) and \\((0,1)\\), one each of the definite classes \\((2,0)\\) and \\((0,2)\\), one zero form. And the bridge back down the ladder: completing the square is the \\(2 \\times 2\\) congruence — P04’s corner payment — and its leftover obeys \\(a \\cdot (c - b^2/a) = \\det A\\), all \\(18\\) forms with \\(a \\neq 0\\): the “leftover” of the class-12 move was the determinant all along, and the determinant’s sign is what the signature remembers.

Encounter 3 — the circle needs two charts. Return to \\(\\S2\\) and ask the global question: how much of the circle can one dictionary cover? Chart A hangs from the anchor \\((-1,0)\\) with coordinate \\(t\\); chart B hangs from \\((1,0)\\) with its own slope coordinate \\(s\\). The misses are census facts: chart A cannot see \\((-1,0)\\) (\\(t = \\infty\\)), chart B cannot see \\((1,0)\\) (\\(s = \\infty\\), which is \\(t = 0\\)). On the overlap the two dictionaries must agree about the world, and they do — by a transition law the machine checks on all \\(46\\) chart-internal slopes: \\(s = -1/t\\). The transition is projective: it is the same law that turns a line’s point at infinity into a real number, and it is the t-line’s honest confession that the two anchors’ dictionaries are one dictionary in two wardrobes. The round trip through both charts returns every point to itself, \\(46\\) of \\(46\\). And the reflection receipt ties the identity element into the census: \\(P(-t) = (x, -y)\\) — the mirror through the \\(x\\)-axis, all \\(47\\) window slopes — and \\(t \\star (-t) = 0\\): a point and its mirror multiply to the identity point \\((1,0)\\), all \\(47\\) cases, the pair-census reading of “inverse”.

The obstruction is now visible, and it deserves its name. No single chart covers the circle — not because our dictionaries were lazy, but because the circle is compact (closed and bounded, every sequence has a limit point on it) and the line is not, and a bijection cannot reconcile that: the chart preserves too much structure to carry a round line onto an open line. The invariant that measures the impossibility precisely is the winding number — how many times a closed trip around the circle turns — and it is the first obstruction this book has met that lives on the whole world, not on a patch: every patch of the circle straightens, the whole circle provably does not. The move’s honest global price is exactly this: charts are local, obstructions are global, and the boundary between them is where the curved world keeps its secrets.
