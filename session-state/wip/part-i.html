8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ · The four bracelets, by hand

(a) List the canonical forms of the binary triangles — two-colored vertex triples up to all six motions — and confirm the count 4. (b) Compute \(\langle \chi_{\mathrm{paint}}, \varepsilon \rangle\) for \(\chi_{\mathrm{paint}} = (8, 4, 2)\) by hand, and explain in one sentence why the answer means no coloring-function is genuinely antisymmetric. (c) State and defend the general law this page keeps using: why is the multiplicity of the constant wave always exactly the orbit count?

Hint

For (a): fix the number of black vertices — 0, 1, 2, 3 — and check each class is a single orbit. For (b): the inner product is a dimension — what would a function in the sign wave's share have to do under a reflection? For (c): a function on configurations is fixed by the whole group exactly when it is constant on each orbit — and how many independent such functions are there?

Worked answer

(a) \(000, 001, 011, 111\): with zero black vertices only \(000\); with one, the three placements are rotations of one another; with two, the same argument; with three, \(111\). Four orbits, matching \(\langle (8,4,2), \mathbf{1} \rangle = \frac{8+12+4}{6} = 4\). (b) \(\frac{8 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 4 \cdot (-1) + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1}{6} = \frac{8 - 12 + 4}{6} = 0\). A function in the sign wave's share satisfies \(f(g \cdot c) = \varepsilon(g) f(c)\): under a reflection it flips sign, but a reflection fixes its axis configurations — there \(f = -f\) forces \(f = 0\), and the orbit structure finishes the argument: no nonzero function behaves that way, and the count of them is zero. (c) A function on configurations is invariant under the whole group exactly when it is constant on each orbit; the orbit-constant functions form a vector space with one basis vector per orbit — indicator functions of orbits — so the dimension of the invariant subspace is the orbit count. The invariant subspace is the constant wave's full share, and the multiplicity \(\langle \chi, \mathbf{1} \rangle\) is its dimension. Every average on this page is this identity in costume.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · Three colors — the sign wave appears

Color the triangle's vertices with three colors. (a) Compute the paint character over the classes \((1, 3, 2)\) and the bracelet count by inner product; check it against a census of the \(27\) colorings. (b) Compute the full split of the 27-dimensional coloring space into the triangle's three waves, and run both integrality gates. (c) The sign wave now appears with multiplicity 1 — construct an explicit coloring-function that spans its share, and explain why two colors could not produce one.

Hint

For (a): cycles first — how many cycles does each motion have on vertices, and how many fixed colorings does each permit? For (b): three inner products, then the sum check and the sum-of-squares check. For (c): a function must assign opposite values to a configuration and its mirror; try "all three colors present" and read the vertex colors clockwise versus counterclockwise.

Worked answer

(a) The paint character is cycles-to-the-\(k\): identity \(3^3 = 27\), reflection \(3^2 = 9\), rotation \(3^1 = 3\). Bracelets: \(\frac{27 + 3 \cdot 9 + 2 \cdot 3}{6} = \frac{27+27+6}{6} = 10\), and the census of all 27 colorings confirms ten orbits. (b) \(\langle (27,9,3), \mathbf{1} \rangle = 10\); \(\langle (27,9,3), \varepsilon \rangle = \frac{27 - 27 + 6}{6} = 1\); \(\langle (27,9,3), \chi_{\mathrm{std}} \rangle = \frac{54 + 0 - 6}{6} = 8\). Sum: \(10 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 + 8 \cdot 2 = 27\) ✓; sum of squares: \(10^2 + 1^2 + 8^2 = 165 = \langle \chi, \chi \rangle = \frac{729 + 3 \cdot 81 + 2 \cdot 9}{6} = \frac{990}{6}\) ✓. (c) Define \(F\) on a coloring by: \(F = +1\) if all three colors appear and, read clockwise from some starting vertex, they appear in the cyclic order of the color labels; \(F = -1\) if they appear in the reversed order; \(F = 0\) if some color repeats. A reflection reverses clockwise to counterclockwise — \(F(g \cdot c) = -F(c)\) for flips, and rotations preserve it — \(F(g \cdot c) = F(c)\): exactly the sign wave's law, and \(F \neq 0\), so the share is nonzero, hence (dimension 1) spanned by \(F\). Two colors cannot build \(F\): with a repeated color, every assignment the construction could make collapses to \(F = 0\) — the mirror-image orders stop existing, and the sign share stays empty. The appearance and disappearance of waves as the palette grows is exactly the ledger's kind of news: no census of orbits would have volunteered it.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The cube, extended to its corners

(a) Derive the \(k\)-color face-counting formula \(\frac{k^6 + 3k^4 + 12k^3 + 8k^2}{24}\) from the five breeds, and verify it for \(k = 3\). (b) Verify the inventory polynomial's coefficient of \(x^3\) by hand — exactly-two-or-three territory is where the breeds disagree — and reconcile the coefficient 2 with a picture of the three-black cubes. (c) Now paint the cube's vertices, not its faces: compute the character of the vertex action over the five breeds, then the two-color orbit count, and check it against the face count's failure to predict it.

Hint

For (a): each breed contributes size \(\times k^{\text{cycles}}\). For (b): expand the five breed polynomials and collect \(x^3\) terms. For (c): the vertices are eight, not six — recount every breed's cycle shape on the corner set before raising anything to a power.

Worked answer

(a) Breed by breed: identity \(k^6\); six quarter-turns, 3 cycles each, \(6k^3\); three face half-turns, 4 cycles, \(3k^4\); eight vertex turns, 2 cycles, \(8k^2\); six edge half-turns, 3 cycles, \(6k^3\). Sum and divide by 24: \(\frac{k^6 + 3k^4 + 12k^3 + 8k^2}{24}\). At \(k = 3\): \(\frac{729 + 243 + 324 + 72}{24} = \frac{1368}{24} = 57\), matching the census. (b) The \(x^3\)-coefficients of the breed polynomials: identity \(\binom{6}{3} = 20\); quarter-turn \((1+x)^2(1+x^4)\) has none; face half-turn \((1+x)^2(1+x^2)^2\) has \(4\); vertex turn \((1+x^3)^2\) has \(2\); edge half-turn \((1+x^2)^3\) has none. Weighted: \(\frac{20 + 3 \cdot 4 + 8 \cdot 2}{24} = \frac{48}{24} = 2\). The two three-black cubes: the belt (three faces meeting one edge in a band around the cube) and the corner (three faces sharing a vertex) — adjacent-to-adjacent versus all-at-one-point, and no rotation turns one into the other. The coefficient 2 is that story as arithmetic. (c) Vertex cycles per breed: identity \(1^8\) (8 cycles); quarter-turn \(4{+}4\) (2 cycles — the two face-axis 4-cycles of corners); face half-turn \(2{+}2{+}2{+}2\) (4); vertex turn \(3{+}3{+}1{+}1\) (4 — the axis corners stand still); edge half-turn \(2{+}2{+}2{+}2\) (4). So the paint character is \((256, 4, 16, 16, 16)\) and the count is \(\frac{256 + 6 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 16 + 8 \cdot 16 + 6 \cdot 16}{24} = \frac{256 + 24 + 48 + 128 + 96}{24} = \frac{552}{24} = 23\). The face count for two colors was 10; the vertex count is 23, and no formula relating them existed in the face ledger — the character had to be recomputed on the new set, exactly Key 1's discipline: the group is the same 24 rotations, but the action is new, and the action is what the table tabulates.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The diagnosis — same group, different count

A colleague paints the eight faces of an octahedron two colors and announces: "The rotation group is the cube's — twenty-four elements, the same five breeds — so the answer is 10, like the cube." You own this page's move. Diagnose the claim: what went wrong, what is the right answer, and what does the slip teach that no example on this page quite does?

Hint

Reach for the character before accepting any count. The octahedron is the cube's dual: its faces correspond to the cube's vertices. Which set is the group acting on, and what are the cycle shapes on that set?

Worked answer

The claim confuses the group with the action. The rotation group is the same abstract group — twenty-four elements, five breeds — but a count is a property of the action, and the octahedron's eight faces are not the cube's six faces: duality matches them to the cube's eight vertices. So the character must be recomputed on that set, and it is Exercise 3(c)'s: cycles \(8, 2, 4, 4, 4\) per breed, paint character \((256, 4, 16, 16, 16)\), count 23 — not 10. The right answer is 23, and the census of the octahedron's own faces confirms it. The lesson: "same group" licenses nothing; the page's Key 1 demands the group and the set and the action, and the pattern's most seductive failure mode is exactly this one — importing a familiar count because the symmetry feels the same. The character table of the abstract group is reusable across actions only after the character of the action has been built; the cube at 10 and the octahedron at 23 are the same group disagreeing with itself, and the diagnosis took one paragraph of counting instead of any algebra at all.

Exercise 5 ◐ · Necklaces and their mirrors

Necklaces ignore mirror images; bracelets count them together. Explore where the two answers diverge. (a) For \(n = 6\), find a binary necklace that is not its own mirror, and name its partner. (b) Count the achiral necklaces — self-mirroring ones — for \(n = 4, 6, 8\), using the identity \(2 \cdot \mathrm{bracelets} = \mathrm{necklaces} + \mathrm{achiral}\), and check it against the sequences \(6, 14, 36\) and \(6, 13, 30\). (c) What happens to the wave ledger when reflections join the group — which waves survive, which end, and why is this the exact point where the page's method starts earning its keep over naive frequency-counting?

Hint

For (a): write the necklace as a length-6 string and compare its rotations against its reversal. For (b): chiral necklaces merge in pairs into bracelets; achiral ones merge with themselves. For (c): Example 3 said it — the group stops being abelian — so ask what one-dimensional waves an abelian group owns that a non-abelian one cannot.

Worked answer

(a) Take \(001011\). Its rotations are \(001011, 010110, 101100, 011001, 110010, 100101\); its mirror (reversal) is \(110100\), which is none of these — the necklace is chiral, and its bracelet-partner is the mirror class \(\{110100\) and its rotations\(\}\). (b) The identity: each chiral necklace pairs with its mirror to give one bracelet, while an achiral necklace is already its own mirror, so \(B = A + (N - A)/2\), i.e. \(2B = N + A\), giving \(A = 2B - N\). For \(n = 4\): \(2 \cdot 6 - 6 = 6\) — every binary necklace of length 4 is self-mirroring. For \(n = 6\): \(2 \cdot 13 - 14 = 12\) of the 14 are achiral; the pair of (a) is the only chiral pair. For \(n = 8\): \(2 \cdot 30 - 36 = 24\) of 36. The machine census — checking each necklace's reversal for membership in its own orbit — confirms all three, and the chiral residue is the honest measure of how much symmetry a reflection adds: it is never zero for long, and it grows. (c) With reflections, the group becomes dihedral, non-abelian: conjugacy classes stop being singletons, and the reflection classes cannot support one-dimensional waves the way rotations did — the sign-like characters exist, but the genuinely new waves are two-dimensional, and the table stops being a square. This is precisely the point of the method: naive frequency-thinking (Example 3's) survives only while the group is abian-poor; the character ledger handles the square, the triangle, the cube — five breeds, two wave-sizes at once — with the same three obligations. The exploration's open end: the dihedral split of the \(2^n\)-dimensional coloring space is a table the reader can now build for themselves, and its entries are not in this book — yet.
