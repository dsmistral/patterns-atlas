2 · A card that settles

A builder needs the diagonal of a unit square — the number that squares to \(2\). School arithmetic offers recipes and no answers: no fraction squares to \(2\), so the answer has to be cornered, not written. The recipe the ancients used is one line: to refine a guess \(x\), average it with the number divided by it, \[ g(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\left(x + \frac{2}{x}\right), \] and repeat. Start at the crudest honest guess there is, \(x_0 = 1\), and keep a card of the hits:

Read the error column, not the guess column. The errors run \(4.1421 \times 10^{-1}\), \(8.5786 \times 10^{-2}\), \(2.4531 \times 10^{-3}\), \(2.1239 \times 10^{-6}\), \(1.5947 \times 10^{-12}\), \(8.99 \times 10^{-25}\): correct decimals \(0, 1, 2, 5, 11, 24\) — doubling per hit, hit after hit, on a card that stabilizes at ten-decimal display after four hits and then keeps improving where the printer cannot show it.

Now the cold questions, and the third is the whole page. First: do the hits settle at all — is there some spot the guesses are walking into, or could an endless recipe wander forever? Second: if they settle, do they settle at the true diagonal — the number that squares to \(2\) — and not at some impostor the recipe happens to like? Third: how close is hit \(n\), in advance, before anyone runs it? A finite card answers none of these. P02's discipline stops where the data stops, and the data never stops; P15's ε-trap extracts digits beautifully once convergence is granted — but granting it was exactly what P15's contrast left unpaid, deferring the receipt to this page; and P21's machine would catch a crowd — the orbit is an endless record in a bounded room — but P21's catch could be anywhere and could be several, and P21 charged no price for the approach. The three questions need a machine that pays for all three at once.

So stand where P17's skeptic stood: a pattern suspected, three questions, nothing certified. What would a machine even look like, that could turn one rule into all three answers? Not a card — cards end. Not a trap alone — traps need something to catch. The next section names the shape of the hypothesis that buys the whole receipt, and the section after runs it on this very card, in public, with the price tag attached.
