Exercise 3 (●●●). Bernstein by hand, at all three depths. (a) Compute \(B_4(f)(\frac{1}{2})\) for \(f(x) = |x - \frac{1}{2}|\) directly from the five-term sum, and confirm Example 3's table entry. (b) Re-run Example 3's proof yourself, naming each quantity: given \(\varepsilon\), where does \(\delta\) come from, where does \(M\) come from, and what exactly must \(n\) exceed for the split to land below \(\varepsilon\)? (c) The rate: show why the gap at the kink shrinks like a constant over \(\sqrt{n}\), and identify the constant \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \approx 0.39894\) by treating \(X/n\) as a bell curve centered at \(x\) with spread \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\).

Hint

(a) Five terms, weights \(\binom{4}{k}\frac{1}{16}\); the middle term vanishes. (b) Example 3's display already carries the two halves — quote them and say which fact supplies each. (c) For a bell curve with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the mean absolute deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\); at \(x = \frac{1}{2}\) the binomial spread is exactly \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\).

Worked

(a) \(B_4(f)(\frac{1}{2}) = \frac{1}{16}\left[f(0) + 4f(\frac{1}{4}) + 6f(\frac{1}{2}) + 4f(\frac{3}{4}) + f(1)\right] = \frac{1}{16}\left[\frac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 0 + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\right] = \frac{1}{16} \cdot 3 = \frac{3}{16} = 0.1875\) — the table's first row, exactly. (b) \(\delta\) comes from uniform continuity of \(f\) on the compact interval \([0,1]\): it is the window inside which \(f\) cannot move more than \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). \(M\) is the maximum of \(|f|\), which exists for the same compactness reason. The split: on the event \(|X/n - x| < \delta\), the gap is at most \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) by the choice of \(\delta\); on the complement, which Chebyshev bounds at \(\frac{1}{4n\delta^2}\), the gap is at most \(2M\). So \(|B_n(f)(x) - f(x)| \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{M}{2n\delta^2}\), and the second term falls below \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) exactly when \(n > \frac{M}{\varepsilon\delta^2}\) — a bound that consults nothing about \(x\), which is why one \(n\) serves the whole interval. (c) Near a kink, the gap \(B_n(f)(x) - f(x)\) is, to first order, the mean absolute deviation of \(X/n\) from \(x\): the coin's tally is approximately a bell curve with standard deviation \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\), and a bell curve's mean absolute deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}\). At \(x = \frac{1}{2}\), \(\sigma = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\), so the gap is \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} = \frac{0.39894}{\sqrt{n}}\): at \(n = 100\) this predicts \(0.0399\), against the computed \(0.0398\); at \(n = 1000\) it predicts \(0.0126\), matching to four digits. The \(1/\sqrt{n}\) rate is not an accident of the kink — it is the coin's own spread law, and the honest price of the method: quadruple the flips to halve the error.
