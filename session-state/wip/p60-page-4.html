5 · Exhibits: when the completion forgets

Every pattern earns its keep by failing. Three exhibits — a monoid that forgets everything, an element that absorbs the world, and a completion built from the wrong relations — with the mitigation, the price, and the default.

Exhibit A — the monoid that forgets everything. Take the counting numbers with \(a \oplus b = \max(a, b)\): associative, zero \(0\), and \(2 \oplus 5 = 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) while \(2 \neq 3\) — cancellation fails at the smallest honest example. Run the construction anyway, honestly: the witness relation \((a,b) \sim (c,d)\) reads \(\max(a, d, k) = \max(b, c, k)\) for some \(k\), and \(k = \max(a,b,c,d)\) makes both sides \(k\) — every page is the same position as every other. The completion is the one-element group: not a defect of the construction but its verdict, delivered twice. Once by the collapse itself, Fig P60.3; once by universality: \(k \oplus k = k\) forces \(f(k) \cdot f(k) = f(k)\) in any group receiving the monoid, and a group's only idempotent is its identity — every additive invariant of the max monoid is trivial, so no group bigger than a point could have done the job. Difference-questions about max-addition are not merely hard; they are meaningless, and the completion says so by collapsing.

Mitigation: check cancellation before completing — the check is a search for \(a, k, b\) with \(a \oplus k = b \oplus k\) and \(a \neq b\), and at desk size it is a table scan. If the monoid fails, believe the collapse: it says the monoid has no nontrivial additive invariants, which is itself a theorem in disguise, and the honest invariant for max-addition is order-theoretic, not additive — no completion will conjure one.

Price: the collapse is total, and it is honest: "net worth" is a fiction for max-bookkeeping, and the completion is the fiction's auditor, returning zero.

Default: cancellation is the pre-flight check of the whole move; run it first, and if it fails, trust the one-element group — it is a verdict, not a malfunction.

Exhibit B — the absorbing element. Multiply the whole numbers \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\): associative, identity \(1\), and \(0 \times n = 0\) for every \(n\) — zero absorbs. The witness relation on this multiplicative monoid reads \(a \cdot d \cdot k = b \cdot c \cdot k\), and the witness \(k = 0\) makes both sides \(0\): every page is every other page, and the completion is again the one-element group. The absorbing element drags the whole world to a point — not because division is unnatural, but because "what times \(0\) gives \(1\)" deserves silence, and the construction, honest, gives it. Encounter 2 excised the zero and completed the positive whole numbers instead; the repair is exactly that: the completion of a monoid with an absorbing element is trivial, and the only honest completion is of the punctured world.

Mitigation: remove absorbing elements before completing — say which world the differences are about, and complete that world.

Price: the punctured monoid answers questions about the punctured world; anything phrased with the absorbing element in it is outside the completion's jurisdiction, honestly.

Default: an absorbing element is a certificate of collapse: if some \(z\) satisfies \(z \cdot a = z\) for every \(a\), the witness \(k = z\) merges all classes — banish \(z\) or accept the point.

Exhibit C — the wrong relations. Suppose the questions are not "this object minus that object" but "what remains when this cancels along that": \(X\) built from \(Y\) and its quotient \(X/Y\) wants the relation \([X] = [Y] + [X/Y]\) — an exact-sequence relation, not a direct-sum one. The direct-sum completion does not impose it: the pair world prices only sums, so the completed group comes out too big, carrying distinctions the questions never wanted, classes that should have merged. The honest object is a further quotient, imposing the exact-sequence relations on top of the completion; and the two quotients agree in well-behaved worlds — the resolution theorem says that when every object has a finite resolution by the nice ones, the direct-sum completion already satisfies the exact-sequence relations. Named here, proved nowhere on this page. In badly-behaved worlds the disagreement is not noise; it is the invariant that made non-regular rings a subject.

Mitigation: ask which relations the invariants you care about actually satisfy — direct-sum-additive, exact-sequence-additive, or both — and complete against the honest list.

Price: the further quotient is harder to compute (its elements are differences and relations), and in the well-behaved case the price was paid for nothing; the resolution theorem is the refund.

Default: "additive" is not one word: say direct-sum or exact-sequence before completing, and let the say-so be checked like any other promise.

7 · Lineage

  • 1957 — Grothendieck; 1958 — Borel–Serre. The Riemann–Roch manuscript: formulas about differences of vector bundles, \([E] - [F]\), priced by a completed monoid — its \(K\) from the German Klasse, "class". Grothendieck wrote the 1957 manuscript; Borel and Serre wrote it up and published it in 1958. For a field \(k\), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\) by dimension; for the integers of a number field, the same construction refines the classical class group; and the same \(K_0\), transported from algebraic bundles to topological ones by Atiyah and Hirzebruch, became topological K-theory.
  • Early 1970s — Barratt, Priddy, Quillen. \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+} \simeq \Omega^\infty S^\infty\): the group completion of the monoid of the symmetric groups' classifying spaces under block sum is the infinite loop space of the sphere spectrum — so the stable homotopy groups of spheres are a group completion, and the homotopy groups of the left-hand side are the stable stems. The "K-theory of \(\mathbb{F}_1\)" — the field with one element's would-be K-groups — is this page's honest metaphor for that statement.
  • 1976 — McDuff, Segal. The group-completion theorem: the engine that makes group completions of topological monoids computable, behind the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen door and many since. Quoted on this page as consistent with the literature, not source-verified — the scope box and Exercise 5 hold the receipt and the debt.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the receipts above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof.
