3 · Name the move

What the meadow taught, stated once and for all.

Pattern P09 — Find the invariant. When a problem hands you a process — a set of allowed moves carrying states to states — and asks whether some target can be reached, do not attack the target. Hunt the quantity the process cannot change: (1) define a quantity on the states; (2) prove that every allowed move preserves it exactly; (3) compute it at the start and at the target. If it disagrees, the target is unreachable — by any sequence of moves, of any length, ever. If it agrees, nothing follows: the invariant is a necessary condition, never a sufficient one. The same move, pointed the other way, pins values down: when the process must end in a single state and the invariant fixes a value there, that value is the answer, on every route at once.

Or, in words: before asking what a process can do, find what it cannot undo. A quantity that survives every move untouched is a wall: states on opposite sides of the wall never meet, and no amount of cleverness tunnels through arithmetic. But the wall only separates — landing on the same side as your target guarantees nothing, and the move is honest about that asymmetry: one disagreement closes the case forever; any number of agreements prove nothing at all.

Box P09.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The quantity must be defined on states, and computable at both ends. "The class of \(R - G \pmod{3}\)" is computed from any meadow snapshot in one subtraction, and every single-colour target carries one too. What breaks: the vague quantity — a feeling that "the colours stay balanced" is not a quantity; if you cannot compute it on a napkin for any state you might reach, it cannot separate anything.
  2. Preservation must hold for every move — proved, not sampled. Three meeting types, three checks of \(R - G\): changes of \(0\), \(-3\), \(+3\). All three, or nothing. A quantity preserved by most moves is preserved by nothing, because the walk is free to use the move you did not check. What breaks: the phantom invariant — block 5's first exhibit survives one of the three meetings and dies on the other two.
  3. The quantity must separate start from target. The meadow's total \(45\) is perfectly conserved — and perfectly useless, because every target also totals \(45\). An invariant earns its keep only by disagreeing. What breaks: the true-but-useless invariant, block 5's second exhibit.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: an impossibility ("can never"), a forced value ("must always"). Problem shape: a process — meetings, replacements, transformations — rather than a one-shot computation; the target sits at the end of a long walk whose individual steps are easy. Tools at hand: the move's arithmetic is visible enough to test candidate quantities against, a few at a time. If the claim is "compute this number" with no process in sight, this is not your page; if the claim is "no walk reaches there," it is almost nobody's page but this one.
