6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P20, the next move of tier 2 — the same counting skeleton in an algebraic costume. A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots: the "room" is all values it could vanish at, the "bad events" are its zeros, and the count is exact. So a polynomial that vanishes at \(d + 1\) points is not "probably" zero — it is zero, because the room of possible roots is too small to hold that many crimes; and two degree-\(d\) polynomials that agree at \(d + 1\) points agree everywhere. No coin appears anywhere, and yet the engine is this page's: count the outcomes, compare to the room's capacity, conclude. The second door is P25, the deterministic cousin: when the room is small enough — \(K_5\)'s \(1024\) colorings, the \(16\) and \(32\) colorings of the Schur census — the average can be replaced by the entire census, and the machine counts every candidate, good and bad, by name. P19 and P25 are the two ends of one axis: as the room grows, census dissolves into average, and the average is the only census a big room will ever afford. The far doors: P37, which differentiates in the presence of randomness — this page's coin given a calculus; P58, where density becomes recurrence, the soft-analysis shadow of counting; and P59, where the counting itself is sharpened by entropy — how much of a room can the bad events really cover.

The default habit

When a problem jams with the shape of a missing witness — you can say exactly what would count as an answer, but no search, census, or construction can reach it — ask whether the room of candidates can be named so that every one is counted exactly once. Then, in order: design the experiment, remembering the coin is yours to bias (Exhibit C); compute the average crime count by linearity, never demanding independence the sum does not need; compare to one; and price the conclusion before spending it — existence, not construction (Exhibit B), a floor, never a location (the \(R(4,4)\) row). If the count overflows, the verdict is "no verdict" (Exhibit A), and the next move is to redesign the experiment or the room, not to abandon the object. And when the count succeeds, three follow-ups, in rising order of effort: sample it — if the good fraction is overwhelming, a handful of random tries likely lands a witness; walk it — run the conditional-expectation walk of Example 2 and turn existence into a deterministic recipe; and ask whether you needed it at all — the smallest room that settles the question is the best one, and P02's napkin still beats every coin this page ever flips.
