Exercise 4 (◆). Diagnose a proposed application. A student announces: "The random method proves that for every \(n\), there is a two-coloring of \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) with no monochromatic Schur triple — no \(x, y, z\) of the same color with \(x + y = z\)." (a) Run the count at \(n = 4\): list the four triples \((1,1,2)\), \((1,2,3)\), \((1,3,4)\), \((2,2,4)\), compute the expected number of monochromatic triples under the fair coin (a repeated letter like \((1,1,2)\) is monochromatic with chance \(\frac{1}{2}\), a triple of distinct numbers with chance \(\frac{1}{4}\)), and note the verdict. Then run it at \(n = 5\), which adds \((1,4,5)\) and \((2,3,5)\). (b) Find the truth by census: how many colorings of \(\{1,\ldots,4\}\) avoid all four triples, and what happens at \(n = 5\)? Derive it by hand from the constraints, not by listing \(32\) colorings. (c) Diagnose in P01's chair: what was wrong with the student's claim before any counting started, what does the overflowing count actually license, and which pages of this book were the right tools all along?

Hint

(a) Sum the four chances; then add the two new ones. (b) From \((1,1,2)\) and \((2,2,4)\): \(c_1 \ne c_2\) and \(c_2 \ne c_4\), so \(c_1 = c_4\) (two colors only). Then \((1,3,4)\) forces \(c_3 \ne c_1\), and \((1,2,3)\) is satisfied automatically. Count the survivors, and then see what \((1,4,5)\) and \((2,3,5)\) do to them. (c) Ask what the count certifies when it is below \(1\), what it licenses when it is above, and whether any count could ever have certified a statement about every \(n\) at once.

Worked

(a) \(n = 4\): \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2} = 1.5\) — overflow, no verdict. \(n = 5\): \(1.5 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 2.0\), and the count only grows from there. (For calibration, \(n = 3\) has just \((1,1,2)\) and \((1,2,3)\): expected count \(0.75 < 1\), and avoiders do exist — the method is honest exactly where it succeeds.) (b) The constraints chain tightly: \(c_1 \ne c_2\) (else \((1,1,2)\) is mono), \(c_2 \ne c_4\) (else \((2,2,4)\)), so with two colors \(c_1 = c_4\); then \((1,3,4)\) having \(c_1 = c_4\) forces \(c_3 \ne c_1\), and \((1,2,3)\) cannot be monochromatic because \(c_1 \ne c_2\) already. So \(c_2 = c_3\) is the opposite color: exactly two colorings, \(\{1,4\}\) red with \(\{2,3\}\) blue, and the swap — a census of \(2\) out of \(16\), hiding under the overflowed count, Exhibit A's lesson exactly. At \(n = 5\): \((1,4,5)\) demands \(c_5 \ne c_1\) (since \(c_1 = c_4\)), and \((2,3,5)\) demands \(c_5 \ne c_2\) (since \(c_2 = c_3\)); but \(c_1 \ne c_2\), so \(c_5\) must avoid both colors — impossible. Zero colorings of \(32\); in fact no coloring of \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) exists for any \(n \ge 5\), which is Schur's theorem (1916) in its smallest case. (c) The diagnosis, in P01's chair: the student never pinned the claim. "For every \(n\)" is a universal existence statement, and the random method is a per-\(n\) tool — its count at each fixed \(n\) says nothing about the others, and here the counts above \(1\) licensed nothing while the truth was nonexistence. The overflowing \(1.5\) was not evidence against the object (Exhibit A: two good colorings survived it), and the student's mistake was reading silence — in both directions — as a verdict. The right tools were the small census (P02: sixteen and thirty-two colorings are napkin-sized), the pin before the proof (P01: state the quantifiers before counting), and, for the general theorem, the machine census (P25) or Schur's own argument — none of which flip a single coin. The count is a floor and a silence; it was never a survey of the truth.

Exercise 5 (◐). The walk's guarantee versus its luck. Example 2's derandomization walk places each vertex on the side that crosses more of its edges to already-placed vertices (ties broken toward the first side). (a) Prove the guarantee yourself: the walk always ends with at least \(\frac{m}{2}\) crossing edges, whatever the order. (b) The lab census runs the walk over all \(120\) vertex orders of \(K_5\) and finds all \(120\) land the maximum \(6\) — and over the \(24\) orders of the four-vertex path, where \(6\) land on \(2\), one short of the maximum \(3\). Reproduce a failing path walk by hand: vertices \(1, 2, 4, 3\) of the path \(1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\), and watch the guarantee hold while the maximum escapes. (c) Say what the \(K_5\) census does and does not prove: is "every order reaches the maximum on \(K_5\)" a theorem or a coincidence, and what would settle the question for a graph you choose?

Hint

(a) Count edges at the moment their later endpoint is placed; what does the greedy choice secure for each vertex's own earlier edges? (b) Place the vertices in the given order, breaking the one tie toward the first side, and tally crossings as they are secured. (c) A census over all orders is a proof for that graph; a statement about every graph needs either a proof or a counterexample — the path is where the luck runs out.

Worked

(a) Each edge belongs to exactly one placement: the moment its later endpoint (in the walk order) is placed. When vertex \(v\) is placed, the greedy choice puts it on the side crossing at least half of \(v\)'s edges to earlier vertices: the crossing secured is at least the non-crossing among those edges. Summed over all placements — which is all \(m\) edges, each counted once — the crossings are at least half the total. No coin, no room, no memory: a one-pass guarantee, and this is the method of conditional expectations in miniature. (b) Path \(1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\), order \(1, 2, 4, 3\). Vertex \(1\): no earlier edges, placed on the first side, \(A\). Vertex \(2\): one earlier edge, to \(1\) on \(A\); placing on \(B\) crosses it — secured \(1\). Vertex \(4\): no earlier edges, tie, placed on \(A\). Vertex \(3\): earlier edges to \(2\) (on \(B\)) and \(4\) (on \(A\)) — one each, a tie; the tie rule places it on \(A\), securing the edge to \(2\) but not the edge to \(4\). Final sides \(A = \{1, 3, 4\}\), \(B = \{2\}\): crossings are the edges \((1,2)\) and \((2,3)\) — total \(2 \ge \frac{3}{2}\), the guarantee honored, the maximum \(3\) (sides \(\{1,3\}\) against \(\{2,4\}\)) missed by exactly the tie. (c) The census proves, for \(K_5\), that every one of the \(120\) orders reaches \(6\) — that is a theorem about \(K_5\), established by exhaustive check. It does not prove that complete graphs are always so lucky, and it does not transfer: the path census shows a graph where \(6\) of \(24\) orders fall short, so "every order reaches the maximum" is a graph-by-graph property, not a law. What would settle it generally: either a proof (a class of graphs and a rule for when greedy is optimal) or a counterexample — and the hunt for one is exactly how P03's special-case discipline turns into new theorems. The guarantee is forever; the luck is local.
