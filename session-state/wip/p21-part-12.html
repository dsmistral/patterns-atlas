Exercise 3 · The room above the line ●●●
Prove Bolzano–Weierstrass for the unit square: an endless record of hits \(p_n = (x_n, y_n)\) in \([0,1]^2\) has a limit point. (a) Prove the quadrant lemma: split the square into four closed quadrants; some quadrant is hit infinitely often. (b) Build nested squares by repeatedly keeping an endlessly-hit quadrant, and show the two coordinate chains are nested intervals with lengths tending to zero, so each catches a point; show the two catches match. (c) Prove every disk around the catch is hit infinitely often, and compute the tax: the largest square level guaranteed to sit inside a disk of radius \(r\) is the one with side length below \(r/\sqrt{2}\) — for \(r = 0.1\), which level is that?

Hint

For (a), a finite pile of finite piles is finite — four quadrants this time. For (b), the x-intervals and y-intervals are each nested closed intervals of length \(2^{-n}\); the catch's coordinates are the shared points. For (c), the farthest two points of a square of side \(s\) are \(s\sqrt{2}\) apart — the tax \(\sqrt{2}\) is the price of squaring the window.

Worked

(a) If all four quadrants were hit finitely often, the record would be a finite pile of finite piles — finite. It is not; some quadrant is endlessly hit. (b) Keep such a quadrant: a square of side \(1/2\), endlessly hit. Iterate: nested squares of side \(2^{-n}\). The x-coordinates form nested closed intervals of length \(2^{-n}\): by Box P21.A's Half 2 they catch a point \(x^*\); likewise the y-chain catches \(y^*\). The pair \((x^*, y^*)\) lies in every kept square: the x-catch lies in every x-interval, the y-catch in every y-interval, and the squares are products of them. (c) A disk of radius \(r\) around the catch contains the whole kept square once the square's diagonal \(2^{-n}\sqrt{2}\) is below \(r\); that square is endlessly hit, so the disk is hit infinitely often — the catch is a limit point in the honest window sense, whatever the window's shape. The tax: \(2^{-n}\sqrt{2} < 0.1\) first holds at \(n = 4\) (side \(1/16\), diagonal \(\approx 0.0884\) < 0.1; at \(n = 3\) the diagonal \(\approx 0.177 > 0.1\)) — level 4, lab-checked. The square room halved cleanly; that is the whole generalization, and P41 builds on it.

Exercise 4 · The contradiction that wasn't ◆
A student runs §4's machine on the card with the left tie rule and catches \(1/3\). Then, suspicious, she reruns it with the right tie rule and catches \(2/3\). Her complaint: "The machine contradicts itself — first it said the crowd is at \(1/3\), then at \(2/3\). At most one of the runs is right." Diagnose the error precisely: what does the theorem claim, what does a single run certify, and what would it take to make "the" catch unique? Where has the book seen this confusion before?

Hint

Read Box P21's wording again, exactly: what is promised to exist, and what is promised to be unique? Then ask what a machine run is a witness of, and recall the page that censused everything and certified nothing.

Worked

The theorem claims a limit point exists; it does not claim that only one exists, and a single run certifies the existence of the point it caught — nothing more. Both runs are right: \(1/3\) and \(2/3\) are both genuine limit points (both window-certificates hold, by the closed-form arguments of §4), and the card's crowd set is exactly \(\{1/3, 2/3\}\) — the full audit is Exercise 5. The tie rule is part of the machine's design, not of the theorem's content: two different walks down the same tree, two different catches, no contradiction — exactly as a tie in a tournament sends two equally-ranked players to different brackets. The confusion is a type error, existence promoted to uniqueness, and it is P20's census lesson wearing analysis clothes: a complete census of the first thousand hits certifies nothing about the crowd, and one catch certifies nothing about the count of crowds. What would buy uniqueness: an extra hypothesis on the record — Exercise 2's one-crowd record, or P22's contraction, whose shrinking room leaves nowhere else to gather. Uniqueness is always a separate purchase; the receipt should say so.
