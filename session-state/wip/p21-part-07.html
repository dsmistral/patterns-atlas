Example 3 · The trap's floor — the intermediate value theorem

Moves used: P15 (the ε-trap itself, promoted from a trick to a theorem), P05 (the claim normalized to one function set to zero).

P15's ε-trap halved an interval to squeeze a number's digits out, and the page never asked the rude question: when the trap closes, is there anything in it? Take the claim \(x^3 = x + 1\). Normalized (P05): \(f(x) = x^3 - x - 1\), and the claim is \(f(x) = 0\). Probe the walls: \(f(1) = -1\), \(f(2) = 5\) — a sign change across \([1, 2]\). The intermediate value theorem: a continuous function that changes sign across a closed interval crosses zero somewhere inside. And the proof is the machine wearing the trap's own clothes.

Run the halving with the sign as the bit. At each level the interval carries a sign change — its endpoints' values have opposite signs. Halve at the midpoint \(m\): if \(f(m) = 0\), the root is exhibited and the theorem is done, so assume not. Then \(f(m)\) has the sign of one endpoint and not the other, so exactly one half inherits the sign change: keep it. Nested intervals, lengths to zero, a catch \(\rho\). Now continuity closes the case. Suppose \(f(\rho) = s > 0\): a window of \(\rho\) keeps values above \(s/2\); the kept intervals eventually sit inside it; but each of them has a left-or-right endpoint where \(f\) is negative — contradiction. Symmetrically no \(s < 0\). So \(f(\rho) = 0\): the sign change forced a crossing, and the trap's floor is a theorem. P15's walls always closed on something — this is the page that owes them that.

Forty halvings and the walls stand \(2^{-40} \approx 9.1 \times 10^{-13}\) apart — P15's own currency, earned honestly now. The catch: \(\rho \approx 1.3247179572\), and the defining claim pays its own check at the till: \(\rho^3 = \rho + 1 = 2.3247179572\), both sides certified in the lab. The number is the plastic constant, and it has a story — found not by a formula but by a trap with a floor under it; §7 tells where its name came from. The price here is the pleasant one: this machine's bit is the easiest bit in the whole book to compute — one sign look-up per level — and the same trap that proves the theorem is the one that finds the number. Existence and location, same machine, no extra charge.
