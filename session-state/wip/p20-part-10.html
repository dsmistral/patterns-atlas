Exhibit B · The forgery built for your test set — the uncapped degree

A student verifies the fifth-power formula of Example 1 the hard way: evaluate at \(n = 1\) through \(10\), compare with direct summation, ten for ten. Convinced? He should not be, and the reason is a construction. Take the true formula \(F\) and forge \(G(n) = F(n) + (n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-10)\): a different degree-10 polynomial that agrees with \(F\) at every single one of the ten tested points (the correction factor vanishes there), disagrees everywhere else, and at \(n = 11\) overshoots the truth by \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\) — against a true value of \(381{,}876\). Ten tests caught nothing because ten tests cannot: any test set of size \(k\) is defeated, in advance and by construction, by a degree-\(k\) correction. The failure is not in the testing but in what was never stated: a certificate is a claim about the budget, and the student never capped the degree. Example 1's certificate was honest because the difference's degree was bounded by \(5\), from the shape of the claim, before a single test ran. The same uncapped "it's just a polynomial, test it a lot" reflex is exactly how computer algebra systems used to ship bugs, and the repair is structural, not behavioral: no degree cap, no certificate — however impressive the run of agreements looks. (And the trap is two-sided: overestimate the degree and the certificate merely costs extra tests; underestimate it — testing a degree-10 difference at \(7\) points — and the certificate is void, again however many tests pass.)

Exhibit C · The parabola that agrees forever — the pile without the grid

The one-variable budget tempts a false generalization: "a nonzero polynomial in several variables cannot vanish at many points." False, and the counterexample is the first curve anyone draws. The polynomial \(P(x, y) = y - x^2\) vanishes at \((n, n^2)\) for every integer \(n\) — infinitely many agreeing points, unbounded in number, and \(P\) is not remotely zero. The one-variable theorem survives because its proof spends a budget per variable: in \(P(x,y)\) the degree in \(x\) is \(2\), in \(y\) is \(1\), and the honest multivariable certificate is the grid lemma of \S4 — the test points must cover whole rows times whole columns, a product structure, not a pile. No \(2 \times 2\) grid fits inside the parabola's integer points: a full grid \(A \times B\) on the curve would force every \(a^2\) for \(a \in A\) to equal every \(b \in B\), so \(|A| \ge 2\) pins \(A = \{a, -a\}\) and then \(B\) can hold only the single value \(a^2\). The parabola agrees along a one-dimensional thread, and a one-dimensional thread can dodge every rectangular grid while accumulating infinitely many points — which is precisely why \S4's grid lemma demands the grid and \S4's coin prices the pile.

Scope and price of P20

Three boundaries, stated while the exhibits are still warm. (i) The certificate is exactly as reliable as its degree cap. No cap from the shape of the claim, no certificate — Exhibit B's forgery passes any fixed test set by construction, and the cap must come from structure, never from confidence. (ii) The room must outrun the degree. Over a finite room the budget needs \(d + 1\) distinct points in it — Exhibit A's complete census of the five-hour clock is void, and the honest repairs are a bigger room or a coefficient comparison, which is P05's canonical form doing the certifying instead. (iii) In several variables, agreement must have structure. A pile of agreeing points proves nothing at any size — Exhibit C's parabola — and the certified shapes are the grid (\S4, Example 2) and the random point with its priced confidence (\S4, Example 3). What this page defers, it names: the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Noga Alon, 1999), which reads the same budget backwards — not "many agreements force identity" but "a coefficient that must be nonzero forces an input where the polynomial is nonzero", a construction tool that has settled open problems; and the Chevalley–Warning theorem (1935), which counts solutions over finite rooms when the degrees sum small. The price tag, said once: P20 verifies and never explains, and every certificate it issues carries its room's and its degree's fine print on its face.
