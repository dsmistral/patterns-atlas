Corollary 1 — B02's conjecture, hard half: coprime classes are invertible. Suppose \(\gcd(a, n) = 1\). By the identity, \(1 = ax + ny\) for some whole \(x, y\). Reduce mod \(n\): the \(ny\) term vanishes, leaving \(ax \equiv 1 \pmod n\) — the class of \(x\) is the inverse of the class of \(a\). Together with B03's easy half (invertible classes are coprime), B02's Exercise 4 census is now a theorem: the invertible classes mod \(n\) are exactly the classes coprime to \(n\). And the (2026, 1491) story tells itself once more: the certificate \(1 = -170 \times 2026 + 231 \times 1491\) was the inverse; \(-170 + 1491 = 1321\) only dressed it in census range. Check the theorem over full censuses — mod 7, 10, 12, 13 and 31: every coprime class has a certificate inverse, it agrees with the scan's inverse every time, and no other class has an inverse at all. The pattern B02 could only observe is now a consequence, both halves proved.

Corollary 2 — Euclid's lemma, and P05's debt paid. If \(n \mid ab\) and \(\gcd(n, a) = 1\), then \(n \mid b\). Proof: by the identity, \(1 = nx + ay\); multiply through by \(b\): \(b = nbx + aby\). The first term is a multiple of \(n\); the second is a multiple of \(ab\), and \(n\) divides \(ab\); their sum is a multiple of \(n\). So \(n \mid b\). Complete — three lines, and the oldest lemma about divisibility is a corollary of reachability. When \(n\) is prime it reads: \(p \mid ab\) forces \(p \mid a\) or \(p \mid b\) (if \(p\) misses \(a\), then \(\gcd(p, a) = 1\), and the lemma forces \(p \mid b\)). That prime case opens the road to unique factorization — which this page deliberately does not claim; the scope box below holds the line.

With it, the debt P05's page named when it reduced fractions: lowest terms are unique. Suppose \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), both fractions reduced, so \(\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(c, d) = 1\). Cross-multiply: \(ad = bc\). Then \(b \mid ad\) with \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\), so Euclid's lemma gives \(b \mid d\); symmetrically \(d \mid bc\) with \(\gcd(c, d) = 1\) gives \(d \mid b\). Two positive numbers that divide each other are equal — \(b = d\) — and then \(ad = bc\) becomes \(ad = dc\), so \(a = c\). The two reduced forms are the same fraction. A lowest-terms fraction has no rival: the uniqueness half of canonical form, which P05's page honestly could not prove, proved here in four lines.

Corollary 3 — B02's scope-box promise: dividing congruences, precisely. For \(c\) a positive whole number,

\[ac \equiv bc \pmod n \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; a \equiv b \pmod{n / g}, \qquad g = \gcd(c, n).\]

Proof: \(ac \equiv bc\) means \(n \mid c(a - b)\). Write \(c = gc'\) and \(n = gn'\) with \(\gcd(c', n') = 1\); then \(n' \mid c'(a - b)\), and Euclid's lemma — applied to \(n'\) dividing a product while coprime to one factor — gives \(n' \mid (a - b)\), which is \(a \equiv b \pmod{n'}\) with \(n' = n/g\). Every step reverses, so the equivalence is exact. B02's promise was the special case: cancel freely exactly when the canceled factor shares nothing with the modulus (\(g = 1\), nothing shrinks).

B02's counterexample, reconciled at last: \(2 \times 3 \equiv 2 \times 8 \pmod{10}\) (both products land on the class of 6), yet \(3 \not\equiv 8 \pmod{10}\) — so cancellation seemed to fail. The condition says why: \(g = \gcd(2, 10) = 2\), so the honest conclusion is \(3 \equiv 8 \pmod 5\) — and that is true; both are 3 mod 5. The modulus shrank with the cancellation; nothing failed. And when \(g = 1\) nothing shrinks: \(5 \times 2 \equiv 5 \times 9 \pmod 7\) (both land on the class of 3) cancels freely to \(2 \equiv 9 \pmod 7\) — sound, because 5 and 7 share nothing.

Corollary 4 — linear congruences, counted. \(ax \equiv c \pmod n\) is solvable exactly when \(g = \gcd(a, n)\) divides \(c\); and then it has exactly \(g\) classes of solutions mod \(n\). Solvability is Box B04.A read aloud: solving means some combination \(ax - nk = c\), and the combinations of \(a\) and \(n\) are exactly the multiples of \(g\). Counting: divide through by \(g\) — Corollary 3 licenses it, sides and modulus both — to get \(a'x \equiv c' \pmod{n'}\) with \(\gcd(a', n') = 1\); Corollary 1 hands the certificate inverse of \(a'\), so there is exactly one solution class mod \(n'\), and each class mod \(n'\) splits into exactly \(g\) classes mod \(n\). Section 3(c) was this corollary running live: \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\) — \(g = 1\), one class, \(\{11\}\); \(6x \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\) — \(g = 3\) divides 9, three classes, \(\{4, 9, 14\}\); \(4x \equiv 7 \pmod{10}\) — \(g = 2\) misses 7, none. A census over every coefficient \(a\) and target \(c\) for every \(n\) from 5 to 16 agrees exactly with the count — \(g\) classes when \(g \mid c\), zero when not, no exceptions anywhere in the grid.

Scope, honestly. Three boundaries. (i) This page handles pairs. For three numbers, apply the identity twice — reach \(\gcd(a, b)\), then combine with \(c\), using the associativity B03's Exercise 3 proved — done on demand, not built here. (ii) Unique factorization — every whole number above 1 factors into primes in exactly one way — is not claimed on this page. Corollary 2's prime case opens the road, but the assembly needs induction, P66's page; default until then: the road is open, the theorem is not. (iii) Exercise 4's stamp conjecture — largest unmakeable \(ab - a - b\) for coprime \(a, b\) — is verified on two cases and conjectured beyond them; its general proof is genuinely harder than anything here, and stays open. What this page closes, it closes completely; what it defers, it names.
