3 · Worked by hand

Four examples, simplest first, each carried to the end. Under all of them runs Box B03.

(a) gcd(48, 36), and the price of pure subtraction

The dance: \(48 = 1 \times 36 + 12\), then \(36 = 3 \times 12 + 0\). The last nonzero leftover is 12, so \(\gcd(48, 36) = 12\). Done in two divisions. Euclid's subtraction form computes the same thing in four steps — subtract 36 once (\(48 - 36 = 12\)), then subtract 12 three times (\(36 = 3 \times 12\)) — and the difference is harmless here. It is not always harmless: for (1071, 462) below, subtraction takes twelve steps where division takes three, because the subtraction count is exactly the sum of the quotients (\(2 + 3 + 7 = 12\); the lab counts both). When the quotients are large, batching matters.

(b) 1071 and 462, two routes to the same 21

Route one — factorization. Find the prime factors of each number and read off the shared ones. By trial division: \(1071 = 3 \times 357\), \(357 = 3 \times 119\), \(119 = 7 \times 17\), so \(1071 = 3^2 \times 7 \times 17\); likewise \(462 = 2 \times 231 = 2 \times 3 \times 77 = 2 \times 3 \times 7 \times 11\). The primes appearing in both: \(\{3, 7\}\). So the greatest common divisor is \(3 \times 7 =\) 21. The route works, but notice its price: to be sure of the factor lists, we paid trial divisions up to \(\sqrt{1071}\) — and for large numbers that price explodes, which is exactly why modern cryptography trusts big numbers. No shortcut to factoring is known.

Route two — the dance. Divide, keep the leftover, repeat:

\[1071 = 2 \times 462 + 147, \qquad 462 = 3 \times 147 + 21, \qquad 147 = 7 \times 21 + 0.\]

Three divisions, last nonzero leftover 21 — and now look back at Fig B03.1: the quotients 2, 3, 7 are precisely the square counts of the rectangle cutting. The dance and the square-cutting are the same procedure with different notation; the last square's size is the gcd. The dance never asked for a factorization. It found the shared part of both numbers without ever finding a prime — and it is fast precisely because the leftovers shrink quickly (section 4 proves they at least halve every two steps).

(c) 2026 and 1491 — a coprimality test with a payoff

Task two's strip-cutting, now in division notation:

\[2026 = 1 \times 1491 + 535,\; 1491 = 2 \times 535 + 421,\; 535 = 1 \times 421 + 114,\; 421 = 3 \times 114 + 79,\]

\[114 = 1 \times 79 + 35,\; 79 = 2 \times 35 + 9,\; 35 = 3 \times 9 + 8,\; 9 = 1 \times 8 + 1,\; 8 = 8 \times 1 + 0.\]

Nine divisions, ending at leftover 1 — so \(\gcd(2026, 1491) = 1\): the two numbers are coprime, and no factoring was needed to prove it (for contrast, the factor route must factor \(2026 = 2 \times 1013\) with 1013 prime, and \(1491 = 3 \times 7 \times 71\) — the lab confirms both factorizations).

Here is the payoff B02 promised. Its Exercise 4 conjecture — invertible exactly when coprime — has an easy half that this page can already settle, proved in section 4: every invertible class is coprime to the modulus. And the hard half (coprime classes are invertible) is now a concrete question about this very dance: the strip-cutting says 2026 is a good candidate for an inverse mod 1491, and a brute-force search over the classes finds one — \(2026 \times 1321 = 2{,}676{,}346\), and \(2{,}676{,}346 = 1795 \times 1491 + 1\), so \(2026 \times 1321 \equiv 1 \pmod{1491}\). The brute force finds the witness; what it cannot explain is why a witness must exist, and why 1321 was sitting inside the dance all along. That explanation — the dance run backwards — is the whole subject of B04.
