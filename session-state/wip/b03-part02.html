2 · The idea, named

Box B03 — the gcd survives the division dance. For whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(b > 0\), write B01's division \(a = qb + r\) with leftover \(0 \le r < b\). Then the pairs \((a, b)\) and \((b, r)\) have exactly the same common divisors — so, if either pair has a greatest one, both have the same greatest one:

\[\gcd(a, b) \;=\; \gcd(b, r).\]

The Euclidean algorithm: divide, pass from \((a, b)\) down to \((b, r)\), and repeat. The leftovers strictly shrink (\(a > b > r > \dots \ge 0\)), so the dance must end; it ends exactly when a leftover is 0, and the last nonzero leftover is \(\gcd(a, b)\).

Or, in words: chopping \(a\) into \(q\) stacks of height \(b\) plus a leftover \(r\) hides nothing from any common divisor. Whatever divided the whole collection and the stack height also divides the height and the leftover; whatever divides the height and the leftover also divides the whole collection. The family of common divisors moves down the dance untouched — so its largest member moves down with it, and the dance ends at the smallest pair that still carries it.

Two words get defined here, at first use:

  • New word: common divisor. A number \(d\) that divides both \(a\) and \(b\). For 1071 and 462 the full list is \(\{1, 3, 7, 21\}\) (the lab re-finds it by scanning) — and the greatest, 21, is exactly task one's last square.
  • New word: coprime. Two numbers whose common divisors are only 1 — equivalently \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\). Task two proved 1491 and 2026 coprime; 1071 and 462 are not (21 is a common divisor).

Examples and non-examples:

  • Yes, small: \(\gcd(48, 36) = 12\). Dance: \(48 = 1 \times 36 + 12\), then \(36 = 3 \times 12 + 0\) — two divisions, last nonzero leftover 12.
  • Yes, with zero: \(\gcd(a, 0) = a\) for \(a > 0\). Every number divides 0, so the common divisors of \((a, 0)\) are just the divisors of \(a\), and the dance ends before it starts: the last nonzero member of the pair is \(a\) itself.
  • No, both zero: \(\gcd(0, 0)\) does not exist — every number divides both, so there is no greatest one. The honest answer is "undefined", not 0.
  • Yes, trivially: \(\gcd(a, a) = a\) — the first division reads \(a = 1 \times a + 0\) in a single step. (The lab checks these edge behaviors over a grid: \(\gcd(a, 0) = a\), \(\gcd(a, a) = a\) for all \(a \le 200\), zero failures.)

The algorithm is Euclid's, from Book VII of the Elements (Propositions 1–2, around 300 BCE) — though Euclid wrote it as repeated subtraction, which the Greeks called anthyphairesis, mutual subtraction. Division is just subtraction batched: where Euclid subtracts 462 from 1071 twice in a row, we do one division and read the quotient 2. Both views compute the same dance; the division form simply reaches the bottom faster. The claim that the dance always ends, and that its last leftover is the greatest common divisor, is proved in section 4 — nothing in this page rests on Euclid's authority.
