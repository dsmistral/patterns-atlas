5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

The move's failures are worth cataloguing precisely, because most of them are near-misses: operations and pairings that look like compositions and are not. The probes of Box P06.A exist to catch exactly these.

  • An operation can have an identity and still be dead. Term-wise multiplication \\((a, b) \otimes (c, d) = (ac, bd)\\) passes the identity probe: \\((1,1)\\) is its identity, and \\((a,b) \otimes (1,1) = (a,b)\\) holds. It fails the invariant probe: norms do not multiply. Witness, run by the lab as a must-fail row: \\((7,4) \otimes (8,1) = (56, 4)\\), and \\(56^2 + 4^2 = 3152 \ne 4225 = (49+16)(64+1)\\). Mitigation: probe the invariant on small cases before trusting any offered operation — two lines of arithmetic. Price: nearly every operation you invent will fail the probe; that is what the probe is for. Default: identity probe first, invariant probe second, and only then compose.
  • Averaging is not composing. The encounter's two squaring witnesses \\((33, 56)\\) and \\((63, 16)\\) sit on the same circle, and the "obvious" ways of combining coordinates all fail the invariant. Their midpoint \\((48, 36)\\) has norm \\(48^2 + 36^2 = 3600 = 60^2\\) — a different circle, a 60-triple, not a 65-triple. Worse, the plausible mixed pairing — half-sum and half-difference of the two first coordinates, \\(63\\) and \\(33\\), giving \\((48, 15)\\) — has norm \\(48^2 + 15^2 = 2529 \ne 4225\\), and the lab runs this as the page's starred false line. Only the \\(\odot\\) recipe — cross-multiply, subtract, add — carries the norm. Mitigation: when a combining rule is offered, ask which composition it is the recipe for; if it is none, no invariant is coming. Price: the recipe looks arbitrary from outside (block 7 says where it comes from); from inside, the invariant is the explanation. Default: combine by a licensed route or say honestly that you have no way to combine.
  • Composing angles is not adding coordinates. Rotations compose by adding angles — but a reader who transfers that to coordinates writes the false line \\(2\sin 35^\circ = 1.147\\) against the true \\(\sin 70^\circ = 0.940\\) (both lab-checked to three decimals; the discrepancy is the point). The invariant is multiplicative (norms), the composition acts on angles, and no individual coordinate is additive under the composition. Mitigation: compose in the object that owns the identity — the circle pair \\((\cos t, \sin t)\\) — and read coordinates out only at the end. Default: this is exactly what Example 2 does; imitate it.
  • Some classes never compose at all — and a census can prove the "never". Sums of two squares compose (B12). Sums of four squares compose (Euler's four-square identity — the lab verifies it over all \\(65{,}536\\) pairs of quadruples from 0 to 3, and uses it to climb: \\(105\\), which is not a sum of two squares at all, is \\(2^2 + 7^2 + 6^2 + 4^2\\), and composition carries it onward). But sums of three squares do not compose, and the reason is a single honest number: \\(15\\). Both \\(3 = 1 + 1 + 1\\) and \\(5 = 4 + 1 + 0\\) are three-square sums, their product is 15, and \\(15\\) is not a three-square sum — so the class is not closed, so no identity-composition on it can exist, no matter how clever the recipe. The lab's three censuses over \\(1\\) to \\(100\\): 43 numbers are sums of two squares, all 100 are sums of four squares, and exactly 15 refuse three squares — \\(7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71, 79, 87, 92, 95\\) — matching Legendre's criterion (numbers of the form \\(4^a(8b+7)\\)) exactly, in both directions. Honest scope: the full story of which dimensions admit norm-multiplying compositions is a famous theorem (Hurwitz: only 1, 2, 4 and 8) and belongs to a much later tier of this book — an orientation page, forward-referenced, not proved here. What this page certifies is the ground floor: closure is a prerequisite, and a census is how you test it.
  • The identity itself can be misread as "doing something". Composing with \\((1,0)\\) returns the object — \\((2,3) \odot (1,0) = (2,3)\\), \\(4 + 9 = 13\\) unchanged — and a reader expecting the composition to "generate" something new is tempted to skip the identity as useless. The mistake runs the other way: the identity is the certification, the one composition whose output is known in advance, and an operation that fails it (a "composition" that changes the object when composed with its own identity) was never an operation. The lab's 169-pair identity sweep and the four-square identity's \\((1,0,0,0)\\) row are this probe running. Default: run the identity row first, every time, on every new machine.
