7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. The composition of two-square sums is older than its name: Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE) states the two-route rule for combining differences of squares — his word bhāvanā means exactly "composition" — as a machine for manufacturing new solutions from old ones, which is this page's witness factory verbatim. Fibonacci's Liber Quadratorum (1225) carries the same identity into Europe, and Euler's 1748 letter to Goldbach extends the machine to four squares. The complex-number reading — pairs as arrows, norms as squared lengths — is Gauss's language, and Hamilton's 1843 quaternions are the four-square composition discovered as a number system (he carved them into a bridge; ask block 5's Hurwitz pointer why the story stops at eight). None of this history is load-bearing; it says the machine you just learned to aim has been standard issue for fourteen centuries.
