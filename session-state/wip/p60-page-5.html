Exercise 1 ●○○ — the ledger, by hand. (a) Verify that \((2,5)\) and \((3,6)\) are the same position — \(2 + 6 = 5 + 3\) — and find the normal form (a single \([(n,0)]\) or \([(0,n)]\)) of each. (b) Compute \([(3,7)] + [(5,2)]\), reduce to a normal form, and name the integer school calls it. (c) Show \([(4,4)]\) is the zero and check \([(2,0)] + [(0,2)] = 0\) by the ledger's law.

Hint

For (a), the normal form of \((a, b)\) is \((a - b, 0)\) if \(a \geq b\) and \((0, b - a)\) otherwise — the net is the ledger's whole story. For (b), classes add columnwise: \((3,7) + (5,2) = (8,9)\), and then the net speaks. For (c), any page \((n,n)\) balances against \((0,0)\): \(n + 0 = n + 0\).

Worked answer

(a) \(2 + 6 = 8 = 5 + 3\), same position; both have net \(-3\), normal form \([(0,3)]\). (b) \([(3,7)] + [(5,2)] = [(8,9)]\), and \((8,9)\) is the position of \((0,1)\) since \(8 + 1 = 9 + 0\) — the integer \(-1\): net \(-4\) plus net \(+3\). (c) \((4,4) \sim (0,0)\) by \(4 + 0 = 4 + 0\), so \([(4,4)]\) is the zero — as is every \([(n,n)]\); and \([(2,0)] + [(0,2)] = [(2,2)]\), the position of \((0,0)\) by \(2 + 0 = 2 + 0\) — the two answers are negatives, as they must be: nets \(+2\) and \(-2\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the two-point space, by hand. (a) Verify \([(3,1)] - [(1,1)] = [(2,0)]\): remember that \([(1,1)]\) is the zero, and check \((3,1)\) against \((2,0)\) by the ledger's law. (b) Compute the dimension vector of \([(3,1)] - [(2,0)]\) and reduce to a normal form. (c) Confirm that \(\mathbb{N}^2\) cancels componentwise — every instance with entries \(\leq 4\) — and say why that licenses \(K_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2\) rather than a quotient of it.

Hint

For (a), the law \(a + d = b + c\) applied to the two pages settles it. For (b), subtracting \([(2,0)]\) is adding \([(0,2)]\) — the inverse is the flipped page. For (c), cancellation means the map \(\eta\) embeds: no two distinct bundles ever merge.

Worked answer

(a) \((1,1) \sim (0,0)\) by \(1 + 0 = 1 + 0\), so subtracting \([(1,1)]\) changes nothing; and \((3,1)\) against \((2,0)\): \(3 + 0 = 1 + 2\), same position — so \([(3,1)] - [(1,1)] = [(2,0)]\). (b) \([(3,1)] + [(0,2)] = [(3,3)]\), the position of \((1,1)\) by \(3 + 1 = 3 + 1\): dimension vector \((1,1)\) — a virtual bundle, one-dimensional at each point, that no actual bundle instantiates with negative parts... and none needs to: the vector is honest because it is a class of pages, not a bundle. (c) Componentwise: \((a_1, b_1) + (c, d) = (a_2, b_2) + (c, d)\) forces \(a_1 = a_2\) and \(b_1 = b_2\), digit by digit — every bounded instance passes. Cancellation embeds \(\eta\), so the monoid survives into its completion intact, and the completion is exactly the integer lattice \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) — one ledger per point, two points, no merging.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the collapse, proved twice. (a) Verify \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5 = 5\) on the max monoid, and conclude that any group receiving the monoid honestly must merge the images of 2 and 3. (b) Show \((a, 0) \sim (b, 0)\) for all \(a, b \leq 8\): exhibit the witness and check the full \(9 \times 9\) table. (c) Prove that every hom from the max monoid to a group is trivial, using the idempotent \(k \oplus k = k\), and confirm the group fact it leans on — a group's only idempotent is the identity — over the multiplication tables of \(\mathbb{Z}/5\), \(\mathbb{Z}/6\), and \(S_3\).

Hint

For (a), apply \(f\) to both sides of \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) and cancel in the group. For (b), the witness wants both sides equal to it: \(k = \max(a, b)\) wants a look. For (c), \(f(k \oplus k) = f(k) f(k)\), and the left side is also just \(f(k)\).

Worked answer

(a) \(f(2) f(5) = f(5) = f(3) f(5)\), and the group cancels \(f(5)\): \(f(2) = f(3)\) — the merge is forced in every group at once, which is why the completion itself must merge them. (b) At \(k = \max(a,b)\): \(\max(a, 0, k) = k = \max(0, b, k)\), both sides reading \(k\) — all \(81\) pairs \(a, b \leq 8\) pass, so every page \((a,0)\) is the position of every other: one class. (c) \(k \oplus k = k\) gives \(f(k) f(k) = f(k)\), hence \(f(k) = e\) by cancelling one \(f(k)\); and \(x x = x\) forcing \(x = e\) is confirmed by scanning the three tables — \(25\), \(36\), and \(36\) products respectively, no idempotents but the identities. So every \(f(k) = e\): the completion is the one-element group, and Exhibit A's collapse is not one example but the whole story, twice over.

Exercise 4 ◆ — the rationals, diagnosed. Which pattern builds \(\mathbb{Q}\) from \(\mathbb{Z}\)? Name the move, and say what part of it is not this page's machine: exhibit the relation a naive fraction construction would use, prove that relation transitive on \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\), and then watch the same-shaped relation fail transitivity on the max monoid — and say what the two outcomes together teach about the machine's honest jurisdiction.

Worked answer

The move is P31's, one layer up: define by the job — the ring that receives \(\mathbb{Z}\) and inverts every nonzero element, most economically. The machine that does it is localization, a sibling of group completion, not this page's machine run on some monoid of \(\mathbb{Z}\). Group-completing the multiplicative monoid \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\) does produce the nonzero rationals as a multiplicative group — classes \([(a,b)]\) read as \(a/b\), the relation \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\), the ledger with its columns multiplied — but it says nothing about addition, and \(\mathbb{Q}\) is its addition as much as its multiplication; the localization must extend both operations at once, and their interaction (distributivity) is precisely what a one-operation construction cannot see. The naive relation \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\) is transitive on \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\) because that monoid cancels: from \(a d = b c\) and \(c f = d e\), multiply to get \(a c d f = b c d e\) and cancel \(c d\). On the max monoid the same-shaped relation — \(\max(a, d) = \max(b, c)\) — fails at the witness \((2,3), (3,3), (3,2)\), which is Box P60.A's opening exhibit. The two outcomes draw the jurisdiction line: the pair construction is lawful exactly where the monoid cancels, patched by the witness where it does not; and a world with two interacting operations needs the job stated for both at once — P31's argument, run on a ring.

Exercise 5 ◐ — the paper trail. (a) Find the Grothendieck group construction in the literature — it is classical — and compare its relation and universal property with Box P60.A. (b) Locate the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen statement as the literature phrases it, and compare with Box P60.B and the lineage: does the page's phrasing of \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+} \simeq \Omega^\infty S^\infty\) survive? (c) This page quoted the group-completion theorem of McDuff and Segal (1976) as consistent with the literature but not source-verified: find the paper, check the statement's shape, and correct this page's lineage if the record differs.

Hint

For (a), the classical construction is often stated for cancellative commutative monoids, where the witness patch is invisible; ask what changes without cancellation. For (b), the plus is the plus construction on \(B\Sigma_\infty\), and the page's reading — a completion of the block-sum monoid of classifying spaces — is the claim to check. For (c), the receipts to come back with: the paper's own statement, its hypotheses, and what "group completion" means there.

Worked answer

(a) The classical construction for a cancellative commutative monoid is the pairs-and-classes machine of Box P60.A with the witness never needed — the page's patch is the honest generalization, and §5's max monoid is the reason the patch exists: without it, "same position" is not even transitive. (b) The literature's Barratt–Priddy–Quillen identifies \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+}\) with \(\Omega^\infty S^\infty\), whose homotopy groups are the stable stems — matching Box P60.B's phrasing; the group-completion viewpoint reads the left-hand side as the completion of the monoid of the \(\Sigma_n\)'s classifying spaces under block sum, which is this page's reading, and the identification is the statement that the stable stems are a group completion. (c) The honest answer is the one the scope box gave: the 1976 theorem is quoted as literature-consistent, and this exercise's product is the correction — with the correction, if any, going into the lineage section and the lab, exactly as the warn-box promises.
