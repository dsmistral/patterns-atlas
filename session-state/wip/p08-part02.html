2 · Meet the problem

Diwali dinner. Eight relatives, one round table, eight identical chairs. How many different seatings are there?

Start where brute force starts. If the chairs were in a row — chair 1, chair 2, …, chair 8, all different — the count is a permutation count: \(8! = 40{,}320\) ways. So is that the answer? Try it small first (P02's instinct), because something about "round" is itching. Seat three people, \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), around a small table, and list the row-orders:

\[ABC, \quad BCA, \quad CAB, \qquad ACB, \quad CBA, \quad BAC.\]

Six row-orders — but only two seatings. Walk around the table and check: \(ABC\) and \(BCA\) and \(CAB\) are the same seating, everyone with the same left and right neighbours, merely rotated. The other three are the second seating. Each seating was counted three times, once per rotation, and the count repairs itself by division:

\[\frac{3!}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2 \quad \text{seatings.}\]

Four people, same check: \(4! = 24\) row-orders, and each seating is described by exactly four of them (its four rotations), so \(24/4 = 6\) seatings. The pattern is not a coincidence — it is a proof shape. Around a table, the only thing that defines a seating is who sits next to whom; a rotation moves every person but changes no neighbour, so rotated orders are descriptions of one seating. And with all eight relatives distinct, no rotation other than the full turn can return everyone to their own chair — each seating therefore has exactly eight row-descriptions, its eight rotations, and no more. So the dinner count is

\[\frac{8!}{8} = \frac{40{,}320}{8} = 5{,}040 \quad \text{seatings}.\]

Notice what actually happened, because it is the whole move in miniature. The question — who neighbours whom — was unchanged by a rotation; the row-count was not. A count of all descriptions (\(40{,}320\)), divided by the number of descriptions per seating (\(8\), proved, not guessed), produced the count of things actually asked about. Nothing was built; nothing was transformed. The problem arrived with its symmetry installed — the table's roundness did that — and the move just charged the clones for their share. The move has a name, and it is the eighth pattern of the spine.
