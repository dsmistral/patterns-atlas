3 · Worked by hand

Three workings, simplest first, each carried to the end. Under all of them run Boxes B06 and B06.A.

(a) Ben's whole logbook, translated both ways

Ben's dictionary and its reverse sit side by side; run every waypoint of Lecture 0 through both, in one pass:

\[\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}; \qquad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Six translations, zero re-solving — the whole point of filing. One more observation, and it is the deepest fact in this section's neighborhood: in B05, the point \(M = (4,3)\) was the midpoint of the treasure \(P = (7,5)\) and its neighbour \(Q = (1,1)\). The dictionary agrees: in Ben's readings the same three points are \((2,1)\), \((3,2)\), \((1,0)\) — and \((2,1)\) is exactly the average of \((3,2)\) and \((1,0)\). The dictionary did not just locate the points; it carried the arithmetic: midpoints stayed midpoints. B05's Claim 4 promised this for schemes; here it is what the dictionary preserves, and section 4 states it as the property that makes wholesale re-measuring safe.

(b) The wholesale translation — a fence re-measured

A single waypoint is a courier's job; the harbourmaster's real work is the wholesale kind: a whole shape, re-measured in another scheme at once. The island builds a square fence around the treasure — corners \((7,5)\), \((9,5)\), \((9,7)\), \((7,7)\) on the school map, side 2, area 4. Ben must stake it out in his own readings. Run each corner through the reverse dictionary \(M_{\text{school} \to \text{Ben}}\):

\[\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 9 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Look at what came out, with a pencil over Fig B06.2: four corners in, four corners out. Corners go to corners, and the shape's arithmetic travels with them: on the map the square's two diagonals share the midpoint \((8,6)\) — check, \((7,5)\) and \((9,7)\) average to it, and so do \((9,5)\) and \((7,7)\) — and in Ben's book the translated diagonals share \((4,2)\): \((3,2)\) with \((5,2)\), and \((1,4)\) with \((7,0)\). The centre travelled with the fence. And now the honest surprise, the one this block will mine again and again: on Ben's own grid those four readings do not stake a coordinate square — they stake the parallelogram of Fig B06.2, with unequal side-walks: from \((3,2)\) to \((1,4)\) is \(-2\) road-steps plus \(2\) fence-steps, while from \((1,4)\) to \((5,2)\) it is \(4\) road-steps minus \(2\) fence-steps. The fence never stopped being a square — each side still measures \(2\) school units, and the sides still meet at right angles — but its picture belongs to the scheme, not the shape: school prints it square, Ben prints it slanted, and neither picture is wrong. What survives every scheme is the arithmetic — corners to corners, diagonals to diagonals, the shared centre — and, as section 4 proves, the area up to the determinant's printed exchange rate. Hold that surprise: B07 and B08 turn it into a method — choose the scheme whose picture is the simple one.

Now the numbers get interesting. The fence has area 4 in school units. In Ben's units? His cell — one road-step by one fence-step — has area \(|D| = 1\) school unit (Fig B06.1), so his cells count area the same way school squares do, and the fence's area number in Ben readings is \(4 / 1 = 4\): no surprise. But bring in Elena, the surveyor whose directions are \((3,1)\) and \((1,3)\), with \(D = 8\). Her cell has area 8 school units — one of her cells covers eight of Ana's squares — so the very same fence measures \(4/8 = \tfrac{1}{2}\) in her readings. Run her reverse dictionary on the corners, by Box B06.A with \(D = 8\):

\[M_{\text{school} \to \text{Elena}} = \frac{1}{8}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}: \quad (7,5) \to (2,1), \;\; (9,5) \to \left(\tfrac{11}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4}\right), \;\; (9,7) \to \left(\tfrac{5}{2}, \tfrac{3}{2}\right), \;\; (7,7) \to \left(\tfrac{7}{4}, \tfrac{7}{4}\right).\]

Readings with fractions — honest, and worth a pause: Elena's steps are long, so fine positions need fractional step-counts. The shape is unchanged, the area number shrank by exactly her cell's size, and the determinant \(D = 8\) printed the exchange rate before any corner was translated. That is the determinant's second job, promised by B05 and delivered in section 4: \(|D|\) is the area of the scheme's cell, the dictionary's exchange rate for areas.
