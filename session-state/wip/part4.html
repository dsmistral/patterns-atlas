4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per family in the catalogue. The first is Archimedes' own: an area cornered by triangles, two thousand years before calculus. The second certifies a constant's digits with nothing but fraction arithmetic. The third builds the move's closing act — the ε-trap — and shows it pin three famous quantities: a decimal, an area, a root.

Example 1 · The parabola's cap, cornered to \(\tfrac{4}{3}\)

Moves used: P07 building the triangle-filling machine · P02 tabulating the covered sums · P15 trapping the area between covered and overhang.

Take the parabola \(y = x^2\) and the chord joining \((-1, 1)\) to \((1, 1)\). The parabolic segment is the cap between chord and curve, and the question is its area. Calculus answers in one line at the class-12 floor; Archimedes answered it around 250 BC with a squeeze — and this page rebuilds his, because it shows the move running where no formula is allowed to help.

The inscribed triangle first: vertex at \((0, 0)\), base the chord. Its area is exactly \(1\) — the shoelace on \((-1,1)\), \((1,1)\), \((0,0)\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}\,|{-1}(1-0) + 1(0-1) + 0(1-1)| = \tfrac{1}{2}|-2| = 1\). Call it \(T = 1\). The triangle is inside the cap: each slanted side is a chord of the parabola, a parabola lies below every one of its chords, and the base is the cap's own lid — so the covered area starts at \(T\).

Now the machine, one P07-style construction. Each slanted side leaves a sliver between itself and the curve. In each sliver, inscribe the triangle whose base is the sliver's chord and whose apex sits where the tangent parallel to that chord touches the parabola. The left sliver's chord runs from \((-1,1)\) to \((0,0)\), slope \(-1\); the tangent to \(y = x^2\) has slope \(2x\), which equals \(-1\) at \(x = -\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the apex is \((-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4})\). The new triangle \((-1,1)\), \((0,0)\), \((-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{4})\) has area exactly \(\tfrac{1}{8}\) — shoelace again — one-eighth of its parent. The mirror triangle on the right contributes the same, and stage 2 covers \(T + 2 \cdot \tfrac{T}{8} = \tfrac{5T}{4}\).

And the machine repeats: each sliver of a parabola cut by a chord is the whole problem in miniature, so every stage's triangles carve exactly one-eighth out of their parent's leftover — doubling the count, quartering the total. Stage \(k\) contributes exactly \(T \cdot 4^{-(k-1)}\), and the covered sums are a geometric series in disguise:

\[\text{covered}_k \;=\; \bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \cdots + 4^{-(k-1)}\bigr)T \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T\,\bigl(1 - 4^{-k}\bigr).\]

The lower wall is free: every stage's triangles lie inside the cap, so \(\text{covered}_k \le \text{area}\) for every \(k\). The upper wall is the honest part, and it carries an honesty label. Sketch — the missing piece is the enclosure lemma: the leftover after stage \(k\) fits inside a region of area at most \(\tfrac{4}{3} \times\) the total of the stages after \(k\), a tangent-chord chase this book defers. Taken on credit, the lemma delivers the upper wall, and the algebra collapses beautifully:

\[\text{area} \;\le\; \text{covered}_k + \tfrac{4}{3}\,T \cdot 4^{-k} \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T\,\bigl(1 - 4^{-k}\bigr) + \tfrac{4}{3}\,T \cdot 4^{-k} \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T, \qquad \text{for every } k \text{ at once}.\]

The leftover bound and the series tail cancel exactly, and the trap snaps shut around a constant: \(\text{covered}_k \le \text{area} \le \tfrac{4}{3}T\), for every \(k\). The walls do not touch at any finite stage — the lower one rises forever, the upper one never moves — and yet the prisoner has nowhere to stand: §4's third example names the principle that forces \(\text{area} = \tfrac{4}{3}T\) from exactly this shape. (Archimedes, who had no ε, closed it his own way — twice: once by Eudoxus' double contradiction, once by summing the geometric series directly. His Quadrature of the Parabola runs twenty-four propositions to this one number.)

The class-12 floor can check the constant: \(\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^2)\,dx = \bigl[x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\bigr]_{-1}^{1} = \tfrac{4}{3}\) — the trap and the integral agree, two millennia apart. The check is a courtesy; the trap above is the proof.
