Example 3 · The necklace — characters as frequencies

Moves used: P24 the wave ledger, taken literally · P05 the canonical form a rotation census computes · P25 three engines, cross-checked.

The cube's group was rich; now take the poorest group in mathematics and watch the ledger become something you have already met. A binary necklace is a ring of \(n\) beads, each black or white, counted up to rotation — the oldest symmetry-counting problem there is, and P08's average already owns the formula. The rotation group \(C_n\) is abelian: every element is its own conjugacy class, so the character table is a square, \(n \times n\), and every irreducible wave is one-dimensional. Which one-dimensional waves can a rotation own? The waves of P24: \(n\) beads sampled around the circle is a discrete signal, and \(C_n\)'s characters are exactly its Fourier frequencies. For \(n = 4\):

\[\chi_0 = (1,1,1,1), \quad \chi_1 = (1, i, -1, -i), \quad \chi_2 = (1,-1,1,-1), \quad \chi_3 = (1,-i,-1,i),\]

each evaluated at the four rotations \(0, 90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ\): the constant wave, once-around, twice-around, and thrice-around. The orthogonality of P24 — different frequencies share nothing — is the character orthogonality, and the machine verifies it entry by entry: \(\langle \chi_j, \chi_k \rangle = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{k} \chi_j(k)\overline{\chi_k(k)} = 1\) if \(j = k\) and \(0\) otherwise, real part and imaginary part both checked.

Count necklaces with the ledger. The paint character is cycles-to-the-\(k\) as always: the identity rotation has 4 cycles on the bead positions (\(2^4 = 16\) fixed colorings), the \(90^\circ\) and \(270^\circ\) rotations have 1 cycle each (\(2\) each), the \(180^\circ\) rotation has 2 (\(4\)). The inner product with the constant wave gives the necklace count, and the inner products with the other three waves give the split:

\[\langle \chi_{\mathrm{paint}}, \chi_0 \rangle = \frac{16+2+4+2}{4} = 6, \qquad \langle \chi_{\mathrm{paint}}, \chi_j \rangle = (3, 4, 3) \text{ for } j = 1, 2, 3,\]

so the 16-dimensional space of 4-bead colorings splits as \(6 \cdot \chi_0 \oplus 3 \cdot \chi_1 \oplus 4 \cdot \chi_2 \oplus 3 \cdot \chi_3\), with the Parseval gate \(6^2 + 3^2 + 4^2 + 3^2 = 70 = \langle \chi_{\mathrm{paint}}, \chi_{\mathrm{paint}} \rangle = \frac{256 + 4 + 16 + 4}{4}\). The census agrees on the headline: the six binary necklaces of length 4 are \(0000, 0001, 0011, 0101, 0111, 1111\) — canonical forms computed by rotation-minimization, P05's discipline — and the 6 in the split is the multiplicity of the constant wave: orbit count again, in costume.

General \(n\) condenses the whole method into one formula. The rotation by \(j\) positions has \(\gcd(j, n)\) cycles, so the paint character at that rotation is \(2^{\gcd(j,n)}\), and averaging over the group:

\[N(n) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} 2^{\gcd(j,n)} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \,\mid\, n} \varphi(d)\, 2^{\,n/d},\]

the second form collecting equal \(\gcd\)'s by Euler's totient \(\varphi\) — the bridge back to B01 and B02. Three independent engines agree for every \(n\) from 1 to 12 — orbit census by canonical form, the \(\gcd\)-sum, the totient sum — on the sequence

\[2,\; 3,\; 4,\; 6,\; 8,\; 14,\; 20,\; 36,\; 60,\; 108,\; 188,\; 352 \qquad (n = 1, \ldots, 12).\]

And the wave reading of the formula is the honest payoff: \(N(n)\) is the constant wave's share of the paint space, and the entire split — every wave's multiplicity, every answer to "how many colorings behave like this under rotation" — comes off the same one-line table. This is P24's lesson at graduate scale: split into waves, and the counting takes care of itself. What the necklace gains in transparency it loses in reach, though: the moment the group stops being abelian — reflections allowed, Exercise 5 — the one-dimensional waves end and the two-dimensional waves begin, and the table stops being a square. That is not a defect; it is the exact place where character theory starts earning its keep over naive Fourier thinking, and the cube — non-abelian, five classes, three wave-sizes at once — already showed it working.
