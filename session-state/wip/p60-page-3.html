4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — the integers from the counting numbers, built two ways. The ledger's route is now familiar: pairs \((a,b)\), the law \(a + d = b + c\), and every class settling into exactly one normal form — \([(n, 0)]\) for net \(n \geq 0\), \([(0, n)]\) for net \(-n\) — with no page in both columns at once. School's route is different in costume and identical in spine: adjoin a formal inverse, write reduced words like \(3 - 5 + 2\), and simplify by deleting \(n + (-n)\) pairs. Each route claims to have built the integers, and the completion's answer to the quarrel is that there is nothing to quarrel about: both worlds do the same job — receive the counting numbers' addition, answer every difference-question, generate no answers beyond what the job forces — and any two groups doing that job are the same group up to a unique translation. The translation itself is forced: the word \(3 - 5\) must go where it must go, \(\eta(3) + \eta(5)^{-1} = [(3,0)] + [(0,5)] = [(3,5)]\), which is the position of \((0,2)\) since \(3 + 2 = 5 + 0\). Every word meets its page this way, and the two routes close over each other: one construction, two costumes, zero choices.

Encounter 2 — the rationals from the positive whole numbers. Multiplication gives the positive whole numbers a second monoid structure — associative, identity \(1\), and no inverses: one-third is not a whole number, and the monoid cannot hear "what times \(3\) gives \(5\)". The completion answers with pages read as fractions: \([(a,b)]\) is \(a/b\), the law is cross-multiplication \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\) — the same ledger, with the columns multiplied now — and the question is answered by \([(5,3)]\), since \([(5,3)] \cdot [(3,1)] = [(15,3)]\) and \((15,3)\) is the position of \((5,1)\) because \(15 \cdot 1 = 3 \cdot 5\). P16 explains why this completion feels so orderly: unique factorization says every positive whole number is a product of prime powers with counting-number exponents, so the monoid is a free commutative monoid on the primes, and completing it just lets the exponents go negative — \(18/12 = (2 \cdot 3^2)/(2^2 \cdot 3) = 2^{-1} \cdot 3 = 3/2\), one little ledger per prime. The positive rationals are the free abelian group on the primes; the whole numbers sit inside as the classes \([(n,1)]\); and the zero of multiplication is banned from the start — an absorbing element, which §5 shows would collapse the whole construction.

Encounter 3 — the two-point world of virtual bundles. Return to the bundles over \(X = \{p, q\}\): isomorphism classes are dimension pairs \((r_p, r_q)\), direct sum adds componentwise, and the monoid \(\mathbb{N}^2\) cancels — \(a + k = b + k\) componentwise forces \(a = b\) — so the completion embeds it, and \(K_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2\): one integer's worth of freedom per point. In the completed world the question "which bundle \(E\) satisfies \(E \oplus (2, 0) \cong (1, 2)\)?" — silent among actual bundles — has the answer \([(1,2)] - [(2,0)]\), a virtual bundle of dimension vector \((-1, 2)\), an object no actual bundle instantiates but every additive invariant must price correctly. The pricing is the whole point: "dimension at \(p\)" and "dimension at \(q\)" are additive invariants, so they are forced to extend to the virtual world as the two projections \(\mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}\) — and they extend uniquely, which is to say the extension was never in anyone's gift to design.

Three completions — \(\mathbb{Z}\) from the ledger, the positive rationals from the primes, \(K_0\) of a two-point space from the bundles — and one job description behind all of them. The pattern box said it as a construction; the picture says it as a demand: whoever builds a group that receives the monoid honestly and answers its difference-questions must accept that every other additive invariant passes through their group, uniquely. That demand is what makes the completed world not a convenience but a coordinate system: the differences were formal, but the invariant they assemble into is as real as any invariant ever gets — measured by what it forces of every other measurement.
