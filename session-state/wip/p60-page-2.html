3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P60 — Group completion. Three steps, each a construction with a universality proof for its verdict. (1) Name the monoid. Say what adds and what the zero is: counting numbers under addition, pages of a ledger, isomorphism classes of bundles under direct sum, finite sets under disjoint union, positive whole numbers under multiplication. Then ask the only diagnostic question: do the questions you need answered involve differences — formal subtraction, negative coefficients, "what remains when this cancels" — that the monoid cannot hear? (2) Complete. Build the pairs \(M \times M\), read \((a, b)\) as "\(a\) minus \(b\)", and quotient by the ledger's law, patched to survive monoids that do not cancel: \((a, b) \sim (c, d)\) exactly when some witness \(k \in M\) satisfies \(a + d + k = b + c + k\). Classes add columnwise, the zero is \([(0,0)]\), the inverse of \([(a,b)]\) is \([(b,a)]\), and the monoid enters through \(\eta(a) = [(a,0)]\). (3) Factor everything. Call an invariant additive when it is a monoid map from \(M\) into a group — and then it is not yours to design: every additive invariant \(f : M \to G\) factors uniquely through the completion, \(f = \bar{f} \circ \eta\). The completion is the universal invariant: the one all others descend from.

Or, in words: don't hunt for the differences — build the world where differences are native, and force that world to be the most economical one there is. "Most economical" is not praise; it is the content of the universality proof: the completion receives the monoid's addition and answers its difference-questions, and nothing else survives — any group that receives the addition honestly must accept the completion's answers wholesale, uniquely, with no room for invention. The move's strength is what it refuses to promise: if the monoid does not cancel, the completion forgets — the map \(\eta\) can merge distinct objects, and §5's max monoid merges everything — and the completion is then a quotient of hopes rather than a container of facts. What the completion always delivers, honestly, is the algebra of differences: the questions the monoid could not hear, answered in the one world where every additive invariant gets the same answer.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the objects must genuinely add. One associative operation, one zero, and the questions must be difference-questions about it. Jam-mode: two operations at once. A ring carries addition and multiplication, and they interact: adjoining additive inverses turns the counting numbers into the integers, but adjoining multiplicative inverses while respecting addition — the rationals from the integers — is a sibling construction with its own universal property, not this page's machine run twice. The ◆ exercise below walks that diagnosis, and the honest answer is that the two jobs are different.

Key 2 — the relations must be the honest ones. If the questions come from direct sums — "what is this object minus that object" — Box P60.A's relation is the right one. If they come from exact sequences — "what remains after cancellation along a sub-object" — the honest completion quotients further, imposing \([X] = [Y] + [X/Y]\)-style relations, and the two completions agree only in well-behaved worlds (the resolution theorem, named here and proved nowhere on this page). Jam-mode: completing with direct-sum relations when the invariants you care about are only exact-sequence-additive silently hands you the wrong universal object — too big, carrying classes that should have merged.

Key 3 — cancellation decides the price. The natural map \(\eta\) embeds \(M\) into its completion exactly when \(M\) cancels — \(a + k = b + k\) forcing \(a = b\). Without cancellation, \(\eta\) merges, and the merge is information destroyed: measured in §5, not mourned. Jam-mode: the max monoid of §5, where \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) while \(2 \neq 3\), and the completion — as universality demands — is the one-element group.

Box P60.A — the construction, the witness, and the universality proof. The naive version of the ledger's law — declare \((a, b)\) and \((c, d)\) the same when \(a + d = b + c\) — is honest on the counting numbers but a lie on general monoids: it need not even be an equivalence relation. On the max monoid \((\mathbb{N}, \oplus)\), where \(a \oplus b = \max(a, b)\), the naive law holds between \((2,3)\) and \((3,3)\) — \(\max(2, 3) = 3 = \max(3, 3)\) — and between \((3,3)\) and \((3,2)\), but fails between \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\) — \(\max(2,2) = 2\) against \(\max(3,3) = 3\) — so "same position" would not be transitive, and no quotient could be built. The patch is the witness \(k\): declare \((a, b) \sim (c, d)\) when some \(k \in M\) satisfies \(a + d + k = b + c + k\). On the counting numbers the witness changes nothing (cancellation removes it); on the max monoid it repairs transitivity — \((2,3) \sim (3,2)\) via \(k = 3\), since \(\max(2, 2, 3) = 3 = \max(3, 3, 3)\) — and the repair is honest about its price: the max monoid's completion collapses (§5). The verification that \(\sim\) is an equivalence is two lines: reflexivity and symmetry take \(k = 0\); for transitivity, from \(a + d + k_1 = b + c + k_1\) and \(c + f + k_2 = d + e + k_2\), add the second equation's sides into the first and the common witness \(K = d + k_1 + k_2\) appears, giving \(a + f + K = b + e + K\). Addition of classes is well-defined by the same move: if \((a, b) \sim (a', b')\) with witness \(k\), then adding \(c + d\) to the witness equation shows \((a + c, b + d) \sim (a' + c, b' + d)\).

The classes now form a group: \([(a,b)] + [(c,d)] = [(a+c, b+d)]\) is associative and commutative because \(M\)'s addition is; the zero is \([(0,0)]\); and the inverse of \([(a,b)]\) is \([(b,a)]\), since \((a,b) + (b,a) = (a + b, a + b) \sim (0,0)\) with witness \(0\). The monoid enters by \(\eta(a) = [(a,0)]\), and \(\eta(a + b) = [(a + b, 0)] = [(a,0)] + [(b,0)] = \eta(a) + \eta(b)\): the completion receives the addition. Universality is where the group's own lawfulness pays for the monoid's lawlessness: given any monoid map \(f : M \to G\) into a group, define \(\bar{f}([(a,b)]) = f(a) \cdot f(b)^{-1}\). This is well-defined precisely because the witness can be consumed where cancellation is lawful — from \(a + d + k = b + c + k\), applying \(f\) gives \(f(a) f(d) f(k) = f(b) f(c) f(k)\), and the group cancels \(f(k)\), leaving \(f(a) f(b)^{-1} = f(c) f(d)^{-1}\). It is a homomorphism because \(G\)'s image of a commutative monoid is commutative; it satisfies \(\bar{f} \circ \eta = f\) by definition; and it is the only such map, because any homomorphism \(g\) with \(g \circ \eta = f\) must send \([(a,b)] = [(a,0)] + [(0,b)]\) to \(f(a) \cdot f(b)^{-1}\), which is \(\bar{f}\)'s value, so \(g = \bar{f}\). One existence proof, one uniqueness proof: the completion does its job, and nothing else can be talked into doing it — two groups with this job are the same group up to a unique isomorphism, which is P31's argument, now a construction.

Box P60.B — where the move appears. K-theory: Grothendieck's \(K_0\), born in the 1957 work on Riemann–Roch that Borel and Serre wrote up and published in 1958, with its \(K\) from the German Klasse; for a field \(k\), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\), the dimension invariant factored through; for the ring of integers of a number field, the same construction refines the classical class group. Geometry: the Euler characteristic \(\chi\) is additive over disjoint unions and multiplicative over products — \(\chi(\mathrm{pt}) = 1\), \(\chi(S^1) = 0\), \(\chi(S^2) = 2\), \(\chi(S^2 \times S^2) = 4 = 2 \cdot 2\) — and an additive invariant with a group target factors through the completion of the disjoint-union monoid of finite complexes. Stable homotopy: the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen theorem identifies \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+}\) — the group completion of the monoid built from the symmetric groups' classifying spaces — with \(\Omega^\infty S^\infty\), so the stable homotopy groups of spheres are a group completion; the general engine behind such identifications, the group-completion theorem of McDuff and Segal (1976), is quoted here as consistent with the literature, not source-verified. The failure door: monoids that do not cancel — §5's exhibits — where the same construction, run honestly, hands back the one-element group and a lesson about listening for silence.
