New word: fixed point. A fixed point of a map \(g\) is a spot the map leaves alone: \(g(x^*) = x^*\). Feed it in, get it back. It is where an orbit could settle — and, for a contraction, the only place an orbit ever does. New word: orbit. An orbit is the record a repetition leaves: start anywhere, \(x_0\), and apply the map forever, \(x_1 = g(x_0)\), \(x_2 = g(x_1)\), endlessly. The orbit is this page's endless record, and the whole question is where it crowds — this page's answer: not merely where, but provably there, provably alone, and provably by hit \(n\). New word: contraction. A map is a contraction on a room if it pulls any two of the room's points toward each other by at least a fixed factor: \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le c\,|x - y|\) for one constant \(c < 1\), every pair, no exceptions. The pull is the hypothesis that turns repetition into certainty; §5 shows, one exhibit per clause, what each part of it is worth.

1 · Before we begin

The tier-2 ladder so far, one breath: P18 lifted a stuck problem into a room with one more dimension and descended the answer, P19 proved things exist by showing a random pick finds them, P20 certified identities by testing at enough points instead of expanding, and P21 caught the crowd an endless record forms in a bounded room. Four moves, four infinities tamed by finite work — and notice what P21's machine deliberately never promised: which spot, whether only one, and what it costs to get close. This page buys exactly those three promises, and pays for all three with one hypothesis. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P21, because an orbit is an endless record of hits in a room and the whole apparatus applies — this page is P21's engine with a turbocharger bolted on, and every part of §3's certificate is a P21 part wearing a new label. P15, the prerequisite, because its ε-trap is where digits get certified, and because P15's own fourth section ran this page's first example as a contrast — errors 0.414, 0.0858, 0.00245, \(2.1 \times 10^{-6}\), \(1.6 \times 10^{-12}\), doubling per step against bisection's steady 0.301 — with the receipt deferred to exactly this page. And P03, because the pull's fine print is where all the danger lives, and fine print is attacked, not skimmed: the §5 exhibits are hostile maps, and each one kills a version of the move that forgot to check a clause.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Repetition is the oldest computing machine there is — average, again, again — and it is the one machine on this ladder that is genuinely endless: the recipe never halts, never checks a stopping rule, certifies nothing on its own. A card of hits can suggest settling; nothing in the card can prove it, and P02's discipline — stop at the data — is precisely the discipline a forever-rule cannot follow. What would a guarantee even look like? Three sentences: the hits settle somewhere (existence); they settle at one spot only (uniqueness); and the distance from hit \(n\) to the settled spot is at most \(c^n\) times the starting error (price). The good news: all three follow from one hypothesis — the pull, \(c < 1\), checked once — by arguments this short and this old: nested rooms, one line of algebra, a geometric series. The bad news, paid up front: the pull is a license with three separate clauses — the map returns into the room, the constant is strictly below one, the room is closed — and each clause has its own wreckage in §5, run in public, not asserted. And one honest footnote the whole book keeps having to write: the receipt prices the floor, not the penthouse. Runs typically beat their certificates; §4 measures the gap three times; the lesson is P20's — read what the receipt licenses, never more.
