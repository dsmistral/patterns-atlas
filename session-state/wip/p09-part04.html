4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, three domains, two poses of the move: the wall that blocks, and the value that pins.

Example 1 (algebra) — the board that cannot surprise you

The board holds \(2\), \(3\), \(5\). The move: erase any two entries \(a\) and \(b\), write \(a + b + ab\). Two merges later one entry remains. What is it? Route one: merge \(2, 3\) first — \(2 + 3 + 6 = 11\) — then \(11 + 5 + 55 = 71\). Route two: merge \(3, 5\) first — \(3 + 5 + 15 = 23\) — then \(23 + 2 + 46 = 71\). The third route gives \(71\) as well. Coincidence, or forced?

Add one. \(c + 1 = a + b + ab + 1 = (a + 1)(b + 1)\): on the shifted numbers, the merge move is plain multiplication. So the product of (entry \(+\,1\)) over the whole board is conserved:

\[(2+1)(3+1)(5+1) = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 = 72 \quad \text{at every moment, on every route}.\]

When a single entry \(N\) remains, its shifted value is the product: \(N + 1 = 72\), so \(N = 71\) — not on the three routes we tried, on every route, past and future. That is the move's second pose: not a wall that blocks a target, but a fixed value the process must land on. The shift itself is an old friend — adding \(1\) to every entry is P05's canonical dressing, the uniform in which the move's true face (multiplication) shows. And invariants are loyal to their move and nothing else: change the rule to \(a + b - ab\), and \(\prod (x+1)\) dies at once — \(2, 3 \to 2 + 3 - 6 = -1\), and the shifted product falls from \(12\) to \(0\). No borrowing between moves.

Moves used: P09 (the conserved product \(\prod (x+1)\), in its value-pinning pose), P05 (the \(+1\) shift that turns the merge into multiplication).

Example 2 (geometry) — the board that cannot be tiled

Remove two opposite corners from a chessboard — \(a1\) and \(h8\) — leaving \(62\) squares. A domino covers exactly two edge-adjacent squares. Can \(31\) dominoes tile the mutilated board?

Try it small (P02): a \(4 \times 4\) board loses its opposite corners \(a1\) and \(d4\), leaving \(14\) squares and \(7\) dominoes. The attempts jam — every one dies the same death — and the invariant below will explain why, at both sizes at once.

Colour the board as chess demands. A domino, covering two edge-adjacent squares, always covers one square of each colour: moving one square changes the row or the column by one, flipping the square's colour. So a tiled board carries equal colour counts — that is the invariant, and the \(4 \times 4\) attempts were dying on it before we looked. Now the census. The full board holds \(32\) and \(32\). The two removed corners, being opposite, are the same colour — \(a1\) sits at row \(1\), column \(1\) and \(h8\) at row \(8\), column \(8\), and both pair an odd with an odd or an even with an even, so both squares share one colour. The mutilated board holds \(30\) of that colour and \(32\) of the other. Not equal — so no tiling exists: \(31\) dominoes would cover \(31\) of each, and only \(30\) of the one exist.

Now check the check (P03's discipline): remove one square of each colour instead — say \(a1\) and \(h1\). The census reads \(31\) and \(31\); the objection vanishes; and a tiling genuinely exists: rows two through eight tile horizontally, four dominoes each, and row one's six survivors \(b1\) through \(g1\) tile horizontally in three pieces. When this invariant objects, nothing exists; when it stops objecting, this door at least is open. The art of choosing a colouring — there are many, and some see what others cannot — is a move of its own further up the ladder: P75.

Moves used: P09 (the colour census), P02 (the small board that showed the failure's shape), P03 (the control case that checks the check).

Example 3 (numbers) — the nine-point filter

Both B01 and B02 promised this page a piece of currency, so let us count it out: a number and its digit sum are congruent mod \(9\) — \(n \equiv\) the sum of its digits \(\pmod{9}\).

Why, in one line of B02: \(10 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}\), so every power \(10^k \equiv 1\), so \(n = d_k 10^k + \cdots + d_1 10 + d_0 \equiv d_k + \cdots + d_1 + d_0\) — the reduce-first rule applied to the powers. Check it on B02's own teaser numbers: \(47 \equiv 4 + 7 = 11 \equiv 2\), and \(2026 \equiv 2 + 0 + 2 + 6 = 10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9\).

As an invariant it catches wrong arithmetic. Multiply \(1234 \times 5678\). Digit sums: \(1234 \to 1+2+3+4 = 10 \to 1\), and \(5678 \to 5+6+7+8 = 26 \to 8\); so by B02's product rule the true product must sit in class \(1 \cdot 8 = 8 \pmod 9\). A candidate answer \(7{,}006{,}650\) has digit sum \(24 \to 6\): \(6 \neq 8\), so it is certainly wrong, whatever the multiplication looked like. The true product \(7{,}006{,}652\) has digit sum \(26 \to 8\) and passes.

The honest price, paid up front: the filter is a detector, not a proof. \(7{,}006{,}661\) is wrong too — it is the truth plus nine — and its digit sum is also \(26 \to 8\): it sails through. Failing the nines test convicts; passing it proves nothing, because adding \(9\) is invisible to the class. P03 said it first: a check that survives a wrong answer is a detector, and detectors are for catching, not certifying.

And the twin tie, which is why this page and P08 share the example: shuffle a number's digits — any shuffle, any order — and the digit sum does not move, because addition does not care about order. The shuffle is P08's symmetry move acting on the digits; the digit sum is exactly the thing it preserves; read mod \(9\), it is this page's invariant. B01's promise — a digit-sum rule that stays put mod \(9\) no matter how the digits are shuffled — paid in full: one quantity, two coats, two pages.

Moves used: P09 (the mod-\(9\) digit-sum invariant, in its error-catching pose), P05 (reduce each factor to its residue — the canonical representative), P08 (the digit shuffle, whose preserved quantity this is).
