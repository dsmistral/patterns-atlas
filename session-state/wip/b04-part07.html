Exercise 1 ●○○

B03's Exercise 1 reduced \(\frac{322}{1386}\) to \(\frac{23}{99}\) by the dance. Run the climb on the same pair (1386, 322): find whole numbers \(x, y\) with \(1386x + 322y = 14\). Then divide the identity by 14 and say what the result certifies about the reduced fraction.

Hint

The dance is four divisions; the climb is three substitutions. Divide the final identity through by the gcd — every coefficient stays whole.

Worked answer

Dance: \(1386 = 4 \times 322 + 98\), \(322 = 3 \times 98 + 28\), \(98 = 3 \times 28 + 14\), \(28 = 2 \times 14 + 0\). Climb: \(14 = 98 - 3 \times 28\); then \(28 = 322 - 3 \times 98\) gives \(14 = 98 - 3 \times (322 - 3 \times 98) = 10 \times 98 - 3 \times 322\); then \(98 = 1386 - 4 \times 322\) gives \(14 = 10 \times 1386 - 43 \times 322\). Check: \(13{,}860 - 13{,}846 = 14\). Divided by 14: \(10 \times 99 - 43 \times 23 = 1\) — check: \(990 - 989\) — a certificate that \(\gcd(99, 23) = 1\). B03's reduced fraction was in lowest terms "as far as the dance could see"; this is the same fact with its reason attached: the parts of a fully reduced fraction are genuinely coprime, and the certificate proves it without factoring 99 or 23.

Exercise 2 ●●○

(a) Run dance and climb on (31, 7) to find the inverse of the class of 7 mod 31. (b) Solve \(7x \equiv 12 \pmod{31}\). (c) Reconcile with B02's Exercise 4 census: mod 10, find 3's inverse by a two-division dance, and say why the climb beats the scan as moduli grow.

Hint

(a) The dance on (31, 7) is two divisions. (b) Multiply both sides by the inverse. (c) The scan tests classes one at a time; the climb's length grows with the number of digits, not the size — B03's halving engine.

Worked answer

(a) \(31 = 4 \times 7 + 3\), then \(7 = 2 \times 3 + 1\), then \(3 = 3 \times 1 + 0\). Climb: \(1 = 7 - 2 \times 3 = 7 - 2 \times (31 - 4 \times 7) = 9 \times 7 - 2 \times 31\) — check: \(63 - 62\). So the class of 7 is invertible mod 31, with inverse the class of 9 (already in census range). (b) Multiply through by 9: \(x \equiv 9 \times 12 = 108 \equiv 15 \pmod{31}\), since \(108 = 3 \times 31 + 15\). Check: \(7 \times 15 = 105 = 3 \times 31 + 12\). One class, as Corollary 4 counts: \(g = \gcd(7, 31) = 1\). (c) Mod 10: \(10 = 3 \times 3 + 1\), so \(1 = 10 - 3 \times 3\): the class of 3 has inverse \(-3\), dressed as 7 — matching the census row \(3 \times 7 = 21 \equiv 1\). The scan tests up to ten classes; the climb here was one division. For a 100-digit modulus the scan is hopeless, while the climb stays a few hundred divisions — the certificate route scales, the search route does not.

Exercise 3 ●●●

For positive whole \(a, b\): if \((x_0, y_0)\) is one solution of \(ax + by = c\), prove that every solution has the form \(x = x_0 + (b/g)t\), \(y = y_0 - (a/g)t\) for a whole number \(t\), where \(g = \gcd(a, b)\) — and that every pair of that form is a solution. Then apply: one solution of \(4x + 6y = 2\) is \((-1, 1)\); write the full family and check four members, including two with negative entries.

Hint

For one direction, add the family's extra terms and watch them cancel — both \(a \cdot (b/g)\) and \(b \cdot (a/g)\) equal \(ab/g\). For the other, subtract two solutions and apply Euclid's lemma (Corollary 2) after dividing out \(g\); remember \(\gcd(a/g, b/g) = 1\).

Worked answer

Every pair of the form works: \(a\left(x_0 + \frac{b}{g}t\right) + b\left(y_0 - \frac{a}{g}t\right) = ax_0 + by_0 + \frac{ab}{g}t - \frac{ab}{g}t = c\). The added terms cancel because both \(a \cdot \frac{b}{g}\) and \(b \cdot \frac{a}{g}\) equal \(\frac{ab}{g}\). Every solution has the form: let \((x_1, y_1)\) be any other solution and subtract the equations: \(a(x_0 - x_1) = -b(y_0 - y_1)\). Write \(a = ga'\), \(b = gb'\); then \(a'(x_0 - x_1) = -b'(y_0 - y_1)\), and \(\gcd(a', b') = 1\) — if some \(d > 1\) divided both \(a'\) and \(b'\), then \(gd\) would divide both \(a\) and \(b\), past the gcd \(g\), impossible. So \(b' \mid a'(x_0 - x_1)\) with \(\gcd(a', b') = 1\), and Euclid's lemma — this page's own Corollary 2 — gives \(b' \mid (x_0 - x_1)\): write \(x_0 - x_1 = b't\). Substituting back, \(a'b't = -b'(y_0 - y_1)\), so \(y_0 - y_1 = -a't\). Both differences match the family's form, with the same \(t\). Complete. Application: \(g = \gcd(4, 6) = 2\), base \((-1, 1)\) checks \((-4) + 6 = 2\), so the family is \(x = -1 + 3t\), \(y = 1 - 2t\). Members: \(t = 0\): \((-1, 1)\); \(t = 1\): \((2, -1)\), with \(8 - 6 = 2\); \(t = 2\): \((5, -3)\), with \(20 - 18 = 2\); \(t = -1\): \((-4, 3)\), with \(-16 + 18 = 2\). Negative entries are legal — an anti-stack is a stack reversed. A window check over all \(|x|, |y| \le 30\) finds 20 solutions; the family captures all 20, and nothing else in the window solves the equation.

Exercise 4 ◐

A post office sells stamps of ₹4 and ₹7 only, and requires exact postage. An amount is makeable if some combination \(4x + 7y\) hits it exactly. (a) Scan the amounts ₹1 to ₹25 and sort them into makeable and never-makeable. (b) Prove that every amount from ₹18 on is makeable. (c) The (4, 7) data suggests a conjecture: for stamp values \(a\) and \(b\) sharing no factor, the largest never-makeable amount is \(ab - a - b\). Test it on (3, 5) as well, then state honestly what has been proved and what has not.

What you should find

(a) Makeable up to 25: \(4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25\). Never-makeable: \(1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 13, 17\). (b) ₹18 \(= 4 + 2 \times 7\), ₹19 \(= 3 \times 4 + 7\), ₹20 \(= 5 \times 4\), ₹21 \(= 3 \times 7\) — and any makeable amount stays makeable when one more ₹4 stamp joins its combination. So the block 18, 19, 20, 21 covers every amount above it: from ₹22 on, keep adding ₹4 stamps to a base in the block. That is a forced-forever block — the same shape B03 used for its worst-case census, and the formal name for such repetition is induction (P66, named and deferred). (c) For (4, 7): \(4 \times 7 - 4 - 7 = 17\), the largest never-makeable, on the nose. For (3, 5): never-makeable \(1, 2, 4, 7\); largest 7; and \(3 \times 5 - 3 - 5 = 7\) — again on the nose, with every amount from ₹8 on makeable. Two confirmations, zero refutations: a conjecture standing on evidence. What has not been proved: the general claim for arbitrary stamp values sharing no factor. It is a real theorem with a real proof, harder than anything on this page — the scope box said so — and until it is proved somewhere, \(ab - a - b\) is a pattern, not a fact.
