1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Task one. List the multiples of 25 up to 100, and the multiples of 11 up to 99. Hunt for the closest pair across the two lists. How close can you get — and can you get down to 1?

Do it. The multiples of 25 are \(25, 50, 75, 100\); the multiples of 11 run \(11, 22, 33, \dots\) up to \(99\). Within the drawn range the two lists never meet — 25 and 11 share no factor — but they come within one unit of meeting: \(100\) and \(99\) (Fig B04.1). And one unit apart means one subtraction away from a statement about stacks:

\[4 \times 25 - 9 \times 11 \;=\; 100 - 99 \;=\; 1.\]

Neither 25 nor 11 divides 1 — no single kind of stack lands on it — and yet 1 is a combination of them: four stacks of 25, nine anti-stacks of 11, nothing else. The smallest positive thing these two numbers can build together is 1. That is the whole subject of this page, in pencil.

Task two. B03 closed its (2026, 1491) story with a brute force: \(2026 \times 1321 = 2{,}676{,}346\), and \(2{,}676{,}346 = 1795 \times 1491 + 1\), so 1321 is the inverse of the class of 2026 mod 1491. Check both claims with pencil multiplication — two of them. Then ask the question the brute force cannot answer: is 1321 an accident — a witness found by luck — or was it sitting somewhere, waiting to be found? Task one says combinations of 25 and 11 can reach 1 without any luck at all. The quotients of the (2026, 1491) dance are \(1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 3, 1, 8\) — and section 3 runs that dance backwards to produce whole numbers \(x, y\) with \(2026x + 1491y = 1\). Reduce \(x\) into the class range and 1321 appears. Nothing accidental about it.

Notice what the two tasks share. Both found the number 1 as a combination — the first by hunting, the second (in section 3) by a mechanical climb back up B03's chain of divisions. The hunt does not scale: for bigger pairs, the near-miss lies too deep in the multiples to see. The climb does — it reuses the dance B03 already ran. We now name what the climb produces.
