Example 2 · A cut no one could guess — every graph splits nearly in half

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim), P07 (the auxiliary object: the random partition), P11 (the same disguise, now reading an average from above).

Pin the claim: every graph with \(m\) edges has a way to split its vertices into two sides with at least \(\frac{m}{2}\) edges crossing between the sides. Read as a subgraph statement: the crossing edges form a bipartite graph — all crossing edges go side-to-side — so every graph, however tangled, contains a bipartite subgraph holding at least half its edges. The claim is quiet and strong: no hypothesis on the graph, and a bipartite skeleton of at least half its weight always inside.

The room: all \(2^n\) ways to assign \(n\) vertices to two sides. The experiment: a fair coin per vertex. One edge crosses exactly when its two endpoints drew different sides — chance \(\frac{1}{2}\), and the two flips involved are the only ones the event reads. By linearity, the expected number of crossing edges is \(m \cdot \frac{1}{2}\) — and here the move runs backwards: not "the average is below \(1\), so something has none" but "the average is \(\frac{m}{2}\), so some partition has at least \(\frac{m}{2}\)" — an average of numbers that are all below \(\frac{m}{2}\) would itself be below \(\frac{m}{2}\). No independence was consulted: overlapping edges, shared vertices, none of it matters to a linear sum.

Take \(K_5\): \(m = 10\), guarantee \(5\) crossing edges. The truth is better — the best split is \(2 + 3\), crossing \(2 \cdot 3 = 6 = \lfloor \frac{25}{4} \rfloor\) edges, the complete-graph maximum. The guarantee of \(5\) is honest but leave-taking; here is the Figure:

Now the move's most instructive trick: the guarantee can be made deterministic. Walk the vertices one at a time, placing each on the side where it crosses at least half of its edges to the vertices already placed — a local greedy choice with no coin anywhere. Count what the walk earns: each placement secures at least half of that vertex's earlier edges, and every edge is an "earlier edge" of exactly one of its two endpoints (whichever comes later in the walk). Summed over the walk, the crossings secured are at least half of all \(m\) edges. This is derandomization — the method of conditional expectations — and it converts the existence of a good cut into an algorithm that builds one, in one pass, no memory of the room. On \(K_5\) the walk in vertex order \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) lands all \(6\) crossing edges, the maximum.

Price the luck honestly: the walk guarantees \(\frac{m}{2}\) and nothing more. The lab census runs the walk over all \(120\) vertex orders of \(K_5\) — every single one reaches the maximum \(6\) — and over all \(24\) orders of a four-vertex path, where \(6\) of the \(24\) land on \(2\), one short of the maximum \(3\). The guarantee is a theorem; the extra edge is luck, and luck is graph-dependent (\S8, Ex5).
