4 · The move at work

Example 1 · The craftsman's recipe — \(\sqrt{2}\) by averaging, certified

Moves used: P15 (the ε-trap: digits certified by walls, the currency of every error below), P21 (the nested rooms: the orbit's own gaps run the machine).

Name the room. Every sensible guess for \(\sqrt{2}\) lives in \([1, 2]\), and even a silly start walks in: one averaging step lands at or above \(\sqrt{2}\) wherever the start was positive, by the floor below. The map returns into the room, twice over. The floor: by AM–GM, \(g(x) = (x + 2/x)/2 \ge \sqrt{x \cdot (2/x)} = \sqrt{2} \ge 1\) — averaging never undershoots. The ceiling: \(g(x) - 3/2 = (x-1)(x-2)/(2x) \le 0\) on \([1,2]\), so \(g(x) \le 3/2\). Together: \(g([1,2]) \subseteq [\sqrt{2},\, 1.5]\), a strictly smaller room inside the first — the self-mapping clause, checked, not assumed.

Now the pull, and it is two lines of class-12 algebra. For any two guesses \(x, y\) in the room, \[ g(x) - g(y) \;=\; \frac{x - y}{2} + \frac{2/y - 2/x}{2} \;=\; (x - y)\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{xy}\right). \] On the room, \(xy \ge 1\), so \(0 \le 1/(xy) \le 1\) and the bracket sits between \(-1/2\) and \(1/2\): \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le \tfrac{1}{2}|x - y|\). The averaging map is a contraction with \(c = 1/2\), and Box P22.A's whole receipt applies: the card settles, at one spot, priced. The price, quoted in advance: \(e_n \le (1/2)^n e_0\) with \(e_0 = 0.4142135624\) — a guaranteed \(\log_{10} 2 = 0.301\) digits per hit, bisection's own rate, P15's currency. The receipt never lies, and from hit 3 on it is absurdly pessimistic — the run beats it by a factor that compounds to \(10^{22}\). The bonus has a name, and it is the same identity that answers §2's second question. Compute \(g(x) - \sqrt{2}\) directly: putting the two terms over the common denominator \(2x\) gives \[ g(x) - \sqrt{2} \;=\; \frac{x^2 - 2\sqrt{2}\,x + 2}{2x} \;=\; \frac{(x - \sqrt{2})^2}{2x}. \]

Three consequences, each read straight off the identity. Impostors are impossible: a settle point must satisfy \(g(x^*) = x^*\), and for this map that equation is \((x^*)^2 = 2\) — the positive solution is \(\sqrt{2}\) itself, so the catch is the true diagonal, not an impostor the recipe happens to like. One-sidedness: the identity is a square over a positive denominator, so every hit after the first overshoots — the card's errors are all overshoots from hit 1 on. The squaring: \(e_{n+1} = e_n^2/(2x_n)\) — errors square, then halve — so correct decimals go \(1, 2, 5, 11, 24\), doubling per hit. The receipt priced the whole room's worst pair; the run rides the flattening at the answer, where the pull factor is \(g'(\sqrt{2}) = 0\) — flat, the map's one tangent-horizontal spot, sitting exactly at the catch. That is where the doubling comes from: a floor you can quote before running is worth more than a sharp guess you cannot, and the doubling is the map paying its own bonus.

And the card is exact, a fact this page will spend twice. Averaging a fraction gives a fraction: from \(x_0 = 1\), every hit is a ratio of whole numbers — the card read as a chain of fractions,

The residual pins the error exactly: \(a/b - \sqrt{2} = (a^2 - 2b^2)/(b^2(a/b + \sqrt{2}))\), so hit 4 is within \(1.6 \times 10^{-12}\) of \(\sqrt{2}\) — eleven decimals, in integers — hit 5 within \(9 \times 10^{-25}\) — twenty-four decimals — and the pattern predicts level 6 at forty-eight, a 25-digit pair verified in the lab by whole-number arithmetic, no floating point anywhere. The doubling, in exact fractions, forever.

Two prices, paid out loud. First: the receipt quotes 0.301 digits per hit and the run delivers doubling — the gap is real, explained, and never promised; a receipt that promised the doubling would be a receipt the map has not signed. Second: the catch comes with no closed form — the recipe is the formula for \(\sqrt{2}\)'s digits, and P15's walls are still needed to certify each digit the recipe delivers; this page guarantees the pipeline, P15 certifies the product.
