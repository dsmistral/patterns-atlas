Exhibit C · The missing tread — the record that is not continuous. The extreme value theorem's third clause, failing with everything else in order. Define \(g\) on the closed bounded room \([0,1]\): \(g(x) = x\) for \(x < 1/2\), and \(g(x) = 0\) from \(1/2\) on. Bounded: yes, every value is at most \(1/2\). Closed room: yes. Door 1 of §4's proof even works: the values have a least ceiling, \(M = 1/2\) — P15's walls can trap the value, and for every \(\delta > 0\) some \(x\) delivers \(1/2 - \delta\). But no \(x\) delivers \(1/2\): the function steps off the ladder at exactly one point. A million-step sweep of the room finds a best of \(0.499999\), at \(x = 0.499999\), and the pattern is a proof: no sweep will ever do better, because \(g(x) < 1/2\) everywhere. The supremum exists; the maximizer does not. What failed is continuity at the single point \(1/2\) — and Door 2 is exactly where the proof used it: the kept intervals shrink toward \(1/2\) while their ceilings stay at \(M\), and continuity is what would force \(g(1/2)\) up to meet them. Tear that one tread and the staircase is still a staircase, the ceiling is still a ceiling — and nothing attains it. The repair: continuous records only. The price, stated honestly: the extreme value theorem says nothing — nothing — about records with a torn tread, however bounded their room; the intermediate value theorem of §4's third example is in the same position, for the same reason.

Scope and price of P21

What the move gives. In any closed, bounded room, an endless record crowds: a limit point exists, and the halving machine locates it to any precision, one bit per level. Under continuity, three existence floors: maxima are attained, sign changes are crossed by roots, and the crowd can be read as a convergent subsequence — the family of traps that must close.

What it does not give, honestly. First, uniqueness is never promised: one record can crowd in many places — §4's card crowds at two — and which crowd a run catches is the tie-break's choice, not the theorem's. Second, the catch may be a point the record never hits: existence of a crowd is not membership in it. Third, the room's two clauses are separate licenses with separate wreckage (§5, Exhibits A and B), and the continuity clause guards only the maximizer forms (Exhibit C). Fourth, the engine spends the line's no-holes property — in either costume, the nested-interval catch or the least upper wall — and a treatment that hides this is selling a machine with an unpaid bill. Fifth, the grown-up generalization, compactness — the word for closed-and-bounded above the line, and its covering sibling — is a far door (P54), signposted here and built there. And one honest footnote for the curious: a record is a countable swarm in an uncountable room — P14 proved the room's uncountability — and the machine needs nothing more than the swarm's countability to run.
