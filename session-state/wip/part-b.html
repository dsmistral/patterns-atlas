2 · Meet the problem

Two friends paint cubes. Each face gets one of two colors — call them black and white — and the game is to make cubes that are genuinely different: two paint jobs count as the same cube if some rotation turns one into the other. How many genuinely different cubes are there?

Count naively and the answer is \(2^6 = 64\): six faces, two choices each. But rotate: a cube painted with five white faces and one black face is the same cube no matter which face is black, because a quarter-turn carries any face to any other. The 64 is an overcount, and symmetry is the culprit — the exact situation P05 was named for. The machine settles the census question first, P25-style: enumerate all 64 colorings, generate all 24 rotations of the cube as matrices, apply every rotation to every coloring, and count the orbits — the equivalence classes under "some rotation carries one to the other". The answer is 10. Ten genuinely different black-and-white cubes, out of 64 paint jobs. The full census is cheap at this size, and it is the receipt every shortcut below must reproduce.

But the census is a sledgehammer, and it scales terribly — paint with three colors and the pool is \(3^6 = 729\); with six colors, \(6^6 = 46{,}656\). The classical shortcut notices that the 24 rotations come in five breeds, classified by how they permute the six faces:

A rotation can only fix a coloring that is constant on each of its cycles — every face in a cycle repaints together — so the number of colorings a rotation freezes is \(k\) raised to its cycle count, for \(k\) colors. For two colors the five breeds freeze \(64, 8, 16, 4, 8\) colorings respectively, and the honest average of P08 — one face of it — gives the orbit count:

\[\frac{1}{24}\left(1 \cdot 64 + 6 \cdot 8 + 3 \cdot 16 + 8 \cdot 4 + 6 \cdot 8\right) = \frac{64 + 48 + 48 + 32 + 48}{24} = \frac{240}{24} = 10.\]

The average is not a trick; it is bookkeeping. Each orbit of size \(s\) contributes \(|G|/s \cdot s = |G|\) to the sum \(\sum_g \mathrm{fix}(g)\), because a coloring with stabilizer of size \(24/s\) is fixed by exactly those \(24/s\) rotations — the orbit-stabilizer theorem, the same fact that makes P08's average exact rather than approximate. One color: \((1 + 6 + 3 + 8 + 6)\) weighted properly gives \(1\). Three colors: \(\frac{729 + 6 \cdot 27 + 3 \cdot 81 + 8 \cdot 9 + 6 \cdot 27}{24} = \frac{1368}{24} = 57\), and the census of all 729 agrees. Six colors: the machine formula says \(\frac{6^6 + 3 \cdot 6^4 + 12 \cdot 6^3 + 8 \cdot 6^2}{24} = 2226\) — far past brute-force patience, arrived at in one line.

So the shortcut works, and it is faster. But watch what it discards. Ask the sledgehammer's question again, sharpened: of the ten cubes, how many have exactly one black face? The average as computed is silent — it counted orbits, all of them, and kept nothing else. Ask how does the space of all colorings decompose under the rotation group — into what symmetry waves, with what amplitudes — and the average cannot even hear the question. Yet both answers exist, both are computable from the same five breeds of rotation, and — this is the discovery the page is named for — both fall out of one small table, if you keep the counts the average threw away and arrange them properly. The census gave us the raw counts. The next section builds the table.
