2 · Meet the problem

The oldest unresolved question in mathematics was a postulate. Euclid's fifth — in the form John Playfair standardized in his 1795 Elements edition, the form this page uses for \\(S\\) — says: through a point not on a line, exactly one line runs parallel to it, meeting it never. Proclus, in the fifth century, already recorded the discomfort: the postulate reads like a theorem awaiting its proof, and for two thousand years geometers tried to give it one — by assuming the rival and hunting for the contradiction, the exact shape of P11. Gerolamo Saccheri took the hunt furthest: his 1733 Euclides ab omni naevo vindicatus — Euclid freed of every flaw — proved theorem after theorem inside the rival geometry, each looking false and none contradictory, and concluded, wrongly, that the rival was "repugnant to the nature of the straight line". Johann Lambert got closer in his 1766 Theorie der Parallellinien, published only after his death: he found that the rival geometry's triangles fall short of 180° by an amount that behaves like an area, and he refused to publish, because he could not find the contradiction and would not say there was none. The question was never "are the theorems pretty" — it was "does a contradiction exist", an ocean-sized universal negative about proofs.

Then the creators stopped asking. Nikolai Lobachevsky published "O nachalakh geometrii" — On the principles of geometry — in the Kazanskii vestnik across 1829–1830, building the rival geometry with full deliberateness. János Bolyai, twenty-one, wrote to his father in late 1823: aus dem Nichts habe ich eine neue, andere Welt erschaffen — out of nothing I have created a strange new world. Gauss had the same world in his private notebooks and advised silence. But none of the three owned a certificate that the new world could exist: their tables of theorems were evidence, not proof, and the old scandal stood — maybe the rival was inconsistent, and Euclid's postulate was a theorem whose proof was merely difficult. What was missing was the observation that reframes everything: you do not certify a world by exploring it. You certify it by building it inside a geometry you already trust, and then the two-worlds argument runs: if the trusted world is consistent, so is the strange one, and no proof from the shared axioms can ever choose between them.

Eugenio Beltrami made that observation rigorous in 1868, with a construction — the Saggio di interpretazione della geometria non-euclidea — that built the rival geometry inside ordinary Euclidean geometry, its points living in a region of the plane, its lines in curves, so that "the parallel postulate fails" became an ordinary, consistent fact about a Euclidean object. Felix Klein recast the construction in projective clothes (1871, in the Mathematische Annalen); Henri Poincaré made it famous with the disk of Example 3 (1882, Théorie des groupes fuchsiens, Acta Mathematica 1:1–62). The definition this history distilled, and this page uses:

Definition (independence). A statement \\(S\\) is independent of an axiom list \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) when \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash S\\) and \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash \\neg S\\): no proof from the axioms ends at \\(S\\), and none ends at its negation. The witness pair that certifies it: two worlds — structures interpreting the language of \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) — with \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash \\mathcal{A}\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash S\\), \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\mathcal{A}\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\neg S\\). Then no proof of \\(S\\) from \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) can exist — a proof would hold in \\(\\mathcal W_2\\), which obeys the axioms and denies \\(S\\) — and symmetrically none of \\(\\neg S\\). One direction of the bridge (two worlds ⇒ no proof) is the soundness of the proof rules, an induction over proof length; the converse (no proof ⇒ worlds exist) is Gödel's completeness theorem, P34's companion and Exhibit C's subject.

Three questions, one device. First: what does it take to certify a world — how do you check that a strange structure obeys the axioms, when "obeying" is exactly what is in doubt? The answer below is P25's: obedience is checked instance by instance, and when the world is finite — or the axiom instances form a finite pool — the check is a census. Second: how small can the worlds be? The smallest separating pair below has three elements each, and the machine that finds them is the same one that proves smaller pairs cannot exist. Third: what survives when the axiom list is infinite — arithmetic itself, where P34's \\(G\\) lives? There the worlds exist (they are called nonstandard models), but they cannot be census-certified, and the honest version of the argument is a construction of the literature. Each question gets a receipt below: rival monoid tables, an ordered field, and a disk — two worlds apiece, all machine-checked, and the universal negative falls in each case.
