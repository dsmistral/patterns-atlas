Exercise 1 ●○○

(a) Compose \\((2, 3)\\) with the identity \\((1, 0)\\), both routes, and confirm the norm is untouched: \\(2^2 + 3^2 = 4 + 9 = 13\\). (b) Compose \\((1, 4)\\) with \\((2, 3)\\) — both routes — and confirm each output carries norm \\(221 = 17 \cdot 13\\).

Hint

The recipe is \\((a, b) \odot (c, d) = (ac - bd,\ ad + bc)\\); the second route swaps the sign: \\((ac + bd,\ ad - bc)\\). With \\(c = 1, d = 0\\) every product term containing \\(d\\) dies.

Worked answer

(a) \\((2,3) \odot (1,0) = (2 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 1) = (2, 3)\\), and the sign-flipped route gives \\((2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot 1) = (2, -3)\\) — same object up to the free sign, norm \\(4 + 9 = 13\\) both times. (b) Route one: \\((1 \cdot 2 - 4 \cdot 3,\ 1 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 2) = (-10, 11)\\), norm \\(100 + 121 = 221\\). Route two: \\((1 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3,\ 1 \cdot 3 - 4 \cdot 2) = (14, -5)\\), norm \\(196 + 25 = 221\\). Two witnesses, one composition — B12's Lecture 0, reproduced as a warm-up. The lab re-runs both parts exactly.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Compose a general pair \\((a, b)\\) with \\((1, 1)\\). Show that the norm of the output is exactly twice the norm of the input, and check the instance \\((3, 4) \odot (1, 1) = (-1, 7)\\): \\(50 = 1 + 49\\). Then answer precisely: why is \\((1, 1)\\) not an identity — and what invariant value must a true identity always carry?

Hint

Expand \\((a-b)^2 + (a+b)^2\\) and watch the cross terms cancel. For the last question: if \\(e\\) is an identity and invariants multiply, compute \\(I(e) \cdot I(z)\\) two ways.

Worked answer

\\((a,b) \odot (1,1) = (a - b,\ a + b)\\), so the output norm is \\((a-b)^2 + (a+b)^2 = 2a^2 + 2b^2 = 2(a^2+b^2)\\) — the doubling identity, discovered rather than memorized. Instance: \\((3,4) \odot (1,1) = (-1, 7)\\), norm \\(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\\). Why not an identity: an identity must return the object, and \\((a-b, a+b) \ne (a,b)\\) (try \\((3,4)\\): you got \\((-1,7)\\)). The invariant value: \\(I(e)I(z) = I(e \odot z) = I(z)\\) for every \\(z\\), so \\(I(e) = 1\\) whenever some object has \\(I(z) \ne 0\\) — a true identity always carries invariant 1, and \\(N(1,1) = 2\\) confesses immediately. The lab's 169-pair identity sweep is this demand running on \\((1,0)\\).

Exercise 3 ●●●

Find every representation of \\(1025\\) as a sum of two squares \\(x^2 + y^2\\) with \\(0 \le x \le y\\), prove your list is complete, and then produce each representation by composition routes from \\(1025 = 5^2 \cdot 41\\) (Example 1 sketches the route; your job is to carry it out and to reconcile it with the census).

Hint

Completeness first: \\(y \le 32\\) (why?), so the census is 32 checks of "is \\(1025 - y^2\\) a square?". Then build \\(5^2\\) as \\((1,2) \odot (1,2)\\) — both routes — and compose each route-output with \\((4, 5)\\), the representation of 41.

Worked answer

Census: for \\(y\\) from 1 to 32, the hits are \\(1025 - 32^2 = 1\\), \\(1025 - 31^2 = 64\\), \\(1025 - 25^2 = 400\\) — and nothing else (every other gap misses squarehood; the lab scans all pairs up to 33 and confirms). So exactly three: \\((1, 32)\\), \\((8, 31)\\), \\((20, 25)\\). Routes: \\((1,2) \odot (1,2)\\) gives \\((-3, 4)\\) and \\((5, 0)\\), both norm 25 — the second trivial, parked on the axis. Compose with \\((4,5)\\) (norm 41): from \\((3,4)\\), routes \\((-8, 31)\\) and \\((32, -1)\\); from \\((5,0)\\), both routes \\((20, 25)\\). Every census hit is route-produced, including the one that needed the trivial rep — and the fact that the trivial rep was forced into the census is exactly why composition beats hunting: it never forgets a witness.

Exercise 4 ◆

A student is asked to simplify \\(\tfrac{1}{2 + \sqrt{3}}\\), and writes: multiply numerator and denominator by \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\), giving \\(\tfrac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{(2+\sqrt{3})(2-\sqrt{3})} = \tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{1} = 2 - \sqrt{3}\\). Diagnose the move: name the pattern (or patterns) at work, and say what the fraction \\(\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{2-\sqrt{3}}\\) really is.

Worked answer

The fraction \\(\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{2-\sqrt{3}}\\) is the multiplicative identity \\(1\\), wearing a chosen shape — and multiplying by it is P06, compose with identity, in its purest form: the composition changes nothing (legality, Box P06.A demand 1) while the chosen shape of the identity does all the work, moving the object into the canonical form \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\) with a rational denominator (P05's uniform). The conjugate \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\) is not decoration: it is the shape whose composition with \\(2 + \sqrt{3}\\) parks the norm on the axis — \\((2+\sqrt{3})(2-\sqrt{3}) = 4 - 3 = 1\\) — the same conjugation-parking that pairs enjoy (Example 1's lemma), now on real surds. One shape of 1 canonicalizes; another shape of 1 would not. That is the whole move: identity guaranteed, shape chosen.

Exercise 5 ◐ The four-square frontier

Lagrange's theorem says every whole number is a sum of four squares — the lab census over \\(1\\) to \\(100\\) counts \\(100\\) out of \\(100\\). Explore the boundary: find the smallest number that genuinely needs all four squares, and verify it by hand. Then take the lab's 15 three-square refusers and hunt for the pattern that characterizes them (write each refuser in the form \\(4^a(8b+7)\\); check the criterion both ways — every refuser has the form, every number of the form refuses). Finally, write out Euler's four-square composition by hand on the quadruple \\((2, 7, 6, 4)\\) (which represents 105) composed with the identity \\((1, 0, 0, 0)\\), and then with itself — and say what the identity row certifies.

Hint

Three squares fail first at 7 — try to write \\(7\\) as \\(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\\) with \\(x, y, z \\le 2\\) and watch every combination miss. For Euler's identity, use the recipe from block 5's lab: four squares in, four squares out, cross-terms signed so the norm multiplies exactly.
