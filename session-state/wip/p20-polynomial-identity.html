The Patterns Atlas · P20 · the third move of tier 2

Polynomial identity power

Two students expand the same product and disagree on one coefficient; nobody wants to redo twenty-four multiplications. A rival claims a formula for the sum of the first \(n\) fifth powers, and testing it at every integer would take a lifetime. The move this page teaches refuses both chores: an identity — a claim that two polynomial expressions are the same object at every input — is certified not by expanding but by evaluating. The engine is pure school mathematics: a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) cannot vanish at more than \(d\) points, because every root donates a linear factor and the degree budget runs out. So agreement at \(d + 1\) points forces agreement everywhere — the room of places a false difference could hide is too small to hold \(d + 1\) crimes. This page cashes the certificate on a four-consecutive-integers surprise, buys Vandermonde's identity with four small sums instead of one heroic expansion, and hands a sixteen-variable determinant identity of Lewis Carroll to a coin — the very coin P19 taught you to flip, now certifying algebra instead of existence. The wreckage gets three exhibits: a five-hour clock where a complete census certifies nothing, a forgery that passes any test set you fixed in advance, and a parabola that agrees at infinitely many points and is still not zero.

New word: monomial. A monomial is a single term — a coefficient times a power product, like \(3x^2y\) or \(-7x^4\). A polynomial is a sum of monomials, and monomials are the room's census unit: the number of monomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(n\) variables counts exactly how many independent coefficients a polynomial of that shape can carry (\S3 counts this room twice). Class-12 algebra already owns the two facts this page leans on hardest: the factor theorem — \(P(a) = 0\) exactly when \((x - a)\) divides \(P(x)\) — and its census consequence, that a polynomial equation of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) roots. New word: identity. An identity is the claim that two expressions are the same object: their difference is the zero polynomial — every coefficient zero, so it vanishes at every input, always. An equation asks where a difference vanishes; an identity asserts the difference is nothing at all. The two feel similar and are worlds apart: solving an equation hunts through the room of inputs, while proving an identity is a claim about the whole room at once. New word: vanishing set. The vanishing set of a polynomial \(P\) is the set of inputs where \(P\) evaluates to \(0\) — the roots, gathered into one object. The school fact above, restated in the room language of P19: the vanishing set of a nonzero one-variable polynomial of degree \(d\) holds at most \(d\) points. The crimes a false identity can commit are few, and counting them — not hunting them — is the entire move.

1 · Before we begin

Tier 2 is three climbs old in a moment. P18 taught the lift — prove the easy version in a bigger room, then pay for the trip back down — and P19 taught its sideways twin: when you cannot find the witness, count the room instead, and if the failures cannot cover it, a survivor exists. This page is the door P19 opened on its way out: the same counting skeleton in an algebra costume, with the coin replaced by a degree. The spine moves it leans on are old friends. P02 built conjectures from small tables, and it is about to do it again; P05 taught the classic way to prove an identity — normalize both sides to one canonical form, expand, compare — and this page is P05's shortcut in one specific but vast family: when the canonical forms are too expensive to reach, the evaluations do the certifying for you. P10 supplies the counting idiom — more crimes than the budget allows, so some crime cannot happen — and P11 is the skeleton underneath, as always: a nonzero difference with \(d + 1\) roots is a contradiction run to its face.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Algebra overflows with claims of the shape "these two expressions are secretly the same object" — formulas for sums, products of factors that collapse, determinants that equal other determinants, a friend's expansion that must match yours. The classical certificate is expansion: put both sides in canonical form and compare coefficients. Expansion is honest, and it is exactly wrong for the scale where identities matter: a \(4 \times 4\) determinant identity in the sixteen entries expands, raw, into a hundred and twenty products of six entries apiece held against each other with signs, and merging those terms correctly is precisely the part a human cannot do without error. The other classical move — "test it at a few values" — is what everyone actually does, and everyone correctly distrusts it, because a test that passes is a sighting, not a proof. What has been missing is the price tag: exactly how many tested values convert sighting into proof, and exactly when the conversion fails. The answer is the whole move: one more than the degree — and the failure has a shape too, a room too small for the degree, which \S5 exhibits on a five-hour clock. Between the certificate and the wreckage stands one honest bookkeeping rule, the whole page in a line: the difference of two polynomials is itself a polynomial, and a nonzero one has nowhere near enough roots to fake agreement at \(d + 1\) points.

2 · Four integers, one square

Run the small-case machine of P02 on a pile of arithmetic. Multiply four consecutive integers and add one:

\(n\)\((n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n+2)\)plus onesquare?
3\(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 120\)\(121\)\(= 11^2\)
4\(3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 = 360\)\(361\)\(= 19^2\)
5\(4 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 = 840\)\(841\)\(= 29^2\)
7\(6 \cdot 7 \cdot 8 \cdot 9 = 3024\)\(3025\)\(= 55^2\)

Four perfect squares, and the roots \(11, 19, 29, 55\) are not random: each is \(n^2 + n - 1\) — for \(n = 3\), \(9 + 3 - 1 = 11\); for \(n = 7\), \(49 + 7 - 1 = 55\). The conjecture assembles itself:

\[(n-1)\,n\,(n+1)\,(n+2) \;+\; 1 \;=\; \left(n^2 + n - 1\right)^2 \qquad \text{for every } n.\]

A conjecture is not a proof, and "for every \(n\)" is exactly the demand that kills testing: no run of lucky cases ever reaches all integers. Two classical doors lead out. The clever one is P05's: spot that \((n-1)n(n+1)(n+2) = (n^2+n)(n^2+n-2)\), substitute \(m = n^2+n\), and read \(m(m-2)+1 = (m-1)^2\) — one line, one idea, done. It is beautiful, and it is ungeneralizable: the idea lives in this identity and nowhere else. The second door looks dumber and is the page. Call the left side minus the right side \(D(n)\) — the difference — and observe what shape it has before knowing anything else: a product of linear factors against a square, so \(D\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(4\), however it expands. Now the school census: if \(D\) were nonzero, it could vanish at at most \(4\) points — each root donates a factor \((n - a)\), and four of them exhaust the degree budget. So evaluate. Five values, chosen small:

\(n\)\(-1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)
left side\(1\)\(1\)\(1\)\(25\)\(121\)
right side\(1\)\(1\)\(1\)\(25\)\(121\)

Five agreements. If \(D\) were nonzero it could afford four vanishings, and it has been caught at five — one over budget, contradiction, P11 in miniature. So \(D\) is the zero polynomial: every coefficient zero, the two sides the same object, the identity proved for every \(n\) at once — including the integers nobody will ever test. Count what each proof paid. P05's route paid one idea. This route paid zero ideas and five subtractions: it never looked at the structure, and it did not need to. That trade — insight for census — is the entire pattern, and it scales precisely where insight dries up.

Now the general law, and its proof is school algebra run twice. The certificate theorem. If two polynomials of degree at most \(d\) agree at \(d + 1\) distinct points, they are identical. Proof: their difference \(D\) has degree at most \(d\), and it vanishes wherever the two sides agreed. Suppose \(D\) is nonzero and pick one root \(a\): the factor theorem divides it out, \(D(x) = (x - a)\,Q(x)\) with \(\deg Q = \deg D - 1\). Each further root of \(D\) must be a root of \(Q\) (a product vanishes only where a factor does), so the roots of \(D\) are all roots of the quotient, and the division can be repeated until the degree budget is exhausted. Counting honestly: a nonzero polynomial of degree \(k\) has at most \(k\) roots — one factor theorem application costs one degree, and the budget cannot go below zero. So a nonzero \(D\) of degree at most \(d\) vanishes at at most \(d\) points; it has been caught vanishing at \(d + 1\); the supposition dies, \(D = 0\), and the two sides are one object. That is the entire engine — P10's arithmetic wearing algebra clothes: \(d + 1\) crimes into a budget of \(d\).

The size \(d + 1\) is not a polite margin — it is exact, in both directions. Too few points certify nothing: the polynomial \(x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)\) is nonzero, has degree \(4\), and vanishes at all four of \(0, 1, 2, 3\) — a false identity can agree with a true one at every point of any test set of size \(d\), and \S5's Exhibit B builds this trap at scale. Too many points is waste, but honest waste: testing at \(d + 5\) points costs five extra evaluations and buys nothing the first \(d + 1\) did not. The certificate's price is exactly \(d + 1\) evaluations, and the degree must be known — or honestly overestimated — before the testing starts. Overestimate and you pay extra tests; underestimate and the certificate is void, however many tests pass (\S5, Exhibit B again).

A skeptic should now object, and the objection is the deep one: this is not mathematics, it is checking. The five-point proof never understood why four consecutive integers make a square — the substitution \(m = n^2 + n\) understands it, and the certificate merely confirms it. The objection is correct, and it draws the real boundary: insight invents, the certificate verifies, and these are different jobs done by different tools. P02's tables conjecture; P05's canonical forms explain; P20's evaluations settle. What the objection misses is how much of mathematics is the second job. When two people disagree on one coefficient of a twenty-four-term expansion, no insight exists that settles who is right — but five subtractions do. When a computer algebra system multiplies two symbolic matrices and must be sure the result is right, nothing is understood at all — and the evaluations are still exactly enough. And when the identity has sixteen variables, the boundary between the jobs becomes the whole story:

Lewis Carroll — the same pen that wrote the Alice books — published in 1866 an identity now called Desnanot–Jacobi (his own version is known as condensation): for a square matrix \(M\), the determinant of the whole times the determinant of the inner core equals a difference of two products of corner minors. For a \(4 \times 4\) matrix this is a claim about the sixteen entries, and both sides are polynomials of total degree \(6\) — a fact you can check by looking at the recipe, never by expanding. Expand anyway, in imagination: the two sides are, raw, \(24 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 6 \cdot 6 = 120\) products of six entries each, held against each other with signs — and merging the like terms out of that pile, sign by sign, is exactly the step that breeds errors. The certificate theorem still applies — each entry appears with degree at most \(2\), so a grid of \(3\) values per variable certifies, all \(3^{16} = 43{,}046{,}721\) evaluations of it — but at \(n = 10\) the same grid is \(3^{100}\) evaluations, beyond every computer that will ever be built. The deterministic certificate drowns in the dimension of the room. What survives is the census idea at a sharper resolution — \S4 counts the room monomial by monomial, \S4's last example hands the room to a coin, and the five-hour clock of \S5 shows the one room where even a complete census lies.

3 · Name the move

P20 · Polynomial identity power — agree at enough points, agree everywhere

When a claim smells like an identity — two polynomial expressions that must be the same object at every input, with expansion hopeless or error-prone — do not expand. Subtract: form the difference \(P - Q\), the one object the claim is about. Cap the degree: bound \(\deg(P-Q)\) from the shape of the expressions, never by expanding them — an overestimate costs tests, an underestimate voids the certificate. Test at \(d + 1\) points, where \(d\) is that cap: if the difference evaluates to zero at more points than its degree, it is the zero polynomial, and the identity holds everywhere — including where no test ever looked. Price the certificate honestly: it verifies and does not explain; it is exactly as reliable as the degree cap; and it needs a room bigger than the degree — over a finite room, or in several variables, the fine print of Box P20.A applies before anything is tested.

In words: a false identity cannot hide everywhere, because the place it hides — the vanishing set of the difference — is an object with a budget. The move has three working parts. First, the claim must genuinely be an identity — a statement about all inputs at once — and not an equation; testing converts "same at these points" into "same everywhere" only when the degree cap makes the difference's budget smaller than the test count. Second, the degree cap must come from the shape of the expressions — products of known-degree factors, determinants as sums of products of entries — because the entire certificate's validity is the cap's validity; this is the step where errors of ambition (an uncapped "it's just a polynomial") void everything downstream. Third, the points must live in a room the degree fits: over the usual infinite rooms (integers, reals, rationals) any \(d + 1\) distinct points serve; over a finite room of size \(p\), the certificate needs \(d + 1 \le p\); and in several variables the test set must be a grid, not a pile of points (\S5, Exhibit C). The move is the book's cheapest per use: subtract, cap, test, done — and it is the first move whose entire content is a price tag.

Box P20.A · The two readings of one count

Both readings certify identities, and they are the same count wearing two costumes — one algebraic, one geometric.

Root-budget reading (algebra). The difference \(D = P - Q\) is itself a polynomial. If \(D \ne 0\) with \(\deg D \le d\), the factor theorem gives it at most \(d\) roots, one per degree of budget (\S2's proof, run once and cited forever). Testing at \(d + 1\) points spends one more root than the budget holds: contradiction, so \(D = 0\) — P11 in its smallest costume, and the reason a passed test set carries more force than any run of lucky sightings. The budget is exact: \(d\) agreeing points license nothing, as the nonzero witness \(x(x-1)\cdots(x-d+1)\) shows by vanishing at all of them.

Coordinate reading (geometry). The polynomials of degree at most \(d\) form a room of exactly \(d + 1\) dimensions — the monomials \(1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^d\), one coefficient each. Evaluating at a point is a linear measurement of that room, and \(d + 1\) evaluations at distinct points are a complete coordinate system: two points of the room with all \(d + 1\) coordinates equal are the same point. This is P16's dictionary in miniature — the values-at-\(d+1\)-points table is a coordinate scheme on the room of polynomials, and the certificate theorem is the statement that the scheme loses nothing. The room can be counted as fast as read: the polynomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(n\) variables have one monomial per way of spreading at most \(d\) units of degree over \(n\) slots, so the room's dimension is the binomial count \(\binom{d+n}{n}\) — five monomials for degree \(4\) in one variable, six for degree \(2\) in two, \(15\) for degree \(4\) in two, and \(74{,}613\) for degree \(6\) in the sixteen variables of \S2's determinant monster. To pin a polynomial in \(n\) variables you need exactly that many well-placed evaluations — the room of polynomials outnumbers the constraints, and the certificate is the census that spends each evaluation where it buys the most.

Box P20.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Formula checking: the sum-of-powers identities, Vandermonde's convolution, and every "prove this for all \(n\)" claim that is secretly a polynomial in \(n\) (\S4, Example 2 buys one with four small sums). Computer algebra: expansion engines verify their own outputs by evaluation, because the monomial bookkeeping is where machines err exactly like humans. Determinants: Lewis Carroll's 1866 condensation and its relatives — identities in \(n^2\) variables where the grid certificate drowns but the coin survives (\S4, Example 3). The far shore: Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (1999), which runs the certificate backwards to build objects, and the Chevalley–Warning count of solutions over finite fields — both named as deferred doors in \S6, both visible from this page as "the same budget, read harder".

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the claim is an identity — "for every input", not "find the inputs" — and its difference has a degree you can cap from the shape, without expanding. Second, expansion is the alternative and expansion is expensive or error-prone: twenty-four cross-terms and one coefficient in dispute, or sixteen variables and tens of thousands of monomials. Third, there is a room to test in that fits the degree: \(d + 1\) points from an infinite room, or the fine print of Box P20.A when the room is finite or the variables are many. One key alone is a guess; two, a hypothesis; all three, a certificate.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · A formula settled flat — the sum of fifth powers

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim: an identity in \(n\), not an equation), P02 (the small checks that feed the certificate), P11 (the certificate's engine: a nonzero difference cannot have \(d+1\) roots).

Pin the claim first, the P01 way: for every \(n \ge 1\), the sum \(1^5 + 2^5 + \cdots + n^5\) equals the closed form \(F(n) = \frac{n^2 (n+1)^2 (2n^2 + 2n - 1)}{12}\). "For every \(n\)" is the identity demand, and the classical certifying route is grim: expand the formula, sum the powers by some clever stacking argument, and match. The certificate route never expands anything. It needs one observation about shape: if \(F\) is claimed to track a running sum, the thing to test is not \(F\) against an infinite column of additions but \(F\) against its own increments — the formula is right exactly when \(F(0) = 0\) and \(F(n) - F(n-1) = n^5\) for every \(n\), because a sum is nothing but its start plus its increments.

First check: \(F(0) = 0\), read straight off the formula. Second, the increment claim, and here is the degree cap doing its work: \(F(n) - F(n-1)\) is a polynomial in \(n\) of degree at most \(5\) — \(F\) is degree \(6\), and shifting \(n \to n-1\) cannot raise it, while the leading terms of \(F(n)\) and \(F(n-1)\) agree to within degree \(5\), since both approximate the same \(\frac{n^6}{6}\). So the difference \(D(n) = F(n) - F(n-1) - n^5\) has degree at most \(5\), and the certificate needs exactly \(6\) test points:

\(n\)\(F(n)\)\(F(n-1)\)\(F(n) - F(n-1)\)\(n^5\)
1\(1\)\(0\)\(1\)\(1\)
2\(33\)\(1\)\(32\)\(32\)
3\(276\)\(33\)\(243\)\(243\)
4\(1300\)\(276\)\(1024\)\(1024\)
5\(4425\)\(1300\)\(3125\)\(3125\)
6\(12201\)\(4425\)\(7776\)\(7776\)

Six agreements, one over the budget of \(5\): \(D\) is the zero polynomial, so \(F(n) - F(n-1) = n^5\) for every \(n\) — and the finish is a ladder, rung by rung: \(S(0) = 0 = F(0)\), and \(S(k) = S(k-1) + k^5 = F(k-1) + k^5 = F(k)\). The formula is certified for all \(n\) at once, from seven formula evaluations, six subtractions, and no expansion of anything — the degree-six factored form was never opened. (The ladder in the last line is mathematical induction, which this book formalizes as P66; here it is used in its bare school form.)

Count the alternative honestly: to verify the same claim by direct summation you would need every \(n\) — an infinite column of additions — and to verify it by expansion you would multiply out \(\frac{n^2(n+1)^2(2n^2+2n-1)}{12}\), collect like terms, and then still face the question of what the sum side even is as a polynomial. The certificate bypassed both by locating the one polynomial of degree \(\le 5\) that had to vanish and spending its budget. The price, printed as always: the certificate says the formula is right and never once says why fifth powers organize themselves into a square \(n^2(n+1)^2\) times a quadratic — that insight belongs to Faulhaber and Bernoulli (\S7), and no census will ever buy it.

Example 2 · Vandermonde bought with four small sums

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim), P05 (both sides normalized as polynomials in \(m\) — the canonical form is the price of the degree cap), P02 (the four small counts that feed the certificate).

Pin the claim: Vandermonde's convolution — choosing \(3\) objects from a pile of \(m\) red and \(4\) blue means choosing some \(k\) red and \(3 - k\) blue, over all splits:

\[\binom{m+4}{3} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{3} \binom{m}{k}\binom{4}{3-k} \;=\; 4 + 6m + 4\binom{m}{2} + \binom{m}{3} \qquad \text{for every } m.\]

As a statement about numbers it is proved by a double count — P70's department, planned ahead. But the claim is also, structurally, an identity: both sides are polynomials in \(m\) of degree at most \(3\) (the left side is \(\frac{(m+4)(m+3)(m+2)}{6}\); on the right, \(\binom{m}{3}\) carries the degree \(3\)). So the certificate applies, and the price is four evaluations — each one an honest small count:

\(m\)\(\binom{m+4}{3}\)the four-term sumagreement
0\(4\)\(4 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 4\)\(\checkmark\)
1\(10\)\(4 + 6 + 0 + 0 = 10\)\(\checkmark\)
2\(20\)\(4 + 12 + 4 + 0 = 20\)\(\checkmark\)
3\(35\)\(4 + 18 + 12 + 1 = 35\)\(\checkmark\)

Four agreements against a budget of \(3\): the difference is the zero polynomial, and Vandermonde's convolution holds for every \(m\) — \(m = 10^6\) included, never tested, already settled. Four small additions bought a theorem about infinitely many piles.

The same claim with the \(4\) replaced by an arbitrary \(n\) is an identity in two variables, and it exposes the staircase this page climbs next. In several variables the certificate theorem needs a structural upgrade — a pile of agreeing points is no longer enough (\S5, Exhibit C shows why), but a grid of them is. The grid lemma. If a polynomial \(P(x, y)\) has degree at most \(d_x\) in \(x\) and \(d_y\) in \(y\), and it vanishes on all of \(A \times B\) — every combination of a point of \(A\) with a point of \(B\) — where \(A\) and \(B\) have \(d_x + 1\) and \(d_y + 1\) points respectively, then \(P\) is the zero polynomial. Proof: the one-variable certificate, run twice. Fix any \(a\) in \(A\) and look down the vertical line \(x = a\): the slice \(P(a, y)\) is a one-variable polynomial in \(y\) of degree at most \(d_y\), and it vanishes at all \(d_y + 1\) points of \(B\), so it is the zero polynomial — \(P\) vanishes on the entire line \(x = a\), not just at the tested points. Now collect the vertical lines: write \(P(x, y) = \sum_{i \le d_x} c_i(y)\, x^i\), where the coefficients \(c_i\) are polynomials in \(y\). Each \(a \in A\) gives \(\sum_i c_i(y) a^i = 0\) as a polynomial in \(y\), so each power \(y^j\) separately satisfies \(\sum_i a^i c_{i,j} = 0\), where \(c_{i,j}\) is the coefficient of \(y^j\) in \(c_i\). For each fixed \(j\), that is a one-variable polynomial in \(a\) of degree at most \(d_x\) vanishing at all \(d_x + 1\) points of \(A\): zero again, so every coefficient \(c_{i,j}\) is zero, and \(P\) is the zero polynomial. Down the columns once, across the rows once — the budget spent in two directions.

Applied to the full two-variable Vandermonde \(\binom{m+n}{3} = \sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{3-k}\): both sides have degree \(3\) in each variable, so a \(4 \times 4\) grid of \((m, n)\) values certifies it — sixteen small counts instead of an expansion in two variables. The page's lab runs the full two-variable check exhaustively for all \(m, n \le 8\), and it runs the grid lemma's own census: for every grid with \(|A| = d_x + 1\), \(|B| = d_y + 1\) over the small integers, no nonzero polynomial of the capped degrees vanishes on the whole grid — the lemma is a theorem, and the machine confirms the boundary is exactly where the lemma says (\S8, Ex3 walks the proof by hand).

Moves used, one level up: the claim was normalized into polynomial form (P05's canonical habit), the budget was counted per variable, and the grid — not the pile — was the shape the test set needed; the price is that two-variable certificates cost \((d_x+1)(d_y+1)\) tests, and \(n\) variables cost the product of all of them. That exponential is the crack \S4's last example walks through.

Example 3 · The coin's certificate — Carroll's determinants

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim: the Desnanot–Jacobi identity), P19 (the coin, and the union-bound arithmetic that prices its escapes), P07 (the auxiliary object: the random test point).

Return to \S2's jam: Lewis Carroll's condensation identity on a \(4 \times 4\) matrix — the claim

\[\det(M) \cdot \det(M^{\circ}) \;=\; \det(M^{1}_{1})\det(M^{4}_{4}) \;-\; \det(M^{4}_{1})\det(M^{1}_{4}),\]

where \(M^{\circ}\) is the inner \(2 \times 2\) core and \(M^{i}_{j}\) is the \(3 \times 3\) minor deleting row \(i\), column \(j\). Sixteen variables, total degree \(6\), per-variable degree at most \(2\) — so the deterministic grid certificate needs \(3\) values per variable: \(3^{16} = 43{,}046{,}721\) evaluations. Code can do that; a human cannot, and at \(n = 10\) variables the grid is \(3^{100}\) and nothing can. The move's escape hatch was discovered three times over around 1980: do not test the grid — test random points, and count what a false identity's budget lets escape.

The Schwartz–Zippel budget. Let \(D\) be a polynomial in \(r\) variables, of total degree \(d\), not identically zero, and pick a test point uniformly at random from a room \(S^r\), \(S\) any set of \(s\) values. Then the probability that \(D\) vanishes at the random point is at most \(\frac{d}{s}\). The proof is the one-variable budget, peeled one variable at a time. Write \(D\) as a polynomial in its last variable: \(D = c(x_1, \ldots, x_{r-1})\, x_r^{e} + \text{lower powers}\), with \(e \le d\) the highest power of \(x_r\) that actually appears and \(c\) its leading coefficient, itself a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d - e\). Fix the first \(r - 1\) coordinates. If \(c\) does not vanish there, the slice is a nonzero one-variable polynomial of degree \(e\), and at most \(\frac{e}{s}\) of the \(s\) values of \(x_r\) keep \(D\) at zero. If \(c\) does vanish there, the slice can misbehave — but \(c\) is a nonzero polynomial in one fewer variable, and the same argument prices its own vanishing at \(\frac{d - e}{s}\). Adding the two escape routes and peeling inward variable by variable, the total cannot exceed \(\frac{e}{s} + \frac{d-e}{s} = \frac{d}{s}\). (The full proof books this argument as an induction on \(r\); the peeling above is the induction's engine shown once — the missing formal step is exactly the induction's bookkeeping, named here as such.)

Now spend it. Room \(S = \{-99, \ldots, 99\}\), so \(s = 199\); degree \(d = 6\). A single random point escapes with probability at most \(\frac{6}{199} \approx 0.0302\). Test \(k\) independent random points, and a false identity must escape every one:

random tests \(k\)escape chance of a false identity
1\(\le 0.0302\)
5\(\le 2.5 \times 10^{-8}\)
10\(\le 6.2 \times 10^{-16}\)

Ten evaluations of a sixteen-variable identity, each a handful of integer multiplications, and any false version of it survives with chance below one in a quadrillion. For calibration, one honest sighting first, the kind the coin then certifies at scale: the lab checks the identity exactly on integer matrices, and the first one — \(M\) with rows \(2,1,0,1\), \(1,3,1,0\), \(0,1,2,1\), \(1,0,1,3\) — has \(\det(M) = 12\), inner core determinant \(5\), left side \(60\), and corner-minor products on the right that also land on \(60\), exactly. The sighting agrees; the coin converts agreement into a certificate that scales.

Three certificates, one ledger. Example 1 spent the budget on increments and finished with an induction ladder — deterministic, exact, \(7\) evaluations. Example 2 spent it once per variable and needed the grid's structure — deterministic, exact, \((d_x+1)(d_y+1)\) evaluations, exponential in the number of variables. Example 3 refused the exponential: the coin buys confidence instead of certainty, and the confidence's price is printed to the digit. One engine underneath all three — the difference is a polynomial, the polynomial has a budget, and the certificate spends the tester's points against it. And the lineage note that closes the circle: this is P19's counting skeleton exactly — a false identity's vanishing points are its crimes, the room \(S^r\) is the space of candidates, and the union bound prices what the crimes can cover. Erdős counted colorings; Schwartz counted roots of a difference. The coin never left the building.

5 · When it fails

Three exhibits, one per working part. Each is a place the certificate genuinely breaks — not a hard identity it merely fails to settle, but a way its own logic goes wrong.

Exhibit A · The five-hour clock — a complete census that certifies nothing

Take the difference \(x^5 - x\) into the arithmetic of remainders mod \(5\) — the five-hour clock of B01 and B02, where \(5\) wraps to \(0\). Evaluate the difference at every single residue, an honest and complete census of the room:

\(a\)\(0\)\(1\)\(2\)\(3\)\(4\)
\(a^5 - a\)\(0\)\(0\)\(30\)\(240\)\(1020\)
mod \(5\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)\(0\)

All five residues vanish. The difference agrees with zero at every point of the room — a stronger test than any certificate of \S4 ever ran — and yet \(x^5 - x\) is blatantly not the zero polynomial: its \(x^5\) coefficient is \(1\). The certificate theorem has not failed; it was never licensed. Its budget argument needs \(d + 1 = 6\) distinct test points, and this room has only \(5\) — the degree does not fit in the room, and every conclusion drawn from the census is a type error. What the census actually proved is a different, honest statement: \(a^5 \equiv a \pmod 5\) for every residue \(a\) — Fermat's little theorem in its smallest case, a true fact about the function the clock sees. Over a finite room, two different forms can be the same function: \(x^5 - x\) and the zero polynomial are indistinguishable to the five-hour clock while being different objects, and the repair is always the same — certify in a room that outruns the degree. Over the integers the same polynomial shows its honest budget: \(x^5 - x = x(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)\) has exactly three real roots, three of its five allowed, a vanishing set nowhere near big enough to fake an identity over any room with six points in it.

Exhibit B · The forgery built for your test set — the uncapped degree

A student verifies the fifth-power formula of Example 1 the hard way: evaluate at \(n = 1\) through \(10\), compare with direct summation, ten for ten. Convinced? He should not be, and the reason is a construction. Take the true formula \(F\) and forge \(G(n) = F(n) + (n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-10)\): a different degree-10 polynomial that agrees with \(F\) at every single one of the ten tested points (the correction factor vanishes there), disagrees everywhere else, and at \(n = 11\) overshoots the truth by \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\) — against a true value of \(381{,}876\). Ten tests caught nothing because ten tests cannot: any test set of size \(k\) is defeated, in advance and by construction, by a degree-\(k\) correction. The failure is not in the testing but in what was never stated: a certificate is a claim about the budget, and the student never capped the degree. Example 1's certificate was honest because the difference's degree was bounded by \(5\), from the shape of the claim, before a single test ran. The same uncapped "it's just a polynomial, test it a lot" reflex is exactly how computer algebra systems used to ship bugs, and the repair is structural, not behavioral: no degree cap, no certificate — however impressive the run of agreements looks. (And the trap is two-sided: overestimate the degree and the certificate merely costs extra tests; underestimate it — testing a degree-10 difference at \(7\) points — and the certificate is void, again however many tests pass.)

Exhibit C · The parabola that agrees forever — the pile without the grid

The one-variable budget tempts a false generalization: "a nonzero polynomial in several variables cannot vanish at many points." False, and the counterexample is the first curve anyone draws. The polynomial \(P(x, y) = y - x^2\) vanishes at \((n, n^2)\) for every integer \(n\) — infinitely many agreeing points, unbounded in number, and \(P\) is not remotely zero. The one-variable theorem survives because its proof spends a budget per variable: in \(P(x,y)\) the degree in \(x\) is \(2\), in \(y\) is \(1\), and the honest multivariable certificate is the grid lemma of \S4 — the test points must cover whole rows times whole columns, a product structure, not a pile. No \(2 \times 2\) grid fits inside the parabola's integer points: a full grid \(A \times B\) on the curve would force every \(a^2\) for \(a \in A\) to equal every \(b \in B\), so \(|A| \ge 2\) pins \(A = \{a, -a\}\) and then \(B\) can hold only the single value \(a^2\). The parabola agrees along a one-dimensional thread, and a one-dimensional thread can dodge every rectangular grid while accumulating infinitely many points — which is precisely why \S4's grid lemma demands the grid and \S4's coin prices the pile.

Scope and price of P20

Three boundaries, stated while the exhibits are still warm. (i) The certificate is exactly as reliable as its degree cap. No cap from the shape of the claim, no certificate — Exhibit B's forgery passes any fixed test set by construction, and the cap must come from structure, never from confidence. (ii) The room must outrun the degree. Over a finite room the budget needs \(d + 1\) distinct points in it — Exhibit A's complete census of the five-hour clock is void, and the honest repairs are a bigger room or a coefficient comparison, which is P05's canonical form doing the certifying instead. (iii) In several variables, agreement must have structure. A pile of agreeing points proves nothing at any size — Exhibit C's parabola — and the certified shapes are the grid (\S4, Example 2) and the random point with its priced confidence (\S4, Example 3). What this page defers, it names: the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (Noga Alon, 1999), which reads the same budget backwards — not "many agreements force identity" but "a coefficient that must be nonzero forces an input where the polynomial is nonzero", a construction tool that has settled open problems; and the Chevalley–Warning theorem (1935), which counts solutions over finite rooms when the degrees sum small. The price tag, said once: P20 verifies and never explains, and every certificate it issues carries its room's and its degree's fine print on its face.

6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P23, this page's constructive twin. The certificate theorem says \(d + 1\) agreements force identity; read backwards, the same theorem is a recipe: \(d + 1\) values at distinct points determine a polynomial of degree at most \(d\), uniquely, and Lagrange's formula builds it on the spot from the readings alone. Testing and building are one theorem wearing opposite quantifiers — P20 asks "are these two the same object?", P23 answers "here is the object, from its measurements" — and P23's real subject is what you can then do with a formula the data only partially chose: carry it across boundaries the data never mentioned. The second door is P25, the census end of this page's axis: when the room is small enough, the \(d + 1\) certificate points can be replaced by the entire room, checked exhaustively by machine — and this page's lab is already a miniature P25, running complete small censuses the prose then cites as receipts. The third door is P73, where identities change clothes: wear a whole sequence as one formal power series and the claims you must prove become comparisons of coefficients — with a twist this page prepared you for: over an infinite series there is no "test at enough points", and the certificate of that room is P05's canonical comparison, ruling by coefficients because no census can ever tour the room. The far doors: P27, where the root budget grows a calculus — the argument principle counts a polynomial's zeros inside a contour by an integral, this page's budget theorem read by machinery; and P30, where Exhibit A's wreckage is promoted into a method: work one prime at a time, in rooms where the census is complete and cheap, and reassemble — the five-hour clock stops being a trap the moment you stop asking it for identities and start asking it for residues.

The default habit

When a claim smells like "two costumes, one object" — a formula to hold for all \(n\), an expansion that must match a friend's, a determinant that must equal another determinant — run the move in its fixed order, and do not skip the pricing. Subtract first: form the difference; it is the only object the page ever reasons about, and its degree is the whole ledger. Cap the degree from the shape of the claim — never from confidence: products of known-degree factors, sums of known-degree terms, the recipe of the claim itself — and state the cap out loud, because the cap is the certificate and everything downstream is rented from it. Choose the test to fit the room and the variables: \(d + 1\) distinct points over an infinite room; a grid — whole rows times whole columns — in several variables; and when the grid drowns in its own size, a coin, with the confidence priced in advance. Check the room outruns the degree before trusting any census of a finite room. And price the conclusion: the certificate buys certainty about the form and nothing about the reason — verification never explains, and Exhibit B's ten-for-ten student is exactly what skipping the pricing step looks like.

7 · Where it came from

The budget fact is school-old; the certificate habit is machine-new. Johann Faulhaber published the sum-of-powers formulas — the fifth-power formula of Example 1 among them — in 1631, and Jacob Bernoulli republished and generalized them in Ars Conjectandi (1713), openly crediting Faulhaber with the discovery for the powers he had reached. What neither man had was a reason the formulas must exist — the certificate of this page cannot supply that either; insight invents, verification confirms, and the ledger between them is honest. Lewis Carroll — the Charles Dodgson whose day job was mathematics at Christ Church, Oxford — published his determinant identity of \S2's escalation, the condensation method, in 1866; the identity is true, but his engine divided by intermediate minors and jammed whenever one vanished, and the method slept for a century until the 1990s revived it with routes around the zeros. The randomized line is a three-year burst: Richard DeMillo and Richard Lipton, in 1978, proposed testing algebraic programs on random inputs; Richard Zippel, in 1979, needed sparse polynomials reconstructed from few values; and Jacob Schwartz, in 1980, wrote the multivariable budget this page calls the coin — the lemma Example 3 uses, named Schwartz–Zippel for its two sharpest statements. Three independent discoveries in three years is what a move looks like when the age needs it: computers had made identity-checking a bottleneck, and the bottleneck picked its cure. Noga Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (1999) ran the budget backwards and made it a construction tool, as \S5 promised; Claude Chevalley and Ewald Warning, in 1935, had already found the census law of the finite rooms Exhibit A mapped — a system of small-degree polynomial equations over a finite field cannot have exactly one solution. The color budget spends itself on Dodgson: he published the mathematics under his own name and the Alice books under a pen name, and he is the only author in this book whose invented words outlived his theorems by a wide margin — "frabjous", from Jabberwocky, for a triumphant day. The word is color; the identity is load-bearing.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 (●○○). (a) Rebuild \S2's certificate by hand: evaluate both sides of \((n-1)n(n+1)(n+2)+1 = (n^2+n-1)^2\) at \(n = -1, 0, 1, 2, 3\), and confirm the five agreements. Then run the substitution proof: set \(m = n^2 + n\) and watch both sides collapse to the same short expression. (b) Confirm the boundary from the other side: evaluate the witness \(x(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)\) at \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\), then at \(x = 4\). (c) Generalize the witness: for any \(d\) chosen test points, exhibit a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) that vanishes at every one of them — and say in one sentence why this does not contradict the certificate theorem.

Hint

(a) All five left-hand values are small — the \(n = 2\) row is the first one that does not give \(1\). (b) Multiply the four factors at \(x = 4\). (c) The product of \((x - t)\) over the test points \(t\) is your friend; then count the witness's degree against the certificate's required count.

Worked

(a) \(n = -1\): \((-2)(-1)(0)(1) + 1 = 1\) against \((1 - 1 - 1)^2 = 1\). \(n = 0\): \(1\) against \((0 + 0 - 1)^2 = 1\). \(n = 1\): \(1\) against \(1\). \(n = 2\): \(25\) against \((4 + 2 - 1)^2 = 25\). \(n = 3\): \(121\) against \((9 + 3 - 1)^2 = 121\). Five agreements for a degree-4 difference — one more than the budget, identity certified. The substitution: \(m = n^2 + n\) makes the left side \((n^2 + n - 2)(n^2 + n) + 1 = (m - 2)m + 1 = m^2 - 2m + 1 = (m - 1)^2\), and the right side is \((m - 1)^2\) outright — both sides the same object in canonical form, which is P05's move doing what it does best: the two-line proof exists, and the certificate is the machine-check behind it. (b) At \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\) some factor is zero, so the witness vanishes — four agreements with the zero polynomial. At \(x = 4\): \(4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 = 24 \ne 0\) — the witness is not zero, and no four-point test could ever have told. (c) Given test points \(t_1, \ldots, t_d\), take \((x - t_1)(x - t_2) \cdots (x - t_d)\): nonzero, degree exactly \(d\), vanishing at all \(d\) points. This does not contradict the certificate theorem because the theorem never promised \(d\) points would certify anything — it promised \(d + 1\) would, and the witness demonstrates the promise is exact: a degree-\(d\) difference can agree with zero at every point of a \(d\)-point test and still be a difference.

Exercise 2 (●●○). (a) Verify Example 2's four certificate sums by hand: at \(m = 0, 1, 2, 3\), confirm that both sides of \(\binom{m+4}{3} = 4 + 6m + 4\binom{m}{2} + \binom{m}{3}\) give \(4, 10, 20, 35\). (b) Sit in P01's chair: the certificate tested \(m\) and never \(n\). Explain exactly why — what was the claim as pinned, and what would have changed had the claim been the two-variable version? (c) Price the boundary: exhibit a nonzero polynomial of degree \(3\) that agrees with the zero difference at \(m = 0, 1, 2\), and say why this kills any three-point test of a cubic.

Hint

(a) \(\binom{m}{2} = \frac{m(m-1)}{2}\), \(\binom{m}{3} = \frac{m(m-1)(m-2)}{6}\); evaluate the right side's four terms separately first. (b) Which variables does the pinned claim actually mention? What degree does the two-variable version have, in each variable — and what test set does Example 2's second half use for it? (c) Build a cubic that vanishes at the three test points, then evaluate it one step to the right.

Worked

(a) \(m = 0\): \(\binom{4}{3} = 4\) against \(4 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 4\). \(m = 1\): \(\binom{5}{3} = 10\) against \(4 + 6 + 0 + 0 = 10\). \(m = 2\): \(\binom{6}{3} = 20\) against \(4 + 12 + 4 \cdot 1 + 0 = 20\). \(m = 3\): \(\binom{7}{3} = 35\) against \(4 + 18 + 4 \cdot 3 + 1 = 35\). Four agreements for a degree-3 difference: certified, exactly at price. (b) The claim as pinned is an identity in the single variable \(m\) — the fixed \(4\) is part of the claim's shape (it is the \(n = 4\) case of the Vandermonde convolution, substituted before testing), so there is no \(n\) in the claim and none to test: one variable, degree \(3\), four points, done. The trap the question guards against: the two-variable convolution \(\binom{m+n}{3} = \sum_k \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{3-k}\) is a different claim — degree \(3\) in each of two variables — and it needs Example 2's \(4 \times 4\) grid, sixteen checks, not four. Testing the one-variable claim does not certify the two-variable one; conflating them is the P01 error of proving something other than what was asked. (c) \(m(m-1)(m-2)\): degree \(3\), zero at \(m = 0, 1, 2\), and \(6\) at \(m = 3\). A nonzero difference could pass any three-point test of a cubic — so the certificate's count is exact, not padded: \(d + 1\) or nothing.

Exercise 3 (●●●). Walk the grid lemma by hand, then break it on schedule. (a) Suppose \(P(x, y) = c_0(y) + c_1(y)\,x\), where \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) are polynomials in \(y\) of degree at most \(2\), and \(P\) vanishes on the grid \(A \times B\) with \(A = \{-1, 1\}\), \(B = \{0, 1, 2\}\). Run both steps of Example 2's proof concretely: first show each vertical slice \(P(a, y)\) is the zero polynomial in \(y\), then solve the two resulting identities for \(c_0\) and \(c_1\). (b) Now raise the cap: exhibit a nonzero polynomial of degree \(2\) in \(x\) that vanishes on that same grid, and say precisely why the lemma no longer applies. (c) Five non-grid points: exhibit a nonzero polynomial vanishing at \((0,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((2,4)\), \((3,9)\), \((4,16)\), state which grid the lemma would need to force it zero, and explain why no such grid sits inside those five points.

Hint

(a) The slice at \(a = -1\) is \(c_0(y) - c_1(y)\); the slice at \(a = 1\) is \(c_0(y) + c_1(y)\). Each has degree at most \(2\) and vanishes at the three points of \(B\) — so each is identically zero. Then add and subtract the two identities. (b) The grid has only two columns; what does a degree-2 polynomial in \(x\) need to be forced zero? (c) The five points share a famous shape; the lemma's requirement is a grid sized to the degrees of the polynomial you exhibited.

Worked

(a) Fix \(a = -1\): the slice \(P(-1, y) = c_0(y) - c_1(y)\) has degree at most \(2\) in \(y\) and vanishes at all three points of \(B\) — three agreements against a budget of two, so it is the zero polynomial: \(c_0(y) - c_1(y) \equiv 0\). Fix \(a = 1\): likewise \(c_0(y) + c_1(y) \equiv 0\). Add the two identities: \(2c_0(y) \equiv 0\), so \(c_0 \equiv 0\); subtract: \(2c_1(y) \equiv 0\), so \(c_1 \equiv 0\). Every coefficient is zero — \(P\) is the zero polynomial, and the two-step proof (columns, then rows) is the same induction the page stated, run at a size where every quantity can be watched. (b) \(P(x, y) = x^2 - 1\): nonzero, degree \(2\) in \(x\), degree \(0\) in \(y\), and it vanishes at both points of \(A\) — hence on the whole grid \(A \times B\). The lemma does not apply because its price went unpaid: a degree-\(2\) cap in \(x\) demands \(|A| = 3\) columns, and this grid has only \(2\). The lemma's boundary is exact in the several-variable case exactly as the root budget is in one variable, and \((x - a_1)(x - a_2)\) is the multivariable page's version of the one-variable witness of Exercise 1. (c) \(P(x, y) = y - x^2\) vanishes at all five listed points — they are five integer points of the parabola, \S5 Exhibit C's curve, and \(P\) is that exhibit's polynomial. The lemma would need the grid sized to \(P\)'s per-variable degrees: degree \(2\) in \(x\), degree \(1\) in \(y\), so \(3 \times 2 = 6\) grid points. No such grid sits inside the parabola's integer points — a full grid on the curve would force two different squares \(a_1^2 \ne a_2^2\) to equal the same two values of \(B\), impossible — so the five points are a pile, and a pile certifies nothing: not at five points, not at five million.

Exercise 4 (◆). Diagnose the classmate. A student reports, triumphantly: "\(x^5 - x\) is zero — I checked all five residues mod \(5\) and every single one gave \(0\)." (a) State precisely what is true in the report: what fact did the census actually establish, about what kind of object? (b) State the error: name the room, the degree, the budget, and the exact type confusion the conclusion commits. (c) Prescribe the repair from the book's own shelf: which bridge pages own the room's arithmetic, what check must run before any finite-room census is trusted, and what tool certifies identities in rooms too small for testing — plus the promotion that turns this room's smallness from a bug into a method.

Hint

(a) Fermat's little theorem, smallest case — a statement about every residue in the room. (b) The certificate needs \(d + 1\) points; count the room's size against \(5 + 1\). What two different things can "zero" mean — a function's readings, or a form's coefficients? (c) B01 and B02 own the clock; this page owns the pre-check; P05 owns the tool that never counts roots at all; and \S6's far doors own the promotion.

Worked

(a) True, and honestly earned: for every residue \(a\) mod \(5\), \(a^5 - a \equiv 0\). The census is complete — five residues, five vanishing readings — and the fact is Fermat's little theorem in its smallest case, a true statement about the function the room can see. (b) False: "\(x^5 - x\) is zero" as a claim about the polynomial. The form has an \(x^5\) coefficient of \(1\), so it is not the zero object. The error is a type confusion between two questions: where does the difference vanish? — an equation, and the census answered it: everywhere in this room — and is the difference the zero polynomial? — an identity, whose certificate needs \(d + 1 = 6\) distinct roots in a room holding only \(5\) residues: void. On a finite room, two different forms (\(x^5\) and \(x\)) can be the same function, so no census of the room — complete or not — can ever distinguish them; "void" is not "confirmed". (c) The repairs, in order: B01 and B02 own the clock's arithmetic, so the room is never mysterious; this page's pre-check — does the room outrun the degree? \(6 > 5\), so no — is what should have stopped the conclusion before it started; P05's canonical comparison certifies identities in rooms too small for testing, because coefficient comparison consults the form and never counts roots at all; and testing in a bigger room confirms the budget honestly — over the reals, \(x^5 - x = x(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)\) has exactly three roots, three of its five allowed. The promotion, named in \S6: P30 makes small rooms a method — work one prime at a time, in rooms where the census is complete — but for statements about residues, never for identities of forms.

Exercise 5 (◐). Build both forgeries yourself. (a) A rival announces that his formula \(F(n)\) — the fifth-power sum formula of Example 1 — is "verified by direct check": he summed at \(n = 1\) through \(10\), ten matches. Build in closed form a rival formula that also passes all ten of his checks and is wrong at \(n = 11\); compute its error there. (b) The parabola's grids: prove that no \(2 \times 2\) grid of integer points lies inside \(\{(n, n^2)\}\), and exhibit the largest grid that does lie inside. (c) Guard the book: one sentence each — which single quantity, fixed before any testing, defeats the forgery of (a), and which structural demand on the test set defeats the pile of (b)?

Hint

(a) Add to \(F\) a correction factor that vanishes at each of his ten points — one factor per point — and evaluate the correction at \(n = 11\). (b) A grid \(A \times B\) on the curve forces every \(a^2\) for \(a \in A\) to equal every \(b \in B\); count what that leaves in \(B\). (c) Exhibit B's moral and Exhibit C's moral, each in one clause.

Worked

(a) Take \(G(n) = F(n) + (n-1)(n-2)\cdots(n-10)\). At each of his ten test points one of the correction's factors is zero, so \(G\) agrees with \(F\) there — ten for ten, "verified" by his own standard. At \(n = 11\) the correction is \(10! = 3{,}628{,}800\), against the true fifth-power sum \(381{,}876\): his method certifies a formula wrong by a factor of nearly ten, and the same construction kills any fixed test set of size \(k\) — the correction is just the product of \((n - t)\) over his points \(t\). Direct check without a degree cap is not weak evidence; it is no evidence, in exactly the sense Exhibit B priced. (b) A \(2 \times 2\) grid \(A \times B\) on the curve needs every \((a, b)\) with \(a \in A\), \(b \in B\) to satisfy \(b = a^2\): then both \(a_1^2\) and \(a_2^2\) must equal both \(b_1\) and \(b_2\), forcing \(b_1 = b_2\) — but \(B\) was required to hold two distinct values. No \(2 \times 2\) grid exists. The largest grid inside is \(2 \times 1\): \(A = \{-1, 1\}\), \(B = \{1\}\), since the two square roots of a shared square supply the two columns and the square itself the single row. The points are a one-dimensional thread, and a thread can dodge every rectangular grid while accumulating points without end. (c) Against (a): the degree cap, fixed from the shape of the claim before any test runs — with the cap, his ten tests were one more than needed and the forgery is impossible to build under it; without it, no run of tests ever suffices. Against (b): the demand that the test set be a grid — or a coin with priced confidence — never a pile: the parabola agrees infinitely often and certifies nothing at any size.
