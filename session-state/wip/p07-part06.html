5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

Construction is craft, and craft misfires in characteristic ways. The failure modes below are worth more than the successes — each is a near-miss that looks like the move and is not.

  • The decoration. An object drawn with no defining property: "let \(P\) be a helpful point on the bank" is not a construction, it is a hope. It cannot enter a proof, because there is nothing to cite — helpful is not a property. The river problem's build worked because \(B'\) came with a recipe (reflect: \((6,2) \mapsto (6,-2)\)) and the recipe handed over a checkable fact (the bank is the perpendicular bisector of \(BB'\)). Mitigation: Box P07.A demand 2 — no recipe, no object. Price: converting "helpful" into a recipe is exactly the hard half of the craft. Default: if you cannot write down what your object is in one sentence a machine could check, you have not built anything yet.
  • The phantom property. A build that exists but does not carry the property you are silently crediting to it. Exhibit: for the wave \(3\sin\theta + 4\cos\theta\), a student "builds" the circle of radius \(3 + 4 = 7\) and reads the maximum off it: 7. The build is real (a circle of radius 7 exists), but its claimed property is false — the amplitude of the wave is \(\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5\), and the grid catches the phantom immediately: the wave's maximum over 721 sampled angles is \(5\), never \(7\) (gap \(2\), Example 1). Mitigation: verify the defining property before use — P01's pinning, applied to your own construction — and run P02's cheap grid check on the resulting claim. Price: one extra check per build. Default: an unverified property is a false property until proved otherwise; your own builds get no discount.
  • The over-build. The move is the registry's heaviest technique, and weight costs. The median theorem can be proved by coordinates in one line (P05's route), by the rectangle in four school steps, or by the circle route leaning on class-10 circle geometry — and the river problem could be done by calculus. When a lighter move already owns the problem, building is waste: more objects on the page, more cases to manage, more chances for phantom properties. Mitigation: route first (P01's claim type, P02's table, P03's cases), build second. Price: sometimes the build is faster than the routing — construction is real work either way, and the registry's chain counts say the work usually pays. Default: the minimal object that answers the named job; no second object without a second job (Example 2's two builds answered two different pedagogical jobs — and the honest note there stands: the circle was the heavier hammer).
  • The build that steals the theorem (circularity). The sneakiest failure: proving a result with a build whose supporting fact secretly uses the result. If the median theorem were proved by "the rectangle's diagonals bisect each other", and the diagonal-bisection fact were itself proved by the median theorem, the proof would be a loop — impressive, and empty. Example 2 was honest about its order: the parallelogram diagonal-bisection property is independent school geometry, reached without ever mentioning medians-to-hypotenuses. Mitigation: audit the supporting facts your build leans on, exactly as P01 audits a problem's givens — and ask of each: could this be proved using my target theorem? If yes, find another route or prove the support first. Price: an honest dependency check every time you lean on a school result. Default: scaffolding must be nailed to the ground, not to the building it supports.
  • The scaffold that will not come down. Sometimes the construction refuses to leave the statement: you build an object, prove a lovely fact, and the fact is about the built configuration, not the problem you were handed. That is not a failure of honesty — but it must be named one of two ways: either the claim is honestly re-stated as being about the larger configuration (the extended claim, published as such), or the build goes back to the workbench. The river answer "touch at \((4,0)\), walk \(6\sqrt{2}\)" needed no \(B'\); the wave's maximum 5 needs no triangle; \(p_5 = 123\) needs no \(\varphi\). Mitigation: Box P07.A demand 4 — write the final claim in the problem's own objects, then check the auxiliary objects are gone from it. Default: if the scaffold is load-bearing in the statement, you have proved a different theorem — say so or rebuild.
Scope note, honest. The registry's own audit says it plainly: this technique's dominance is partly bulk-import glue — its 2,580 graph edges make it the busiest node in the measured graph partly because canon imports attach many theorems to it (block 9 quotes the numbers). The canon side is real too: Pólya's auxiliary problem is a named heuristic in How to Solve It, and no tradition of problem-solving ever taught without constructions. But no metric — chain count or canon — supplies the missing recipe for what to build. The nearest thing to a recipe is analysis, working backwards from the goal: block 7 tells the story; block 6 routes you forward.
