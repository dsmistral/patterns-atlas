The Patterns Atlas · P19 · the second move of tier 2

Count the outcomes

Sometimes you cannot find the thing at all. The coloring you need is one among \(2^{435}\) candidates; the code you need is one among more books than there are atoms; the value you need is not written anywhere you can look. When the search is hopeless and the construction is not in sight, there is a third move: stop hunting for the object and count the room instead. Name the space of all candidates, pick one at random, and ask what a random pick typically gets wrong — if the failures cannot cover the room, a survivor exists, and the proof of it is an average. Show something exists by proving a random pick finds it. The nineteenth move is the probabilistic method: expectation as a counting tool, the union bound as a census of the bad outcomes, and the price printed honestly on the ticket — the proof certifies that the good candidate exists and never once says which one it is. This page runs the move on the problem that created it, Erdős's party colorings, where a five-line count proves that a perfect seating exists at sizes no search will ever reach; then repairs a graph cut with an average, builds a polynomial out of a coin, and studies the wreckage in the three ways the count can fail.

New word: expected value. A random variable is a number attached to a chance outcome — the count of heads in five coin flips, the total shown by two dice. Class-12 probability already owns the whole toolkit this page leans on: the distribution of a random variable lists each value with its probability; the mean (or expected value) \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x \cdot \Pr(X = x)\) averages the values weighted by their chances; the variance \(\mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\big]\) measures the spread around the mean. One identity does more work on this page than every other fact combined, and it is on every class-12 syllabus: the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, always, even when the parts are entangled — \(\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]\), no independence required. New word: indicator. An indicator is the smallest random variable that still counts: \(X_A = 1\) when the event \(A\) happens, \(0\) when it does not. Its mean is its probability — \(\mathbb{E}[X_A] = \Pr(A)\) — and a complicated count ("how many bad groups appear?") becomes a sum of indicators, whose mean is then a sum of probabilities, by linearity. That one sentence is the engine of the entire page. New word: union bound. The probability that at least one of several bad events happens is at most the sum of their probabilities — \(\Pr(A_1 \text{ or } A_2 \text{ or } \dots) \le \Pr(A_1) + \Pr(A_2) + \dots\) — because overlapping ways to fail get counted twice, and over-counting failures is exactly what an upper bound may do. Read it upside down and it becomes the sentence this page actually uses: the probability that no bad event happens is at least \(1 - \sum_i \Pr(A_i)\) — and if that number is positive, a survivor exists.

1 · Before we begin

Tier 2 is two climbs old in a moment: P18 taught the lift — prove the easy version in a bigger room and pay for the trip back down — and this page teaches its sideways twin. The spine's ten-plus-seven moves are all on the shelf above, and one of them matters here more than the rest. P10 forced collisions by counting: when more objects than boxes must be sorted, two objects share a box, and the collision was the conclusion. P10's counting needed two things — a map from objects to boxes, and more objects than boxes. Both are often missing exactly when existence questions are hardest: for a party of thirty people there are \(2^{435}\) acquaintance patterns, no boxes anywhere in sight, and no map that sorts them usefully. The move this page adds is the other direction of counting: when you cannot count objects into boxes, count the room of all candidates itself — how many candidates are bad, and is that smaller than the room? P02 is the close ancestor: its small tables are censuses of little rooms, and this page is the same census habit run on rooms so large that only an average can tour them. P11 is the skeleton underneath, as you will see in §3: the whole method is one contradiction wearing probability clothes. And P15 supplies the arithmetic idiom — the bounds here are walls, sums of small probabilities pushed under one.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Some objects are so buried that no search reaches them and no formula constructs them — yet the problem only asks that one exist. A seating of thirty people with no three mutual acquaintances and no three mutual strangers, if it exists, is one among \(2^{435}\); a good codebook, at the sizes where codes matter, is one among more candidates than particles in the visible universe; and for each of these, the classical tools are silent: construction needs an idea nobody has, the pigeonhole needs a map nobody can write, and the small-case census drowns. The probabilistic method's wager is that existence is a weaker demand than construction — weak enough to be paid for by an average. If a random candidate fails, on average, at fewer than one requirement, then some candidate fails at none — the failures cannot cover the room. The wager pays off shockingly often, and this page pays out three times in §4 alone. The bad news, and §5 spends three exhibits on it: the count can overflow and say nothing while the object exists (Exhibit A); the proof can leave the witness permanently anonymous (Exhibit B); and the experiment itself is a design choice that can be chosen badly (Exhibit C). Between the payouts and the wreckage stands one honest bookkeeping rule, the whole method in a line: expectation is counting, probability is a dialect, and the survivor is real exactly because the bad cannot fill the room.

2 · The party nobody can seat

Six people at a party, and a fact you can check on a napkin: among any six, either three are mutual acquaintances or three are mutual strangers. Pick any person, call her Asha; the other five each either know Asha or do not, so by sheer counting — P10's pigeonhole, five objects into two boxes — at least three of them stand in the same relation to her. Say three know Asha. If any two of those three know each other, they and Asha are three mutual acquaintances; if no two of them do, the three are mutual strangers. The same two sentences run if three do not know Asha. There is no seating of six that escapes: the pattern is forced, and the pigeonhole did the forcing.

Now run it backwards, the direction this page lives in. Does the pattern become unavoidable any earlier? Five people: is there a seating where no three are mutual acquaintances and no three are mutual strangers? This time the pigeonhole is silent — five objects into two boxes only forces a group of three, and groups of three were never the crime. But a construction answers, and it is small enough to hold:

Seat the five around a round table — acquaintances next to each other, strangers apart — and check the crime on pairs. Three mutual acquaintances would be a triangle with all three sides red; but the red pairs form a five-cycle, and a cycle of length five has no triangle inside it: starting anywhere and walking red edges you come home only after five steps, never three. Three mutual strangers would be a triangle with all three sides blue; and the blue star is secretly the same shape as the red ring — walk the blue edges in the order \(1 \to 3 \to 5 \to 2 \to 4 \to 1\) and you have traced \(13, 35, 52, 24, 41\), all five blue pairs, a five-cycle again, no triangle inside it either. So five people can escape both patterns, and the napkin argument says six people cannot: the boundary sits exactly at six, and the escape seating of five is the pentagon of Fig P19.1.

Now make the clique one step bigger and watch every tool die. Question, pinned the P01 way: how many people can a party hold while avoiding both a red clique of four and a blue clique of four — and, in general, of \(k\)? For \(k = 3\) the answer was six, found by napkin plus pentagon. For \(k = 4\) the answer is known — eighteen — but not by napkin: the escape seating on seventeen people exists and can be written down (§4 names it), and the eighteenth person's impossibility took real work. For \(k = 5\) nobody knows the answer; the world's best efforts pin it only between 43 and 48. And the reason nobody knows is structural, not temporal: a seating of \(n\) people is a yes/no choice on each of the \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\) pairs, so the room of all seatings has \(2^{n(n-1)/2}\) members. For \(n = 30\) that is \(2^{435}\), a number with 131 digits — the visible universe holds perhaps \(10^{80}\) atoms, and even the atom count raised to the power \(1.6\) falls short of this room. Search is dead, census is deader, and for general \(k\) no construction idea has ever survived contact with the problem.

In 1947, Paul Erdős published the question that dissolves the jam: who said anything about finding the seating? The claim to prove is only that a good seating exists. So make the room itself the experiment. Seat the thirty-two people of a \(k = 10\) party by coin flips: each pair independently acquainted or not, a fair coin per pair. Now count the crime the way an accountant would, one group at a time. Fix any one group of ten people. Its \(\binom{10}{2} = 45\) pairs are all red with probability \(2^{-45}\), all blue with probability \(2^{-45}\), so the group is monochromatic with probability \(2 \cdot 2^{-45} = 2^{-44}\). Groups of ten among thirty-two people: \(\binom{32}{10} = 64{,}512{,}240\) of them. The expected number of monochromatic ten-groups in a coin-flip seating — the average, over the whole room of \(2^{496}\) seatings, of the count of crimes — is, by linearity of expectation, the sum of one \(2^{-44}\) per group:

\[\mathbb{E}[\text{mono } K_{10}\text{s in } K_{32}] \;=\; \binom{32}{10} \cdot 2^{-44} \;=\; \frac{64{,}512{,}240}{17{,}592{,}186{,}044{,}416} \;\approx\; 0.0000037 \;<\; 1.\]

That is the whole proof, and its last step is the move itself. If every seating of the thirty-two had at least one monochromatic group of ten, the average count over all seatings would be at least \(1\) — an average of numbers that are all \(\ge 1\) cannot be \(0.0000037\). So some seating has none. A party of thirty-two people with no ten mutual acquaintances and no ten mutual strangers exists — and the proof never names it, never draws it, never could. The room of candidates had \(2^{496}\) members; the count of the bad, per candidate on average, was less than one; the failures cannot cover the room. Existence without construction, at a size no search will ever visit — from a five-line average. And not only does a good seating exist: since the bad seatings hold on average \(0.0000037\) crimes, at least all but a \(0.0004\%\) fraction of the room is good — almost every flip of the coin lands a witness.

A skeptic should now object, and the objection is the right one. Is this really a proof — does something so lucky-sounding license a fact? Yes, and for a reason worth saying twice: nothing in it is lucky. The coin flips are a counting device, not a gamble — the expectation is an average over the entire, completely deterministic room of all \(2^{496}\) seatings, exactly the kind of census P02 taught you to run, computed by average because enumeration is impossible. "Probability" here is bookkeeping language for a fraction of a room; no randomness enters the conclusion, which is a fact about all seatings at once. The sections that follow name the move and its promises (\S3), run it three more times — a Ramsey bound in general (\S4, Example 1), a cut no one could guess (\S4, Example 2), a polynomial built from a coin (\S4, Example 3) — and then study its wreckage (\S5), because the method's honesty lives exactly where it fails: the count can overflow, the witness can stay anonymous, and the experiment itself can be the wrong one.

3 · Name the move

P19 · Count the outcomes — prove something exists by proving a random pick finds it

When a problem jams in a way that smells like a missing witness — you can say exactly what would count as an answer, the room of candidates is honest and huge, and neither search nor census nor construction can reach inside it — do not hunt the witness. Name the room of all candidates and the precise definition of bad (a candidate that fails). Count the bad on average: run the honest thought experiment of picking a candidate at random, and sum, over every bad event, its individual chance — no independence required. Compare the average count to one: if the expected number of failures in a random candidate is less than \(1\), then some candidate has none, and the witness exists. Price the conclusion honestly: the proof licenses existence, not construction; the witness stays anonymous until someone earns it by other means.

In words: some objects are hard not because they are rare but because the room they live in is too big to search. The move has three working parts. First, the room and the crime must be nameable — every candidate counted exactly once, every bad event precisely defined, the coin flip understood as bookkeeping (a fraction of the room), never as a gamble. Second, the average must be computed by linearity — the expected count of bad events is the sum of the per-event chances, and this single-step sum is why the method survives sizes no enumeration can visit; independence is not needed and demanding it is the classic beginner's error. Third, the conclusion must be priced — what arrives is an existence claim with a quantitative floor (\S3, Box P19.A), not a name, and a count that overflows past \(1\) kills the argument, never the object. The move is the book's first that proves things about objects it cannot point at.

Box P19.A · The two readings of one inequality

Both readings certify existence, and they are the same computation wearing two costumes.

Expectation reading. Let \(X\) be the number of bad events in a random candidate: the sum of indicator variables \(X = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_m\), where \(X_i\) is \(1\) when bad event \(i\) occurs and \(0\) otherwise. Linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X_1] + \cdots + \mathbb{E}[X_m]\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]\) is just the chance of event \(i\) — a sum of single-event chances, with no independence anywhere in sight (the ten-groups of §2 overlapped freely; the sum did not care). If \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\), then a candidate with \(X = 0\) must exist: if every candidate had \(X \ge 1\), the average of \(X\) over the room could not be below \(1\). The same step gives the quantitative floor: since \(X \ge 1\) on at most an \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)-fraction of the room, \(\Pr[X = 0] \ge 1 - \mathbb{E}[X]\) — for the \(k = 10\) party, at least a \(1 - 0.0000037\) fraction of all \(2^{496}\) seatings is good.

Probability reading. Bad events \(A_1, \ldots, A_m\); the union bound says \(\Pr[A_1 \text{ or } \cdots \text{ or } A_m] \le \Pr[A_1] + \cdots + \Pr[A_m]\) — the room covered by the bad events is at most the sum of the rooms they cover separately, because overlap is counted several times on the right and only once on the left. If that sum is below \(1\), the bad events cannot cover the whole room; the uncovered part is exactly the good candidates, and its fraction is again at least \(1 - \sum_i \Pr[A_i]\). Same inequality, two dialects: an accountant's average of crime counts, or a cartographer's bound on coverage. Read "probability" as "fraction of the room" and both dialects are pure counting — which is how Erdős's 1947 paper actually wrote them (\S7).

Reductio in disguise. The move is P11 wearing the room as its quantifier: assume every candidate is bad, average the crime count over the room, and the assumption forces an average of at least \(1\) — contradicting the computed \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\). The contradiction releases a fact about an unnamed object, and P11's price schedule applies unchanged: the stronger the assumption (all candidates bad), the harder it falls.

Box P19.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Ramsey theory: the §2 bound and its general form (\S4, Example 1), plus the Paley seating that makes \(R(4,4) > 17\) concrete. Graph theory: every graph hides a balanced cut holding at least half its edges (\S4, Example 2), with the derandomization walk as its deterministic echo. Analysis: Bernstein's 1912 proof of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, where the polynomial witness is the expectation (\S4, Example 3). Information theory: Shannon's 1948 random codebooks, whose witnesses stayed anonymous for sixty-one years (\S5, Exhibit B). Later doors: the Lovász Local Lemma (1975) and its constructive rebirth by Moser–Tardos (2010), and the 2015 Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava resolution of Kadison–Singer — the move's modern limit case, where the room is a polynomial family (\S5 names what it defers).

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the jam smells like a missing witness at a size beyond search and census — you can define the object, and nothing else about the room is within reach. Second, the room and the experiment are both design choices you own: name the room so every candidate is counted once, and pick the experiment — a fair coin is only the default, and a biased coin can succeed where the fair one overflows (\S5, Exhibit C). Third, the bad-event count splits into per-event averages computable without independence — one clean chance per crime, summed. One key alone is a guess; two keys, a hypothesis; all three, a plan.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · The general Ramsey bound — an inequality no search could earn

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim, in general \(k\)), P10 (the collision being dodged, one room up), P11 (the reductio in disguise, per Box P19.A).

Pin the claim the P01 way, now in general: for every \(k \ge 4\), there is a seating of \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\) people with no monochromatic group of \(k\) — that is, \(R(k,k) > \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\). The room: all \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) colorings, the coin flip per pair. The bad events, one per \(k\)-group: the group is all-red or all-blue, chance \(2 \cdot 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} = 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}\) each. The average crime count, by linearity:

\[\mathbb{E}[\text{mono } K_k\text{s in } K_n] \;=\; \binom{n}{k} \cdot 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}.\]

Two honest estimates finish it. First, \(\binom{n}{k} \le \frac{n^k}{k!}\): every ordered \(k\)-tuple of distinct people names a \(k\)-group, there are at most \(n^k\) ordered tuples, and each group is named \(k!\) times — a bound, not a count, and the looseness is priced below. Second, with \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\) so that \(n^k \le 2^{k^2/2}\), the exponents collapse:

\[\mathbb{E} \;\le\; \frac{2^{k^2/2}}{k!} \cdot 2^{1-k(k-1)/2} \;=\; \frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}.\]

Is \(\frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}\) below \(1\)? At \(k = 4\) it is \(\frac{2^3}{24} = \frac{1}{3}\), and each step from \(k\) to \(k+1\) multiplies it by \(\frac{\sqrt{2}}{k+1}\) — below \(1\) already at \(k = 4\), so the bound only shrinks. For every \(k \ge 4\): average crime count under \(1\), witness exists, \(R(k,k) > \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\). The actual values, computed exactly for each \(k\) — this is what the argument earns:

Now the honesty owed. The \(k = 4\) row certifies only \(R(4,4) > 4\) — while the truth is \(R(4,4) = 18\). The count is a floor, not an estimate: it says good seatings are plentiful, never that the bound is anywhere near tight. The gap has a name and a cure. The named cure: seat seventeen people at vertices \(0\) through \(16\), color the pair \(\{i,j\}\) red exactly when \(i - j\) is a quadratic residue mod \(17\) — the residues are \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, 9, 13, 15, 16\}\), eight of the sixteen nonzero differences, a perfect split. This is the Paley seating, and it has no monochromatic group of four in either color: all \(\binom{17}{4} = 2380\) four-groups fail to be monochromatic, so \(R(4,4) > 17\), one short of the truth. The page's lab runs all 2380 checks. Note what each method paid: the coin argument bought abundance — a \(0.97\)-fraction of good colorings at \(k=4\) — while the Paley seating bought a name — a witness you can write on one line, at the price of one hundredfold more people. Existence is cheap; named existence is a different market.

Moves used, one level up: the room was designed (colorings of \(K_n\)), the experiment designed (fair coin), the count linear — and the price paid was the truth's location: the floor sits far below the fact.

Example 2 · A cut no one could guess — every graph splits nearly in half

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim), P07 (the auxiliary object: the random partition), P11 (the same disguise, now reading an average from above).

Pin the claim: every graph with \(m\) edges has a way to split its vertices into two sides with at least \(\frac{m}{2}\) edges crossing between the sides. Read as a subgraph statement: the crossing edges form a bipartite graph — all crossing edges go side-to-side — so every graph, however tangled, contains a bipartite subgraph holding at least half its edges. The claim is quiet and strong: no hypothesis on the graph, and a bipartite skeleton of at least half its weight always inside.

The room: all \(2^n\) ways to assign \(n\) vertices to two sides. The experiment: a fair coin per vertex. One edge crosses exactly when its two endpoints drew different sides — chance \(\frac{1}{2}\), and the two flips involved are the only ones the event reads. By linearity, the expected number of crossing edges is \(m \cdot \frac{1}{2}\) — and here the move runs backwards: not "the average is below \(1\), so something has none" but "the average is \(\frac{m}{2}\), so some partition has at least \(\frac{m}{2}\)" — an average of numbers that are all below \(\frac{m}{2}\) would itself be below \(\frac{m}{2}\). No independence was consulted: overlapping edges, shared vertices, none of it matters to a linear sum.

Take \(K_5\): \(m = 10\), guarantee \(5\) crossing edges. The truth is better — the best split is \(2 + 3\), crossing \(2 \cdot 3 = 6 = \lfloor \frac{25}{4} \rfloor\) edges, the complete-graph maximum. The guarantee of \(5\) is honest but leave-taking; here is the Figure:

Now the move's most instructive trick: the guarantee can be made deterministic. Walk the vertices one at a time, placing each on the side where it crosses at least half of its edges to the vertices already placed — a local greedy choice with no coin anywhere. Count what the walk earns: each placement secures at least half of that vertex's earlier edges, and every edge is an "earlier edge" of exactly one of its two endpoints (whichever comes later in the walk). Summed over the walk, the crossings secured are at least half of all \(m\) edges. This is derandomization — the method of conditional expectations — and it converts the existence of a good cut into an algorithm that builds one, in one pass, no memory of the room. On \(K_5\) the walk in vertex order \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5\) lands all \(6\) crossing edges, the maximum.

Price the luck honestly: the walk guarantees \(\frac{m}{2}\) and nothing more. The lab census runs the walk over all \(120\) vertex orders of \(K_5\) — every single one reaches the maximum \(6\) — and over all \(24\) orders of a four-vertex path, where \(6\) of the \(24\) land on \(2\), one short of the maximum \(3\). The guarantee is a theorem; the extra edge is luck, and luck is graph-dependent (\S8, Ex5).

Example 3 · A polynomial built from a coin — Bernstein, 1912

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim: Weierstrass's theorem), P07 (the auxiliary object: a coin and a tally), P15 (the squeeze, in its probabilistic dialect: Chebyshev's split).

Pin the claim: Weierstrass's approximation theorem — if \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), then for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is a polynomial \(p\) with \(|p(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon\) at every \(x\) at once. An existence claim in its purest costume: a polynomial is promised, no recipe given. In 1912 — thirty-five years before Erdős's seatings — Sergei Bernstein made the room itself the experiment and found that this time the witness walks out of the proof holding a name tag.

The room and the experiment: fix a point \(x\) in \([0,1]\) and flip a coin that lands heads with chance exactly \(x\), \(n\) times. Let \(X\) be the number of heads. The tally \(X/n\) is the coin's own estimate of its own bias, and its arithmetic is honest: \(\mathbb{E}[X/n] = x\) exactly, and the spread is small — variance \(\frac{x(1-x)}{n} \le \frac{1}{4n}\), tightening like a vice as \(n\) grows. Now apply the function: since \(X\) takes values \(0, 1, \ldots, n\) with \(\Pr[X = k] = \binom{n}{k} x^k (1-x)^{n-k}\), the expectation of \(f(X/n)\) is a finite sum, and that sum is a polynomial in \(x\):

\[B_n(f)(x) \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[f\!\left(\tfrac{X}{n}\right)\right] \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} f\!\left(\tfrac{k}{n}\right) \binom{n}{k} x^k (1-x)^{n-k}.\]

Why is \(B_n(f)\) close to \(f\)? Because \(X/n\) is close to \(x\), and \(f\) cannot manufacture jumps. Uniform continuity on \([0,1]\) (a compactness fact, not a coin fact) gives a \(\delta\) with \(|u - v| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(u) - f(v)| < \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). Chebyshev's inequality prices the far misses: \(\Pr[|X/n - x| \ge \delta] \le \frac{x(1-x)}{n\delta^2} \le \frac{1}{4n\delta^2}\). Split the expectation along that boundary — near \(x\), the gap is at most \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\); far, the gap is at most \(2M\) (with \(M\) the maximum of \(|f|\)) on at most a \(\frac{1}{4n\delta^2}\)-fraction of the room:

\[|B_n(f)(x) - f(x)| \;\le\; \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{2M}{4n\delta^2} \;<\; \varepsilon \qquad \text{once } n > \tfrac{M}{\varepsilon\delta^2}.\]

The split runs for every \(x\) at once — the bounds \(M\), \(\delta\), and \(\frac{1}{4n\delta^2}\) nowhere consult \(x\) — so one \(n\) uniformizes the whole interval. That is the theorem. And now the difference from Examples 1 and 2, the one worth the ledger: here the witness is explicit. The polynomial is not merely certified to exist; it is the expectation itself, written down in closed form, computable by anyone for any \(f\). The proof and the recipe are the same object — existence and construction collapse into one, because the object the count controls is the object the theorem wants.

Watch it work on the worst customer: \(f(x) = |x - \frac{1}{2}|\), a kink — the exact shape smooth things approximate worst. Evaluate \(B_n(f)\) at the kink \(x = \frac{1}{2}\), where the error peaks:

The gap shrinks like \(\frac{0.39894}{\sqrt{n}}\) — the constant is \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\), the coin's kink reading its own bell curve. Price the rate honestly: to halve the error you must quadruple the work. Bernstein's polynomial proves the theorem and earns a recipe, but the recipe is a slow one — approximation theory's later triumphs (splines, Chebyshev economization, and the constructive waves of P24's world) exist because the probabilistic witness, though named, was expensive. The move never promised efficiency; it promised existence, and here it happened to hand over the object itself.

Three counts, one ledger. The Ramsey bound priced abundance with an anonymous witness; the cut priced abundance and then — by walking the conditional expectations — cashed it into a deterministic recipe; Bernstein priced a bound and found the witness standing in plain sight, as the expectation itself. Three jams, one repeated shape: name the room, design the experiment, sum the per-event averages linearly, and state the price before spending. Every promise of Box P19.A passed in all three — and the wreckage in \S5 is where the promises go to die.

5 · When it fails

Three exhibits, one per promise. Each is a place the move genuinely breaks — not a hard problem it merely fails to solve, but a way its own logic goes wrong.

Exhibit A · The vacuous count — overflow proves nothing

Count the mono-triangle events on \(K_5\), both colors: \(\binom{5}{3} = 10\) triangles, each monochromatic with chance \(2 \cdot \frac{1}{8}\), so the expected count of bad events is \(10 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 2.5\) — comfortably above \(1\). The method's ledger says: no conclusion. A beginner reads \(2.5\) as "the thing probably does not exist." The census says otherwise: exactly \(12\) of the \(1024\) colorings of \(K_5\) have no monochromatic triangle — the pentagon seating of \S2 and its eleven siblings. The failure is the argument's, never the object's. Worse, the failure has a mechanism: the union bound counts a coloring with three mono triangles three times, and a room full of overlapping crimes inflates the average without erasing the quiet corners. Overflow is ambiguity — the count cannot distinguish "crimes everywhere" from "crimes piled up in a few seats." When the count overflows, the honest move is to change the count, not to bury the object.

Exhibit B · The anonymous witness — existence without a name

In 1948, Claude Shannon proved that randomly built codebooks achieve channel capacity — nearly error-free communication at the fastest possible rate, a year after Erdős's seatings, and in the same dialect. What Shannon's argument does not give is the codebook itself: it certifies that good ones dominate the room while naming none of them. Coding theorists lived in that gap for sixty-one years — constructions existed, but none reached capacity — until Erdal Arıkan's polar codes (2009) finally wrote an explicit capacity-achieving code down. The Ramsey seatings are still in the gap: no explicit coloring of any \(K_n\) is known that matches what the coin certifies for \(n\) near \(2^{k/2}\), and decades of trying have not closed the distance. The honest nuance cuts the other way too: when the good fraction is overwhelming — the \(k = 10\) party, where at least \(1 - 0.0000037\) of the room is good — then sampling finds a witness almost immediately; flip the coin, check, done. Anonymous is not unreachable. What the move truly cannot do is force the witness to be simple, short, or human-readable — and for some theorems (Shannon's, until 2009) that absence lasted a working lifetime.

Exhibit C · The wrong experiment — the coin is a design choice

Off-diagonal Ramsey: \(R(3,4)\), avoiding a red triangle and a blue four-clique at once. Try the default experiment — fair coin — on \(n = 5\) people. Bad events: red triangles, \(10\) of them, chance \(p^3\) each; blue four-cliques, \(5\) of them, chance \((1-p)^6\) each. At \(p = \frac{1}{2}\): \(10 \cdot \frac{1}{8} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{64} = 1.328125\) — overflow, and the method, tried once, says nothing about parties of five. Now bias the coin: red edges rare, \(p = 0.4\). The same sum reads \(10 \cdot 0.4^3 + 5 \cdot 0.6^6 = 0.64 + 0.233 \approx 0.873 < 1\) — witness exists, \(R(3,4) > 5\), and the experiment, not the theorem, is what changed. (The truth is \(R(3,4) = 9\): an eight-person seating avoids both — red edges form the cycle of eight plus the four opposite chords — and it has no red triangle among its \(56\) triples and no blue four-clique among its \(70\) four-groups; the lab runs both censuses.) The room was never the problem; the measure on it was. Treating "the probabilistic method" as one fixed recipe — fair coin, count, compare to one — is exactly the cargo cult this page exists to dismantle.

Scope and price of P19

Three boundaries, stated while the exhibits are still warm. (i) Existence is not construction. The move's output is a certificate that the room is not empty; Exhibit B prices the gap — sixty-one years between Shannon's random codebooks and Arıkan's named one, and the Ramsey gap still open. (ii) A successful count is a floor, never a ceiling. The \(k = 4\) row certifies \(R(4,4) > 4\) against a truth of \(18\); abundance says nothing about location, and quoting a probabilistic bound as if it approximated the truth is the classic misuse. (iii) The union bound wastes overlap, so overflow is ambiguous. Exhibit A's \(2.5\) hid twelve good colorings; when the count fails, redesign the experiment (Exhibit C) before abandoning the object. What this page defers, it names: the second-moment method — asking not just \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\) but how \(X\) concentrates — and the Lovász Local Lemma (1975), which finds good candidates even when the bad events are everywhere, and whose constructive rebirth by Moser and Tardos (2010) is the derandomization story of Example 2 at full power. The price tag, said once: P19 buys abundance cheaply and must never be cashed as if it bought a name.

6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P20, the next move of tier 2 — the same counting skeleton in an algebraic costume. A nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots: the "room" is all values it could vanish at, the "bad events" are its zeros, and the count is exact. So a polynomial that vanishes at \(d + 1\) points is not "probably" zero — it is zero, because the room of possible roots is too small to hold that many crimes; and two degree-\(d\) polynomials that agree at \(d + 1\) points agree everywhere. No coin appears anywhere, and yet the engine is this page's: count the outcomes, compare to the room's capacity, conclude. The second door is P25, the deterministic cousin: when the room is small enough — \(K_5\)'s \(1024\) colorings, the \(16\) and \(32\) colorings of the Schur census — the average can be replaced by the entire census, and the machine counts every candidate, good and bad, by name. P19 and P25 are the two ends of one axis: as the room grows, census dissolves into average, and the average is the only census a big room will ever afford. The far doors: P37, which differentiates in the presence of randomness — this page's coin given a calculus; P58, where density becomes recurrence, the soft-analysis shadow of counting; and P59, where the counting itself is sharpened by entropy — how much of a room can the bad events really cover.

The default habit

When a problem jams with the shape of a missing witness — you can say exactly what would count as an answer, but no search, census, or construction can reach it — ask whether the room of candidates can be named so that every one is counted exactly once. Then, in order: design the experiment, remembering the coin is yours to bias (Exhibit C); compute the average crime count by linearity, never demanding independence the sum does not need; compare to one; and price the conclusion before spending it — existence, not construction (Exhibit B), a floor, never a location (the \(R(4,4)\) row). If the count overflows, the verdict is "no verdict" (Exhibit A), and the next move is to redesign the experiment or the room, not to abandon the object. And when the count succeeds, three follow-ups, in rising order of effort: sample it — if the good fraction is overwhelming, a handful of random tries likely lands a witness; walk it — run the conditional-expectation walk of Example 2 and turn existence into a deterministic recipe; and ask whether you needed it at all — the smallest room that settles the question is the best one, and P02's napkin still beats every coin this page ever flips.

7 · Where it came from

The counting engine is older than the coin. Pafnuty Chebyshev's inequality — the split that powers Example 3 — circulated from the 1860s, with Irénée-Jules Bienaymé holding an 1853 priority the name never recorded; either way the point is the same: a distribution's average and spread alone bound its tails, no shape assumptions wanted. Sergei Bernstein put that split to existential work in 1912, in a paper whose full title is its own abstract — Démonstration du théorème de Weierstrass fondée sur le calcul des probabilités, published in two pages by the Kharkov Mathematical Society — proving the approximation theorem by averaging over coin flips and handing over the polynomial as the average itself. Paul Erdős, in 1947, aimed the same machinery at Ramsey numbers in "Some remarks on the theory of graphs" (Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 53, 291–297) — and here is the historical nuance this page's \S2 dressed in coins: the 1947 paper is pure counting, written in the language of averages over color classes without a probability word anywhere; the probabilistic dialect the page uses was adopted later, in the collaboration with Alfréd Rényi that built random graph theory at the end of the 1950s. Claude Shannon, in 1948, proved coding capacity by averaging over random codebooks in A Mathematical Theory of Communication — two rooms, two subjects, one move, one year apart. The method's internal frontier has a fifty-year arc: the Lovász Local Lemma (Erdős and László Lovász, 1975) rescued witnesses from rooms where the bad events almost cover everything; Robin Moser and Gábor Tardos, in 2010, made its witnesses constructible — the derandomization walk of Example 2 grown to full power; and Adam Marcus, Daniel Spielman, and Nikhil Srivastava, in 2015, resolved the Kadison–Singer problem by running the whole method on a family of polynomials — the move's modern limit case. Noga Alon and Joel Spencer's The Probabilistic Method is the canon's codification, the book this page's §3 boxes are miniature of. And Erdős himself priced the whole subject in the anecdote this page will not need: if aliens land and demand \(R(5,5)\) or the planet burns, he said, compute the count and hand it over — but if they demand \(R(6,6)\), start negotiating. The anecdote is color, not load-bearing; the boundary it draws is real: counting the outcomes has limits it knows by name.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 (●○○). (a) Rebuild the first three rows of Example 1's table yourself, as exact fractions: for \(k = 4, 5, 6\), compute the expected number of monochromatic \(K_k\)'s in \(K_n\) at \(n = \lfloor 2^{k/2} \rfloor\), and check each is below \(1\). (b) The census behind Exhibit A: prove that the triangle-free two-colorings of \(K_5\) are exactly twelve, by showing the red graph must be a five-cycle and counting the labeled five-cycles. Confirm the count against Exhibit A's \(12\) of \(1024\).

Hint

(a) The count is \(\binom{n}{k} \cdot 2^{1-\binom{k}{2}}\); the table's \(n\)-values are \(4, 5, 8\). (b) First show every vertex has red degree exactly \(2\): a vertex with three red neighbors forces a blue triangle among them, and a vertex with three blue neighbors forces a red triangle. Then ask what a graph on five vertices looks like when every degree is \(2\) — and count its labeled shapes.

Worked

(a) \(k = 4\): \(\binom{4}{4} \cdot 2^{1-6} = 2^{-5} = \frac{1}{32}\). \(k = 5\): \(\binom{5}{5} \cdot 2^{1-10} = 2^{-9} = \frac{1}{512}\). \(k = 6\): \(\binom{8}{6} \cdot 2^{1-15} = 28 \cdot 2^{-14} = \frac{28}{16384} = \frac{7}{4096}\). All three below \(1\) — and all three smaller than Example 1's bound \(\frac{2^{k/2+1}}{k!}\), exactly as a floor should be. (b) Fix a vertex \(v\). If \(v\) has three red neighbors, those three pairwise edges cannot contain a red one (with \(v\) they would make a red triangle), so all three are blue — a blue triangle. So the red degree is at most \(2\); swapping colors bounds the blue degree by \(2\), hence the red degree is at least \(4 - 2 = 2\). Every red degree is exactly \(2\): the red graph is a single cycle through all five vertices — a triangle would violate triangle-freeness, so no \(3\)-cycle detour is available. A labeled five-cycle is fixed by a cyclic order of the five vertices, and each cycle is counted once per starting point and once per direction: \(\frac{5!}{5 \cdot 2} = \frac{4!}{2} = 12\). Twelve good colorings, matching the census of Exhibit A exactly — and each one's blue edges form the complementary five-cycle, the pentagon seating of \S2.

Exercise 2 (●●○). Exhibit C's redesign, in your own hands. (a) Run the fair-coin count for \(R(3,4)\) at \(n = 5\): red triangles and blue four-cliques, and show it overflows. (b) Bias the coin to \(p = 0.4\) and show the expected count drops below \(1\), certifying \(R(3,4) > 5\). (c) The truth is \(R(3,4) = 9\), witnessed by the eight-person seating of Exhibit C. Verify its two properties yourself: show the red graph (the cycle of eight plus the four opposite chords) contains no triangle, and — the part that is genuinely work — explain why checking all \(\binom{8}{3} = 56\) triples and all \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\) four-sets is exactly the machine-census job of P25, not a job for this page's coin.

Hint

(a) \(10\) red-triangle events at \(p^3\), \(5\) blue four-clique events at \((1-p)^6\). (b) Compute \(10 \cdot 0.4^3\) and \(5 \cdot 0.6^6\) separately, then add. (c) For the triangle claim, notice the red graph is triangle-free because a cycle plus opposite chords cannot close a triangle: every red edge connects vertices at distance \(1\) or \(4\) around the cycle, and three such steps cannot return home. For the second half, count the checks: what does the coin's E-value verify, and what does the census verify?

Worked

(a) \(10 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 + 5 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^6 = 1.25 + 0.078125 = 1.328125 > 1\): the fair coin overflows, the method is silent, and nothing about parties of five follows — Exhibit A's ambiguity all over again. (b) \(10 \cdot 0.4^3 = 10 \cdot 0.064 = 0.64\); \(5 \cdot 0.6^6 = 5 \cdot 0.046656 = 0.23328\). Sum \(0.87328 < 1\): the average count is below one, so some seating of five has no red triangle and no blue four-clique — \(R(3,4) > 5\), from a coin whose bias you chose. Note what did the work: shrinking the red-triangle term (the dominant one) by making red rare, at the price of growing the blue term — the design trade-off is the exercise. (c) The red graph is the cycle \(C_8\) (edges between vertices at cyclic distance \(1\)) plus the four chords joining opposite vertices (distance \(4\)). A triangle would be three vertices mutually at distances \(1\) or \(4\); but \(1 + 1 = 2\), \(1 + 4 = 5\) (distance \(3\) the other way), and \(4 + 4 = 0\): no sum of two allowed distances lands on an allowed distance, so no triangle closes. The blue four-clique claim is the complement side: every four-set must contain at least one red edge — \(70\) four-sets, each inspected. And here is the boundary between the moves: the coin certifies that some seating exists somewhere in a room of \(2^{28}\) colorings of \(K_8\); verifying this particular named seating is a finite, concrete, boring census — \(56 + 70 = 126\) checks — which is P25's department, deterministic and by name. Existence is the coin's job; verification of a named witness is the machine's.

Exercise 3 (●●●). Bernstein by hand, at all three depths. (a) Compute \(B_4(f)(\frac{1}{2})\) for \(f(x) = |x - \frac{1}{2}|\) directly from the five-term sum, and confirm Example 3's table entry. (b) Re-run Example 3's proof yourself, naming each quantity: given \(\varepsilon\), where does \(\delta\) come from, where does \(M\) come from, and what exactly must \(n\) exceed for the split to land below \(\varepsilon\)? (c) The rate: show why the gap at the kink shrinks like a constant over \(\sqrt{n}\), and identify the constant \(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \approx 0.39894\) by treating \(X/n\) as a bell curve centered at \(x\) with spread \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\).

Hint

(a) Five terms, weights \(\binom{4}{k}\frac{1}{16}\); the middle term vanishes. (b) Example 3's display already carries the two halves — quote them and say which fact supplies each. (c) For a bell curve with standard deviation \(\sigma\), the mean absolute deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\); at \(x = \frac{1}{2}\) the binomial spread is exactly \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\).

Worked

(a) \(B_4(f)(\frac{1}{2}) = \frac{1}{16}\left[f(0) + 4f(\frac{1}{4}) + 6f(\frac{1}{2}) + 4f(\frac{3}{4}) + f(1)\right] = \frac{1}{16}\left[\frac{1}{2} + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + 0 + 4 \cdot \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2}\right] = \frac{1}{16} \cdot 3 = \frac{3}{16} = 0.1875\) — the table's first row, exactly. (b) \(\delta\) comes from uniform continuity of \(f\) on the compact interval \([0,1]\): it is the window inside which \(f\) cannot move more than \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\). \(M\) is the maximum of \(|f|\), which exists for the same compactness reason. The split: on the event \(|X/n - x| < \delta\), the gap is at most \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) by the choice of \(\delta\); on the complement, which Chebyshev bounds at \(\frac{1}{4n\delta^2}\), the gap is at most \(2M\). So \(|B_n(f)(x) - f(x)| \le \frac{\varepsilon}{2} + \frac{M}{2n\delta^2}\), and the second term falls below \(\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\) exactly when \(n > \frac{M}{\varepsilon\delta^2}\) — a bound that consults nothing about \(x\), which is why one \(n\) serves the whole interval. (c) Near a kink, the gap \(B_n(f)(x) - f(x)\) is, to first order, the mean absolute deviation of \(X/n\) from \(x\): the coin's tally is approximately a bell curve with standard deviation \(\sqrt{x(1-x)/n}\), and a bell curve's mean absolute deviation is \(\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}\). At \(x = \frac{1}{2}\), \(\sigma = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}\), so the gap is \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} = \frac{0.39894}{\sqrt{n}}\): at \(n = 100\) this predicts \(0.0399\), against the computed \(0.0398\); at \(n = 1000\) it predicts \(0.0126\), matching to four digits. The \(1/\sqrt{n}\) rate is not an accident of the kink — it is the coin's own spread law, and the honest price of the method: quadruple the flips to halve the error.

Exercise 4 (◆). Diagnose a proposed application. A student announces: "The random method proves that for every \(n\), there is a two-coloring of \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) with no monochromatic Schur triple — no \(x, y, z\) of the same color with \(x + y = z\)." (a) Run the count at \(n = 4\): list the four triples \((1,1,2)\), \((1,2,3)\), \((1,3,4)\), \((2,2,4)\), compute the expected number of monochromatic triples under the fair coin (a repeated letter like \((1,1,2)\) is monochromatic with chance \(\frac{1}{2}\), a triple of distinct numbers with chance \(\frac{1}{4}\)), and note the verdict. Then run it at \(n = 5\), which adds \((1,4,5)\) and \((2,3,5)\). (b) Find the truth by census: how many colorings of \(\{1,\ldots,4\}\) avoid all four triples, and what happens at \(n = 5\)? Derive it by hand from the constraints, not by listing \(32\) colorings. (c) Diagnose in P01's chair: what was wrong with the student's claim before any counting started, what does the overflowing count actually license, and which pages of this book were the right tools all along?

Hint

(a) Sum the four chances; then add the two new ones. (b) From \((1,1,2)\) and \((2,2,4)\): \(c_1 \ne c_2\) and \(c_2 \ne c_4\), so \(c_1 = c_4\) (two colors only). Then \((1,3,4)\) forces \(c_3 \ne c_1\), and \((1,2,3)\) is satisfied automatically. Count the survivors, and then see what \((1,4,5)\) and \((2,3,5)\) do to them. (c) Ask what the count certifies when it is below \(1\), what it licenses when it is above, and whether any count could ever have certified a statement about every \(n\) at once.

Worked

(a) \(n = 4\): \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2} = 1.5\) — overflow, no verdict. \(n = 5\): \(1.5 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 2.0\), and the count only grows from there. (For calibration, \(n = 3\) has just \((1,1,2)\) and \((1,2,3)\): expected count \(0.75 < 1\), and avoiders do exist — the method is honest exactly where it succeeds.) (b) The constraints chain tightly: \(c_1 \ne c_2\) (else \((1,1,2)\) is mono), \(c_2 \ne c_4\) (else \((2,2,4)\)), so with two colors \(c_1 = c_4\); then \((1,3,4)\) having \(c_1 = c_4\) forces \(c_3 \ne c_1\), and \((1,2,3)\) cannot be monochromatic because \(c_1 \ne c_2\) already. So \(c_2 = c_3\) is the opposite color: exactly two colorings, \(\{1,4\}\) red with \(\{2,3\}\) blue, and the swap — a census of \(2\) out of \(16\), hiding under the overflowed count, Exhibit A's lesson exactly. At \(n = 5\): \((1,4,5)\) demands \(c_5 \ne c_1\) (since \(c_1 = c_4\)), and \((2,3,5)\) demands \(c_5 \ne c_2\) (since \(c_2 = c_3\)); but \(c_1 \ne c_2\), so \(c_5\) must avoid both colors — impossible. Zero colorings of \(32\); in fact no coloring of \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\) exists for any \(n \ge 5\), which is Schur's theorem (1916) in its smallest case. (c) The diagnosis, in P01's chair: the student never pinned the claim. "For every \(n\)" is a universal existence statement, and the random method is a per-\(n\) tool — its count at each fixed \(n\) says nothing about the others, and here the counts above \(1\) licensed nothing while the truth was nonexistence. The overflowing \(1.5\) was not evidence against the object (Exhibit A: two good colorings survived it), and the student's mistake was reading silence — in both directions — as a verdict. The right tools were the small census (P02: sixteen and thirty-two colorings are napkin-sized), the pin before the proof (P01: state the quantifiers before counting), and, for the general theorem, the machine census (P25) or Schur's own argument — none of which flip a single coin. The count is a floor and a silence; it was never a survey of the truth.

Exercise 5 (◐). The walk's guarantee versus its luck. Example 2's derandomization walk places each vertex on the side that crosses more of its edges to already-placed vertices (ties broken toward the first side). (a) Prove the guarantee yourself: the walk always ends with at least \(\frac{m}{2}\) crossing edges, whatever the order. (b) The lab census runs the walk over all \(120\) vertex orders of \(K_5\) and finds all \(120\) land the maximum \(6\) — and over the \(24\) orders of the four-vertex path, where \(6\) land on \(2\), one short of the maximum \(3\). Reproduce a failing path walk by hand: vertices \(1, 2, 4, 3\) of the path \(1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\), and watch the guarantee hold while the maximum escapes. (c) Say what the \(K_5\) census does and does not prove: is "every order reaches the maximum on \(K_5\)" a theorem or a coincidence, and what would settle the question for a graph you choose?

Hint

(a) Count edges at the moment their later endpoint is placed; what does the greedy choice secure for each vertex's own earlier edges? (b) Place the vertices in the given order, breaking the one tie toward the first side, and tally crossings as they are secured. (c) A census over all orders is a proof for that graph; a statement about every graph needs either a proof or a counterexample — the path is where the luck runs out.

Worked

(a) Each edge belongs to exactly one placement: the moment its later endpoint (in the walk order) is placed. When vertex \(v\) is placed, the greedy choice puts it on the side crossing at least half of \(v\)'s edges to earlier vertices: the crossing secured is at least the non-crossing among those edges. Summed over all placements — which is all \(m\) edges, each counted once — the crossings are at least half the total. No coin, no room, no memory: a one-pass guarantee, and this is the method of conditional expectations in miniature. (b) Path \(1\!-\!2\!-\!3\!-\!4\), order \(1, 2, 4, 3\). Vertex \(1\): no earlier edges, placed on the first side, \(A\). Vertex \(2\): one earlier edge, to \(1\) on \(A\); placing on \(B\) crosses it — secured \(1\). Vertex \(4\): no earlier edges, tie, placed on \(A\). Vertex \(3\): earlier edges to \(2\) (on \(B\)) and \(4\) (on \(A\)) — one each, a tie; the tie rule places it on \(A\), securing the edge to \(2\) but not the edge to \(4\). Final sides \(A = \{1, 3, 4\}\), \(B = \{2\}\): crossings are the edges \((1,2)\) and \((2,3)\) — total \(2 \ge \frac{3}{2}\), the guarantee honored, the maximum \(3\) (sides \(\{1,3\}\) against \(\{2,4\}\)) missed by exactly the tie. (c) The census proves, for \(K_5\), that every one of the \(120\) orders reaches \(6\) — that is a theorem about \(K_5\), established by exhaustive check. It does not prove that complete graphs are always so lucky, and it does not transfer: the path census shows a graph where \(6\) of \(24\) orders fall short, so "every order reaches the maximum" is a graph-by-graph property, not a law. What would settle it generally: either a proof (a class of graphs and a rule for when greedy is optimal) or a counterexample — and the hunt for one is exactly how P03's special-case discipline turns into new theorems. The guarantee is forever; the luck is local.
