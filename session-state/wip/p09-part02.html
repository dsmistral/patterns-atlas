2 · Meet the problem

A chameleon convention in a meadow: thirteen red, fifteen green, seventeen blue — forty-five chameleons in all. Whenever two chameleons of different colours meet, both leave the meeting wearing the third colour: red meets green, both walk away blue; green meets blue, both walk away red; blue meets red, both walk away green. Meetings of two same-coloured chameleons change nothing. The question: can the meadow ever become single-colour — all forty-five chameleons wearing one colour?

Try it small first (P02's instinct, and here it genuinely works). One red, one green, one blue: a red meets a green, both turn blue, and the meadow is three blues — done in one meeting. Two red, two green, one blue: red meets green, twice, and the meadow is five blues — done again. The small cases melt; the pattern looks friendly. Now the real meadow: thirteen, fifteen, seventeen. Play some meetings and watch the counts:

\[13, 15, 17 \;\to\; 12, 14, 19 \;\to\; 11, 13, 21 \;\to\; 10, 12, 23 \;\to\; \cdots\]

The counts slide around, the meadow churns — and the single colour never arrives. Different orders of meetings, different choices: same churn, same wall. After a while the honest suspicion dawns: maybe no route exists at all. But "I tried and failed" is not a sentence about the meadow; it is a sentence about you.

Here is the pivot — P08's pointer honoured. Stop watching the chameleons one at a time and watch pairs of colours. Take the red count minus the green count, \(R - G\), and ask what each meeting does to it:

  • red meets green: both counts drop by one — \(R - G\) does not move;
  • red meets blue: red drops one, green gains two — \(R - G\) changes by \(-3\);
  • green meets blue: green drops one, red gains two — \(R - G\) changes by \(+3\).

Every meeting changes \(R - G\) by \(0\) or by \(\pm 3\): always a multiple of \(3\). So the leftover of \(R - G\) on division by \(3\) — in B02's language, the class of \(R - G \pmod{3}\) — never changes, not in any meeting, not ever. The start has \(R - G = 13 - 15 = -2\), which is \(1\) more than a multiple of \(3\). Every single-colour target has leftover \(0\): all blue is \(0 - 0\), all red is \(45 - 0\), all green is \(0 - 45\). The start wears class \(1\); every target wears class \(0\); no meeting ever changes the class. The target is unreachable. Not "we did not find the meetings" — there is no sequence of meetings at all: any sequence preserves the class, and the classes disagree.

And the small cases? The same one quantity explains them. One of each colour: \(R - G = 0\), the target's class — and indeed it succeeds. Two, two, one: again class \(0\), and it succeeds. The friendly small cases were friendly because their class matched; the real meadow was never hard, it was sealed. One quantity, computed once, retired every failed attempt we could ever make.
