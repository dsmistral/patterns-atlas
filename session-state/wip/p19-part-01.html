The Patterns Atlas · P19 · the second move of tier 2

Count the outcomes

Sometimes you cannot find the thing at all. The coloring you need is one among \(2^{435}\) candidates; the code you need is one among more books than there are atoms; the value you need is not written anywhere you can look. When the search is hopeless and the construction is not in sight, there is a third move: stop hunting for the object and count the room instead. Name the space of all candidates, pick one at random, and ask what a random pick typically gets wrong — if the failures cannot cover the room, a survivor exists, and the proof of it is an average. Show something exists by proving a random pick finds it. The nineteenth move is the probabilistic method: expectation as a counting tool, the union bound as a census of the bad outcomes, and the price printed honestly on the ticket — the proof certifies that the good candidate exists and never once says which one it is. This page runs the move on the problem that created it, Erdős's party colorings, where a five-line count proves that a perfect seating exists at sizes no search will ever reach; then repairs a graph cut with an average, builds a polynomial out of a coin, and studies the wreckage in the three ways the count can fail.

New word: expected value. A random variable is a number attached to a chance outcome — the count of heads in five coin flips, the total shown by two dice. Class-12 probability already owns the whole toolkit this page leans on: the distribution of a random variable lists each value with its probability; the mean (or expected value) \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x \cdot \Pr(X = x)\) averages the values weighted by their chances; the variance \(\mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2\big]\) measures the spread around the mean. One identity does more work on this page than every other fact combined, and it is on every class-12 syllabus: the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, always, even when the parts are entangled — \(\mathbb{E}[X + Y] = \mathbb{E}[X] + \mathbb{E}[Y]\), no independence required. New word: indicator. An indicator is the smallest random variable that still counts: \(X_A = 1\) when the event \(A\) happens, \(0\) when it does not. Its mean is its probability — \(\mathbb{E}[X_A] = \Pr(A)\) — and a complicated count ("how many bad groups appear?") becomes a sum of indicators, whose mean is then a sum of probabilities, by linearity. That one sentence is the engine of the entire page. New word: union bound. The probability that at least one of several bad events happens is at most the sum of their probabilities — \(\Pr(A_1 \text{ or } A_2 \text{ or } \dots) \le \Pr(A_1) + \Pr(A_2) + \dots\) — because overlapping ways to fail get counted twice, and over-counting failures is exactly what an upper bound may do. Read it upside down and it becomes the sentence this page actually uses: the probability that no bad event happens is at least \(1 - \sum_i \Pr(A_i)\) — and if that number is positive, a survivor exists.
