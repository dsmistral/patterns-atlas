1 · Before we begin

Tier 2 is three climbs old in a moment. P18 taught the lift — prove the easy version in a bigger room, then pay for the trip back down — and P19 taught its sideways twin: when you cannot find the witness, count the room instead, and if the failures cannot cover it, a survivor exists. This page is the door P19 opened on its way out: the same counting skeleton in an algebra costume, with the coin replaced by a degree. The spine moves it leans on are old friends. P02 built conjectures from small tables, and it is about to do it again; P05 taught the classic way to prove an identity — normalize both sides to one canonical form, expand, compare — and this page is P05's shortcut in one specific but vast family: when the canonical forms are too expensive to reach, the evaluations do the certifying for you. P10 supplies the counting idiom — more crimes than the budget allows, so some crime cannot happen — and P11 is the skeleton underneath, as always: a nonzero difference with \(d + 1\) roots is a contradiction run to its face.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Algebra overflows with claims of the shape "these two expressions are secretly the same object" — formulas for sums, products of factors that collapse, determinants that equal other determinants, a friend's expansion that must match yours. The classical certificate is expansion: put both sides in canonical form and compare coefficients. Expansion is honest, and it is exactly wrong for the scale where identities matter: a \(4 \times 4\) determinant identity in the sixteen entries expands, raw, into a hundred and twenty products of six entries apiece held against each other with signs, and merging those terms correctly is precisely the part a human cannot do without error. The other classical move — "test it at a few values" — is what everyone actually does, and everyone correctly distrusts it, because a test that passes is a sighting, not a proof. What has been missing is the price tag: exactly how many tested values convert sighting into proof, and exactly when the conversion fails. The answer is the whole move: one more than the degree — and the failure has a shape too, a room too small for the degree, which \S5 exhibits on a five-hour clock. Between the certificate and the wreckage stands one honest bookkeeping rule, the whole page in a line: the difference of two polynomials is itself a polynomial, and a nonzero one has nowhere near enough roots to fake agreement at \(d + 1\) points.
