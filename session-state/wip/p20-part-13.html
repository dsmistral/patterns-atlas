Exercise 3 (●●●). Walk the grid lemma by hand, then break it on schedule. (a) Suppose \(P(x, y) = c_0(y) + c_1(y)\,x\), where \(c_0\) and \(c_1\) are polynomials in \(y\) of degree at most \(2\), and \(P\) vanishes on the grid \(A \times B\) with \(A = \{-1, 1\}\), \(B = \{0, 1, 2\}\). Run both steps of Example 2's proof concretely: first show each vertical slice \(P(a, y)\) is the zero polynomial in \(y\), then solve the two resulting identities for \(c_0\) and \(c_1\). (b) Now raise the cap: exhibit a nonzero polynomial of degree \(2\) in \(x\) that vanishes on that same grid, and say precisely why the lemma no longer applies. (c) Five non-grid points: exhibit a nonzero polynomial vanishing at \((0,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((2,4)\), \((3,9)\), \((4,16)\), state which grid the lemma would need to force it zero, and explain why no such grid sits inside those five points.

Hint

(a) The slice at \(a = -1\) is \(c_0(y) - c_1(y)\); the slice at \(a = 1\) is \(c_0(y) + c_1(y)\). Each has degree at most \(2\) and vanishes at the three points of \(B\) — so each is identically zero. Then add and subtract the two identities. (b) The grid has only two columns; what does a degree-2 polynomial in \(x\) need to be forced zero? (c) The five points share a famous shape; the lemma's requirement is a grid sized to the degrees of the polynomial you exhibited.

Worked

(a) Fix \(a = -1\): the slice \(P(-1, y) = c_0(y) - c_1(y)\) has degree at most \(2\) in \(y\) and vanishes at all three points of \(B\) — three agreements against a budget of two, so it is the zero polynomial: \(c_0(y) - c_1(y) \equiv 0\). Fix \(a = 1\): likewise \(c_0(y) + c_1(y) \equiv 0\). Add the two identities: \(2c_0(y) \equiv 0\), so \(c_0 \equiv 0\); subtract: \(2c_1(y) \equiv 0\), so \(c_1 \equiv 0\). Every coefficient is zero — \(P\) is the zero polynomial, and the two-step proof (columns, then rows) is the same induction the page stated, run at a size where every quantity can be watched. (b) \(P(x, y) = x^2 - 1\): nonzero, degree \(2\) in \(x\), degree \(0\) in \(y\), and it vanishes at both points of \(A\) — hence on the whole grid \(A \times B\). The lemma does not apply because its price went unpaid: a degree-\(2\) cap in \(x\) demands \(|A| = 3\) columns, and this grid has only \(2\). The lemma's boundary is exact in the several-variable case exactly as the root budget is in one variable, and \((x - a_1)(x - a_2)\) is the multivariable page's version of the one-variable witness of Exercise 1. (c) \(P(x, y) = y - x^2\) vanishes at all five listed points — they are five integer points of the parabola, \S5 Exhibit C's curve, and \(P\) is that exhibit's polynomial. The lemma would need the grid sized to \(P\)'s per-variable degrees: degree \(2\) in \(x\), degree \(1\) in \(y\), so \(3 \times 2 = 6\) grid points. No such grid sits inside the parabola's integer points — a full grid on the curve would force two different squares \(a_1^2 \ne a_2^2\) to equal the same two values of \(B\), impossible — so the five points are a pile, and a pile certifies nothing: not at five points, not at five million.
