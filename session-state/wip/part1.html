The Patterns Atlas · B06 · bridge page, group B · before P16

Change of basis

Same vector, different numbers — and how to translate. One surveyor's \((3,2)\) is another's \((7,5)\); the dictionary between their schemes is a tiny table of numbers, and building it, reversing it, and composing it is the whole craft. The table even knows the schemes' areas: its determinant is the exchange rate.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it is the second page of group B — the coordinates block. B05 built the measuring scheme itself: a basis is a pair of directions, a reading is a pair of numbers, and the scheme — not the point — owns the numbers. This page builds the translation between two schemes, the way a currency table translates rupees into dollars without any coin changing hands. B07 then hunts the directions a matrix leaves alone, and B08 reaps the harvest: choosing the scheme in which a tangled system falls apart. All four stand behind P16, the pattern that solves a problem by re-dressing it — because re-dressing, for the plane, is exactly the wholesale translation this page puts in your hands. You already own the floor from B05: readings, schemes, the two demands, and the determinant \(D = ps - qr\) as a scheme's health certificate. Take a pencil; you will build a dictionary, break it, and reverse it, all before meeting a general formula.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

The island is back, and so is the cast. A lighthouse at \(O\); a treasure at \(P = (7,5)\) — that is Ana's reading, the school scheme, the one on the map. Ben, you will remember, measures along his coast road \((1,1)\) and his fence \((2,1)\), and his report of the very same treasure was \((3,2)\). Between the two reports stands a new character: the harbourmaster, who keeps the official map and needs every surveyor's waypoints entered on it.

Task one: Ben's note must go on the map. Ben hands in his logbook: three waypoints, all in his own readings — the treasure \((3,2)\), a wreck \((2,1)\), and a well \((1,4)\). The harbourmaster's map speaks only school. Translate each waypoint with a pencil, leg by leg, exactly as B05 taught: a reading \((a,b)\) means \(a\) road-steps plus \(b\) fence-steps, and one road-step is \((1,1)\) in school numbers, one fence-step \((2,1)\):

\[3 \times (1,1) + 2 \times (2,1) = (7,5), \qquad 2 \times (1,1) + 1 \times (2,1) = (4,3), \qquad 1 \times (1,1) + 4 \times (2,1) = (9,5).\]

Three translations done, and a pattern is already showing — look back at the three computations. Every one of them used only two pieces of outside information: the pair \((1,1)\) and \((2,1)\). Ben's readings just say how many of each step; the map needs what each step is. The harbourmaster, doing this all day for every waypoint of every surveyor, does not re-think the arithmetic each time. He writes the two step-vectors in a little table — one column per step-type — and reads off every translation from it:

\[M_{\text{Ben} \to \text{school}} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1\cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 2 \\ 1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Check it against the pencil: the first row of the table says "school-east = 1 per road-step and 2 per fence-step" (\(1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 2 = 7\)); the second row says "school-north = 1 per road-step and 1 per fence-step" (\(1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 2 = 5\)). The table is not a new idea — it is the same leg-by-leg walk, filed for reuse. That table is the whole of this page.
