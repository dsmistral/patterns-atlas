The Patterns Atlas · P14 · the fourteenth move of the spine

The diagonal argument

Some impossibilities are aimed at lists. The census taker claims a numbered row for every number there is, and no search can walk an infinite list to catch him out — every witness you interview might be on the very next row. The fourteenth move takes the claim at its word and builds the witness on the spot: walk down the diagonal of the claimed list, and construct one object that differs from row one in its first digit, from row two in its second, from row three in its third — one deliberate difference per row, every row at once. The list, whatever is on it, has already missed your object. It is the rare refutation that does not hunt for a flaw: it manufactures the miss, to order.

New word: enumeration. An enumeration of a set is a list that numbers its members: a first row, a second row, a third row, and so on, with nothing left over and nothing listed twice — formally, a one-to-one pairing between the set and the counting numbers \(1, 2, 3, \ldots\). To say a set can be enumerated is to say that someone could, in principle, count it off, one member per row, forever. New word: shadow number. Given a claimed enumeration of objects with digits, the shadow number is the number built to differ from row \(n\) in its \(n\)-th digit — one difference per row, charged against every row simultaneously. It is the list's miss, made concrete. New word: uncountable. A set is uncountable when it admits no enumeration at all: every claimed list — clever, long, infinite — provably misses something. The shadow is how the miss gets proved, and this page builds shadows for lists, for maps, and for machines.
