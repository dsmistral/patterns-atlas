The Patterns Atlas · B08 · bridge page, group B · before P16

Diagonalization: decoupling by relabelling

One feed of a machine is a column walk; a hundred feeds look like a hundred tangles. Relabel the plane by the machine's own rails and the tangles dissolve: each rail-reading stretches by its own factor and never speaks to the other. This page names that relabelling — diagonalization — reaps its harvest (powers, orbits, closed forms), and says honestly which machines refuse it.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it is the fourth and last page of group B — the coordinates block. B05 built the measuring scheme: a basis is a pair of directions, a reading is a pair of numbers. B06 built the translation between two schemes: the dictionary, its reverse, and the determinant as the exchange rate for areas. B07 found the machine's own directions — the rails — and filed them as a scheme. This page reaps what those three sowed: relabelled into that scheme, a coupled machine becomes two uncoupled one-number stretches, and its whole future — every power, every orbit — collapses into short formulas. The name for the move is diagonalization; the box it buys is the block's last; and the two honest failures (one rail, or none) get their own worked section. All four pages stand behind P16, the pattern that solves a problem by re-dressing it: to re-dress a problem that wears a machine is exactly this relabelling. You already own every tool on the page: the rails and the hunt (B07), the dictionaries both ways (B06), the health test (B05). Take a pencil; you will feed one point three times the hard way, and watch the same computation collapse, before you meet a single new word.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

The island is back, and so is the harbourmaster's distortion machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), still on the office desk: feed it a map point, out comes a new one. Last page the office hunted the machine's two rails — the red diagonal \((1,1)\), stretched \(\times 4\), and the blue cove arrow \((1,-2)\), stretched \(\times 1\) and so not moved at all — and filed them as the machine's own measuring scheme. Today's question is the one the office has been putting off. The storm is not finished: the coastline machine now runs every year, each spring eating the whole map and returning the next year's. The harbourmaster wants the map of spring 3 — and of spring 10 — without walking the machine one year at a time.

Task: feed the point \((3,0)\) three years running, by hand, in school numbers. One feed is the column walk B06 taught; one point per year is all the office asks. Year by year, on paper:

\[M(3,0) \;=\; 3\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 0\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (9,6),\]

\[M(9,6) \;=\; 9\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 6\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (27+6,\; 18+12) \;=\; (33,30),\]

\[M(33,30) \;=\; 33\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 30\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; (99+30,\; 66+60) \;=\; (129,126).\]

Three feeds, six grown multiplications, and the numbers couple at every step: both entries of each answer hear from both entries of the question. Now put the pencil down and read the four points again: \((3,0)\), \((9,6)\), \((33,30)\), \((129,126)\). Something never moved. Subtract within each point: \(3-0\), \(9-6\), \(33-30\), \(129-126\) — the answer is \(3\), four times running. The tangle has a spine: some one-number story the machine is protecting while it scrambles everything else.

Now the same three years, done the rails way. Split the starting point on the machine's two rails — find the step-counts \(a, b\) with \((3,0) = a\cdot(1,-2) + b\cdot(1,1)\). Two little equations: \(a + b = 3\) along the first coordinate, \(-2a + b = 0\) along the second, so \(b = 2a\) and \(3a = 3\). Whole numbers: \(a = 1\), \(b = 2\). The point is one blue step plus two red steps,

\[(3,0) \;=\; 1\cdot(1,-2) \;+\; 2\cdot(1,1).\]

And a machine that only stretches rail arrows cannot scramble rail step-counts — that is what B07's rails are. The blue step sits on the \(\times 1\) rail: returned as itself, this year and every year. The red content \(2\cdot(1,1)\) sits on the \(\times 4\) rail: one year makes it \(8\cdot(1,1)\), two years \(32\cdot(1,1)\), three years \(128\cdot(1,1)\). Reassemble spring 3:

\[1\cdot(1,-2) \;+\; 128\cdot(1,1) \;=\; (129,126).\]

The same answer — and this route was barely arithmetic: multiply 2 by 4, three times. The protected \(x - y = 3\) is no longer mysterious either: it is the standing-still blue piece, whose \(x - y\) is \(1 - (-2) = 3\), wearing school clothes. Fig B08.1 draws the whole story.

What the pencil just showed. A coupled computation — both coordinates mixed at every step — became, in the right reading, two uncoupled one-number stories. That is the whole phenomenon, and it wants its law. When can a machine be read this way at all? B07 answered half of that: it must own two rails, and the discriminant says whether it does. What exactly does the rail reading buy for the machine itself — spring 10, spring 100, a formula for every spring at once? And what honest price does the relabelling charge, on the machines the door refuses? The next section names the move; the name is the block's last new word.
