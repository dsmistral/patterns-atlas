Coordinates are a measuring scheme

A point does not come with numbers attached. Numbers come from a scheme — a pair of directions you choose — and the readings the scheme takes are what school called the point's coordinates. Choose the scheme well and a problem simplifies; choose one direction twice and the scheme goes blind in an entire half of the plane.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it opens group B — the coordinates block. Four pages share this block: this one builds the measuring scheme itself (what school bookishly calls a basis); B06 translates readings between two schemes; B07 hunts the directions a matrix leaves alone; B08 reaps the harvest, choosing the scheme in which a tangled system falls apart into two easy ones. All four stand behind P16 — the pattern that solves a problem by re-dressing it — because re-dressing a geometry problem is re-measuring its points in a better scheme. You already own the school floor: position vectors, and the components of a point. This page re-derives what "components" really are — a reading taken by a scheme someone chose — and shows that the choosing, not the reading, is where the power lives. Take a pencil and a ruler; you will describe one treasure four ways before you meet a single formula.
