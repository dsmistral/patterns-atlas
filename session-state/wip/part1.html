The Patterns Atlas · P17 · the seventeenth move of the spine

Flip to the dual

Every problem about points has a secret twin that is a problem about lines — every counting question about vertices has one about faces, every statement about unions one about intersections. The twin is not far away in another subject, the way P16's solved sibling was; it is the same problem, stood on its head, with the roles of its objects traded. The seventeenth move is the trade itself, done honestly: write down the dictionary that swaps the roles, check that it carries the problem's relations — and notice that it carries some of them backwards — then solve whichever side is easier and read the other side's answer off for free. A theorem about points becomes a theorem about lines without proving anything new. A census of five solids becomes two pairs and a loner. Half of set algebra proves the other half. The flip is the cheapest machine mathematics owns for manufacturing theorems — and this page builds it, checks it, and shows exactly where its fine print bites.

New word: dual. The dual of a problem or statement is its mirror: the same question asked with the roles of the two kinds of object traded — points for lines, vertices for faces, unions for intersections. "Dual" is an adjective hunting for its dictionary: calling a statement a dual licenses nothing until the trade is written down and checked, which is this page's whole business. New word: incidence. Incidence is the bare relation between points and lines: a point lies on a line, a line passes through a point. Nothing about distance, order, or size — just the on/through fact. Incidence is the relation the point-line flip carries, and it is all it carries. New word: involution. An involution is a flip that comes home: doing it twice returns exactly what you started with. Complementing a set twice gives the set back; flipping a point to a line and back gives the point back. The double flip is the third promise a dual must keep, and the failures of that promise are the most instructive wreckage on this page.

The spine so far, one breath: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on, P13 convicted whole families by manufacturing ever-smaller criminals, P14 built the one object no list can contain, P15 trapped the unwritable between two computable walls, and P16 licensed whole problems to change clothes. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P16, because the flip is the dictionary move's mirror twin: P16 translated a problem into a solved problem's clothes, and this page discovers that some problems carry their own twin inside them — the dictionary here runs from a problem to its own roles-swapped image, and every one of P16's three promises (one-for-one, relations carried, both directions) is reused as-is. P09, because a flip that changes the sign of a quantity leaves its zero untouched — the determinant ahead flips from \(21\) to \(-21\), and "equals zero" survives the flip the way invariants survive everything. And P03, because a flipped statement is a claim, and claims are attacked: the noun-swapping version of the flip produces falsehoods on demand, and the hostile cases are where the honest version earns its keep.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Every geometry book states theorems about points and then, mysteriously, also states a second theorem that is the first one with the words swapped — one theorem about three lines meeting at a point for every theorem about three points lying on a line, a face-counting formula for every vertex-counting formula. The mysterious double bookkeeping is not a coincidence of subject matter; it is a machine, and the machine has a shape you can check. The good news: nothing new needs building. The flip's dictionary is one line long, its checks are P16's checks with the words traded, and the harvest — theorems arriving free, censuses halving, half an algebra proving the other half — is the largest in the book per line of work spent. The bad news, paid up front: the machine's fine print is where all the danger lives. The naive version — swap the nouns and hope — is a lie generator, and even the honest version carries exactly the relations it was checked to carry, backwards, and nothing else. The fine print gets three exhibits, and the exhibits are the page's real content.
