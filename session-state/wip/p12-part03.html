3 · Name the move

What the census taught, stated once and for all.

Pattern P12 — Name the obstruction. When a hunt stalls, or a proof of impossibility succeeds, do not pocket the verdict and move on: interrogate the argument for the wall it leaned on — the property every candidate must satisfy and the target violates. Then promote that wall to a named obstruction, in three acts. (1) Prove the necessity: show that every candidate the rules could possibly produce satisfies the property — a theorem over all routes, never a sample of the ones you tried; the instrument of choice is a census over classes (remainders, colours, parities, degrees), because a census covers every candidate at once. (2) Check the violation: one computation on the target — \(n \bmod 4 = 3\), the board's colour counts, the graph's odd degrees — and the target is sealed without a single search. (3) State the family: name the whole class the wall seals — "no number \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\)", not "not \(2027\)" — so every future instance answers at the same instant, and the boundary of the family is part of the statement: where the wall ends, the question reopens. Then read the wall backwards: the boundary is a compass, telling you exactly which modified problems are no longer doomed — the eighth bridge, the opposite-colour mutilation, the number that passes the class test.

Or, in words: when something cannot happen, find out which class of somethings cannot happen and say so in a form you can check in one line. A proof that ends "so \(2027\) is not a sum of two squares" is one dead target; a proof that ends "no number leaving remainder \(3\) on division by \(4\) is a sum of two squares" is a wall with a name, and it works forever, on questions you have not met yet. But the wall is a one-way instrument — it rules families out, it never rules a single target in — and its necessity must be proven, not presumed: a wall that some legal route walks around is a phantom, and phantoms are indistinguishable from walls until you check the routes.

Box P12.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The property must be necessary — every route, proven. "Every square leaves remainder \(0\) or \(1\) on division by \(4\)" is a theorem about all squares: even, odd, enormous, imaginary — none exempt, none sampled. A property that holds only for the candidates you happened to try is a coincidence, and a proof that leans on it seals nothing. What breaks: the phantom wall — §5's first exhibit watches a student seal a family with a wall that two legal candidates simply walk around.
  2. The target must violate it — checked, not felt. One honest computation on the target: \(2027 = 4 \cdot 506 + 3\); the mutilated board's colour census; the walk's odd-degree count. If the target satisfies the property, the wall is silent — silence is not a verdict, and §5's second exhibit charges the full price for reading silence as yes. What breaks: the one-way filter billed as two-way — the move's most common misuse.
  3. The family must be stated with its boundary. The wall's power is that it answers questions in advance — but only inside its family. "No \(n \equiv 3 \pmod 4\)" is exactly as wide as the census proved, and not one residue wider; the numbers that pass the test but fail anyway (\(21\)) mark the boundary honestly. What breaks: the family overreach — billing a census for targets it never covered; §5's fifth exhibit catches a proof charging a neighbour for a debt the family never owed.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: an impossibility or a "cannot" — no solution, no route, no arrangement — especially one that will recur as a family ("is this number a sum of two squares?" arriving again and again). Problem shape: the direct hunt is endless (candidates never run out), but the candidates all pass through a few common checkpoints — remainders, colours, parities — so a census can watch all of them at once. Tools at hand: an invariant from P09 or a crash from P11 whose working part is checkable on the target in one line. If the obstruction resists naming — if the proof works but no reusable property falls out — say so and keep the proof; a named wall is a bonus, never a requirement. And if the target passes the check, remember: passing means the wall is silent, and silence was never a verdict.
