Exercise 1 · Warm-up: both halves, by hand ●○○
Back to §2's card, rule now revealed (§4). (a) Prove that both halves of \([0,1]\) are hit infinitely often — so the machine's level-1 choice really was a tie, and the tie rule really was needed. (b) Run levels 3 and 4 of the machine by hand: for each, say which half of the current room is hit infinitely often and why, and write the interval the machine holds after level 4.

Hint

For (a): the odd formula gives hits less than \(1/2\) once \(1/(2n+2) < 1/6\); the even formula gives hits at least \(1/2\) on the same kind of condition. For (b): use the closed-form way to test a whole interval — ask which \(n\) land a hit inside it, and whether that set is finite or endless.

Worked

(a) Odd hits: \(1/3 + 1/(2n+2) < 1/2\) exactly when \(2n+2 > 6\), i.e. every odd \(n \ge 3\) — infinitely many hits inside \([0, 1/2]\). Even hits: \(2/3 - 1/(2n+2) \ge 1/2\) exactly when \(2n+2 \ge 6\), i.e. every even \(n \ge 2\) — infinitely many hits inside \([1/2, 1]\). Both halves endlessly hit: a genuine tie, and the left-rule convention is the only thing that broke it. (b) Level 3, room \([1/4, 1/2]\): the left half \([1/4, 3/8]\) holds every odd hit with \(2n+2 > 24\), endlessly many; the right half \([3/8, 1/2]\) holds exactly the six hits the table counted, then goes quiet — keep left. Level 4, room \([1/4, 3/8]\): the left half \([1/4, 5/16]\) holds no hit at all (odd hits never go below \(1/3\), and the even formula would need \(2n+2 \le 48/11\), impossible for even \(n\)); the right half holds all the rest — keep right. After level 4 the machine holds \([5/16, 3/8]\), matching the table's level-4 row.

Exercise 2 · A one-point crowd ●●○
A new record: \(b_n = 1/2 + (-1)^n/(2n)\). Its first eight entries: 0, 0.75, 0.333333, 0.625, 0.4, 0.583333, 0.428571, 0.5625. (a) Prove that every window around \(1/2\) is hit infinitely often. (b) Prove that any other point \(x \ne 1/2\) has a window hit only finitely often — take the window of radius half the distance from \(x\) to \(1/2\), and count. (c) Conclude that the crowd set is exactly \(\{1/2\}\), and notice something the theorem never promised: \(1/2\) is never hit. The machine that must exist — where does its tie rule send it?

Hint

For (a), \(|b_n - 1/2| = 1/(2n)\), and \(1/(2n) < \epsilon\) is a one-line inequality. For (b), write \(d = |x - 1/2|\) and ask when \(b_n\) can land within \(d/2\) of \(x\): the even-\(n\) and odd-\(n\) formulas answer separately, and each gives a bounded interval of possible \(n\).

Worked

(a) A window of radius \(\epsilon\) around \(1/2\) holds hit \(n\) exactly when \(1/(2n) < \epsilon\), i.e. \(n > 1/(2\epsilon)\): every hit past that bar, of both parities, lands inside — infinitely many. (b) Let \(d = |x - 1/2| > 0\), window radius \(d/2\). Even \(n\): \(b_n = 1/2 + 1/(2n)\) lands within \(d/2\) of \(x\) only if \(1/(2n)\) lies within \(d/2\) of \(x - 1/2\), a distance-\(d\) target; that forces \(1/(2n)\) into an interval of length \(d\) away from zero, i.e. \(n\) between two positive bars — finitely many. For \(x = 0.6\) (so \(d = 0.1\)): even hits inside \((0.55, 0.65)\) are exactly \(n \in \{4, 6, 8, 10\}\), four of them, lab-verified. Odd \(n\): \(b_n < 1/2\), on the wrong side of \(1/2\) for any \(x > 1/2\), and symmetrically for \(x < 1/2\). So every \(x \ne 1/2\) has a quiet window: not a limit point. (c) The crowd set is exactly \(\{1/2\}\); and \(b_n = 1/2\) would need \((-1)^n/(2n) = 0\), impossible — the catch is never a member of the crowd it gathers. The machine's tie rule is never consulted after level 1 — both halves of \([0,1]\) are endlessly hit (parity splits them evenly), and from level 2 on the crowding side of \(1/2\) is the only endless one; every walk, any tie rule, catches \(1/2\). A one-crowd record makes the machine look unique; §4's card was the honest counterexample.
