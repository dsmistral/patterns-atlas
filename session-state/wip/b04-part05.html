(c) Linear congruences — solved by certificate

Bézout certificates turn congruence equations into arithmetic. Three cases, carried to the end.

Coprime coefficient: \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\). The dance on (13, 5): \(13 = 2 \times 5 + 3\), then \(5 = 1 \times 3 + 2\), then \(3 = 1 \times 2 + 1\), then \(2 = 2 \times 1 + 0\). Climb: \(1 = 3 - 2 = 3 - (5 - 3) = 2 \times 3 - 5 = 2 \times (13 - 2 \times 5) - 5 = 2 \times 13 - 5 \times 5\). So \(-5 \times 5 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\): the class of 5 has inverse \(-5\), dressed as 8. Multiply the congruence through by 8: \(x \equiv 8 \times 3 = 24 \equiv 11 \pmod{13}\). One class, and it checks: \(5 \times 11 = 55 = 4 \times 13 + 3\). B02's Exercise 3 solved this same congruence by scanning thirteen candidates; the certificate route never scanned at all.

Shared factor: \(6x \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\). Here \(\gcd(6, 15) = 3\), and 3 divides 9 — so combinations of 6 and 15 can reach 9 at all (Box B04.A: they reach exactly the multiples of 3). Write the congruence as \(3 \times 2x \equiv 3 \times 3 \pmod{15}\): a shared factor sits on both sides, and the honest move divides it out including from the modulus — Corollary 3 below is the full law; here it gives \(2x \equiv 3 \pmod 5\). The class of 2 mod 5 has inverse 3, by the one-line certificate \(2 \times 3 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\). So \(x \equiv 3 \times 3 = 9 \equiv 4 \pmod 5\): one class mod 5, which is exactly three classes mod 15 — \(\gcd(6, 15) = 3\) of them: \(x \in \{4, 9, 14\}\). Check all three: \(6 \times 4 = 24 = 15 + 9\); \(6 \times 9 = 54 = 45 + 9\); \(6 \times 14 = 84 = 75 + 9\).

No solution: \(4x \equiv 7 \pmod{10}\). \(\gcd(4, 10) = 2\), and 2 does not divide 7. Box B04.A: the combinations of 4 and 10 are exactly the even numbers — and any class congruent to 7 mod 10 is odd. No solution exists, and no pencil is needed to know it; a scan of all ten classes agrees: none. The gcd settles solvability before any search begins.

4 · Why it is true

Three claims carry this page — the identity (Box B04), the reachable set (Box B04.A), and the climb's correctness — and all three get complete proofs. The onramp's debts then fall out as corollaries, each with its proof too.

The identity, proved by descent. Let \(S\) be the set of positive combinations: \(S = \{\, ax + by > 0 \;:\; x, y \text{ whole} \,\}\), for \(a, b\) not both zero. \(S\) is nonempty — \(a \times a + b \times b\) is a combination (a stack of \(a\) copies of \(a\) plus a stack of \(b\) copies of \(b\)), and it is positive because \(a\) and \(b\) are not both zero. By B01's well-ordering principle, \(S\) has a smallest member; call it \(d = ax_0 + by_0\). Claim: \(d\) divides \(a\). Divide: \(a = qd + r\) with \(0 \le r < d\) (B01's division algorithm). Then

\[r \;=\; a - qd \;=\; a(1 - qx_0) + b(-qy_0),\]

a combination of \(a\) and \(b\), nonnegative and strictly smaller than \(d\). If \(r\) were positive, it would be a member of \(S\) smaller than the smallest member of \(S\) — impossible. So \(r = 0\): \(d \mid a\). The same division applied to \(b\) gives \(d \mid b\). So \(d\) is a common divisor. Finally, let \(c\) be any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\): then \(c\) divides every stack of \(a\) and every stack of \(b\), so \(c\) divides every combination — in particular \(c \mid d\). A positive number that every common divisor divides, and that is itself a common divisor, is the greatest common divisor: \(d = \gcd(a, b)\). And \(d = ax_0 + by_0\) is the identity. Complete.

Notice what powered the proof: a smallest member, a would-be smaller trespasser, and the impossibility of both. That is descent — the move P13's page builds into a whole method. This proof is its first full appearance on the spine; B03's termination argument (leftovers strictly shrink, and well-ordering forbids an endless fall) ran on the same engine, in the small.

The climb, proved. Every leftover in the dance is a combination of \(a\) and \(b\). The first two members trivially are: \(a = 1 \times a + 0 \times b\) and \(b = 0 \times a + 1 \times b\). And each new leftover is \(r = s - q \times t\), where \(s\) and \(t\) are the two members above it — a difference of two combinations with a quotient multiple, which is again a combination, since combinations add, subtract and scale. So the combination property carries down the chain — and the chain is finite (B03's termination), so the propagation is a finite list of identical steps, whose formal name is induction, P66 — named and deferred, exactly as B02 and B03 deferred it. The last nonzero leftover is the gcd (B03), so the gcd arrives carrying a certificate: Box B04, made mechanical. Section 3's climbs were this proof run on real numbers.

Box B04.A, proved. Both directions were the box's own two sentences. The gcd divides \(a\) and \(b\), so it divides every stack and anti-stack, so it divides every combination: every combination is a multiple of the gcd. And Box B04 exhibits the gcd as a combination, so scaling that one certificate by \(k\) reaches every multiple of the gcd. Together: the reachable set is exactly the multiples of the gcd — nothing more, nothing fewer.
