3 · Name the move

P20 · Polynomial identity power — agree at enough points, agree everywhere

When a claim smells like an identity — two polynomial expressions that must be the same object at every input, with expansion hopeless or error-prone — do not expand. Subtract: form the difference \(P - Q\), the one object the claim is about. Cap the degree: bound \(\deg(P-Q)\) from the shape of the expressions, never by expanding them — an overestimate costs tests, an underestimate voids the certificate. Test at \(d + 1\) points, where \(d\) is that cap: if the difference evaluates to zero at more points than its degree, it is the zero polynomial, and the identity holds everywhere — including where no test ever looked. Price the certificate honestly: it verifies and does not explain; it is exactly as reliable as the degree cap; and it needs a room bigger than the degree — over a finite room, or in several variables, the fine print of Box P20.A applies before anything is tested.

In words: a false identity cannot hide everywhere, because the place it hides — the vanishing set of the difference — is an object with a budget. The move has three working parts. First, the claim must genuinely be an identity — a statement about all inputs at once — and not an equation; testing converts "same at these points" into "same everywhere" only when the degree cap makes the difference's budget smaller than the test count. Second, the degree cap must come from the shape of the expressions — products of known-degree factors, determinants as sums of products of entries — because the entire certificate's validity is the cap's validity; this is the step where errors of ambition (an uncapped "it's just a polynomial") void everything downstream. Third, the points must live in a room the degree fits: over the usual infinite rooms (integers, reals, rationals) any \(d + 1\) distinct points serve; over a finite room of size \(p\), the certificate needs \(d + 1 \le p\); and in several variables the test set must be a grid, not a pile of points (\S5, Exhibit C). The move is the book's cheapest per use: subtract, cap, test, done — and it is the first move whose entire content is a price tag.

Box P20.A · The two readings of one count

Both readings certify identities, and they are the same count wearing two costumes — one algebraic, one geometric.

Root-budget reading (algebra). The difference \(D = P - Q\) is itself a polynomial. If \(D \ne 0\) with \(\deg D \le d\), the factor theorem gives it at most \(d\) roots, one per degree of budget (\S2's proof, run once and cited forever). Testing at \(d + 1\) points spends one more root than the budget holds: contradiction, so \(D = 0\) — P11 in its smallest costume, and the reason a passed test set carries more force than any run of lucky sightings. The budget is exact: \(d\) agreeing points license nothing, as the nonzero witness \(x(x-1)\cdots(x-d+1)\) shows by vanishing at all of them.

Coordinate reading (geometry). The polynomials of degree at most \(d\) form a room of exactly \(d + 1\) dimensions — the monomials \(1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^d\), one coefficient each. Evaluating at a point is a linear measurement of that room, and \(d + 1\) evaluations at distinct points are a complete coordinate system: two points of the room with all \(d + 1\) coordinates equal are the same point. This is P16's dictionary in miniature — the values-at-\(d+1\)-points table is a coordinate scheme on the room of polynomials, and the certificate theorem is the statement that the scheme loses nothing. The room can be counted as fast as read: the polynomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(n\) variables have one monomial per way of spreading at most \(d\) units of degree over \(n\) slots, so the room's dimension is the binomial count \(\binom{d+n}{n}\) — five monomials for degree \(4\) in one variable, six for degree \(2\) in two, \(15\) for degree \(4\) in two, and \(74{,}613\) for degree \(6\) in the sixteen variables of \S2's determinant monster. To pin a polynomial in \(n\) variables you need exactly that many well-placed evaluations — the room of polynomials outnumbers the constraints, and the certificate is the census that spends each evaluation where it buys the most.

Box P20.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Formula checking: the sum-of-powers identities, Vandermonde's convolution, and every "prove this for all \(n\)" claim that is secretly a polynomial in \(n\) (\S4, Example 2 buys one with four small sums). Computer algebra: expansion engines verify their own outputs by evaluation, because the monomial bookkeeping is where machines err exactly like humans. Determinants: Lewis Carroll's 1866 condensation and its relatives — identities in \(n^2\) variables where the grid certificate drowns but the coin survives (\S4, Example 3). The far shore: Alon's Combinatorial Nullstellensatz (1999), which runs the certificate backwards to build objects, and the Chevalley–Warning count of solutions over finite fields — both named as deferred doors in \S6, both visible from this page as "the same budget, read harder".

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the claim is an identity — "for every input", not "find the inputs" — and its difference has a degree you can cap from the shape, without expanding. Second, expansion is the alternative and expansion is expensive or error-prone: twenty-four cross-terms and one coefficient in dispute, or sixteen variables and tens of thousands of monomials. Third, there is a room to test in that fits the degree: \(d + 1\) points from an infinite room, or the fine print of Box P20.A when the room is finite or the variables are many. One key alone is a guess; two, a hypothesis; all three, a certificate.
