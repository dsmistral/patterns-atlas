Exercise 1 ●○○

Run the dance on (1386, 322), then use the result to reduce the fraction \(\frac{322}{1386}\) to lowest terms.

Hint

Divide, keep only the leftover, pass down — then divide both numerator and denominator by the last nonzero leftover.

Worked answer

\(1386 = 4 \times 322 + 98\), then \(322 = 3 \times 98 + 28\), then \(98 = 3 \times 28 + 14\), then \(28 = 2 \times 14 + 0\). Last nonzero leftover: 14. So \(\gcd(1386, 322) = 14\), and \(\frac{322}{1386} = \frac{322/14}{1386/14} = \frac{23}{99}\). One more honest check: \(23\) is prime and \(99 = 9 \times 11\), so gcd(23, 99) = 1 — the fraction is fully reduced (the lab runs that final dance too). This is P05's canonical form for fractions, doing exactly what its page promised; the uniqueness of lowest terms, its page said, waits for Euclid's lemma — B04's business.

Exercise 2 ●●○

(a) Prove that consecutive whole numbers are always coprime: \(\gcd(n+1, n) = 1\) for every \(n \ge 1\). (b) Run the dance on (2027, 2026) and count the divisions. (c) Show that the class of \(n - 1\) is invertible mod \(n\) for every \(n \ge 2\), with the inverse exhibited — and reconcile the result with B02's mod-10 census.

Hint

(a) Any common divisor divides the difference of the two numbers. (c) B02's Box B02.A licenses reducing each term of \((n-1)^2\) mod \(n\); expand first.

Worked answer

(a) Let \(d\) divide both \(n+1\) and \(n\). Then \(d\) divides their difference \((n+1) - n = 1\), so \(d = 1\). Complete — one line, the same "linear combination" engine as section 4's hand-me-down. (b) \(2027 = 1 \times 2026 + 1\), then \(2026 = 2026 \times 1 + 0\): two divisions, last nonzero leftover 1. The dance is the proof of (a) made mechanical. (c) \((n-1)^2 = n^2 - 2n + 1\); mod \(n\), B02's Box B02.A reduces \(n^2 \equiv 0\) and \(2n \equiv 0\), leaving \((n-1)^2 \equiv 1\). So the class of \(n - 1\) is its own inverse. Reconciliation: mod 10, the class of 9 is invertible with inverse 9 — exactly the \(9 \times 9 = 81 \equiv 1\) row of B02's Exercise 4 census. The lab checks (c) for every \(n = 2\) to 60: zero failures. Notice the census conjecture's halves in action: (c) exhibits an inverse outright (so the class is invertible, and the easy half of section 4 forces coprimality — gcd(9, 10) = 1 indeed), while the general hard half — every coprime class has an inverse — remains B04's.

Exercise 3 ●●●

(a) Compute \(\gcd(1071, 462, 231)\). (b) Prove the tool you just used: \(\gcd(\gcd(a, b), c) = \gcd(a, b, c)\) — that is, the common divisors of all three numbers are exactly the common divisors of \(\gcd(a, b)\) and \(c\).

Hint

(a) Dance twice. (b) Both directions: Box B03.A for "divides all three ⇒ divides the pair-gcd", and the fact that the gcd divides its own parents for the converse.

Worked answer

(a) First \(\gcd(1071, 462) = 21\) — section 3(b)'s dance. Then \(\gcd(21, 231)\): \(231 = 11 \times 21 + 0\), one division, leftover 0 — the answer is 21. Indeed \(231 = 11 \times 21\), so 21 divides 231 and nothing was lost. (b) Forwards: if \(d\) divides \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), then by Box B03.A \(d\) divides \(\gcd(a, b)\), and \(d\) divides \(c\) — so \(d\) is a common divisor of the pair \((\gcd(a, b), c)\). Backwards: if \(d\) divides \(\gcd(a, b)\), then since \(\gcd(a, b)\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\), transitivity of divisibility gives \(d \mid a\) and \(d \mid b\); with \(d \mid c\), it divides all three. The two common-divisor families are identical, so their greatest members coincide. The lab verifies the identity the exhaustive way: all \(30^3 = 27{,}000\) triples \((a, b, c)\) with \(a, b, c \le 30\), comparing \(\gcd(\gcd(a,b),c)\) against a brute-force scan for the greatest divisor of all three — zero mismatches.

Exercise 4 ◐

How slow can the dance get, really? For every pair \(1 \le b < a \le 1000\), count the divisions. What is the maximum, which pairs achieve it, and what do those pairs have in common? Then check your conjecture's plausibility against Lamé's bound.

What you should find

The lab ran exactly this census — all 499,500 pairs. Maximum: 14 divisions, achieved by exactly one pair: (987, 610). Just behind it, (610, 377) at 13. The pattern is unmistakable: 987, 610, 377 are consecutive Fibonacci numbers, whose every division comes out with quotient 1 — the dance is forced to walk the whole Fibonacci staircase, one rung at a time (Fig B03.3 is this exact shape). Lamé's bound for the worst pair: 610 has three digits, \(5 \times 3 = 15\), and \(14 \le 15\) — the census finds zero violations of the bound anywhere. Honest notes: the census is a detector, not a certificate — it witnesses the pattern but proves nothing about pairs beyond 1000 — and Lamé's theorem is cited here from the canon (1844), not proved; its proof wants the Fibonacci identity machinery and induction, both named and deferred in section 3(d).
