Exercise 1 ●○○ — the sum that stays strange

Prove that a rational number plus an irrational number is always irrational — say \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\). Then the receipt: among all fractions with denominator at most \(10{,}000\), how close does the closest one come?

Hint

Ignite: what exactly must you assume to make the claim false? You need one rational \(r\) and one irrational \(x\) whose sum is rational. What does the arithmetic of rationals — subtracting a rational from a rational — already let you conclude about \(x\)?

Worked answer

Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\) were rational. Subtracting the rational \(\frac{1}{3}\) from a rational leaves a rational — the rationals are closed under subtraction, since \(\frac{a}{b} - \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad - bc}{bd}\) is again a ratio of whole numbers. So \(\sqrt{2} = \left(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2}\right) - \frac{1}{3}\) would be rational — and it is not: §2 cornered it. Contradiction; the sum is irrational. The shape is the general one: a one-move transfer, with §2's theorem as the wall. The receipt: the closest fraction with denominator at most \(10{,}000\) is \(\frac{16385}{9376} \approx 1.7475469\), and it still misses \(\frac{1}{3} + \sqrt{2} \approx 1.7475469\) by about \(3.26 \times 10^{-8}\) — detectors close in, and the wall stands.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the second wall

Prove that \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational. The parity census that cornered \(\sqrt{2}\) is not enough here — \(3\) is odd — so find the wall's local equivalent: prove first that a whole number whose square is divisible by \(3\) is itself divisible by \(3\), then run the corner.

Hint

B02 built the wall: what are the classes of \(n\), \(n^2\) modulo \(3\) for \(n \equiv 0, 1, 2\)?

Worked answer

The lemma first, by B02's classes: if \(n \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) then \(n^2 \equiv 0\); if \(n \equiv 1\) then \(n^2 \equiv 1\); if \(n \equiv 2\) then \(n^2 \equiv 4 \equiv 1\). So \(n^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) happens only in the first class — divisible by \(3\) means divisible by \(3\), squared. The census over \(n \le 400\) agrees: \(n^2\) divisible by \(3\) exactly when \(n\) is. Now the corner: suppose \(\frac{p}{q} = \sqrt{3}\) in lowest terms, so \(p^2 = 3q^2\). Then \(3\) divides \(p^2\), so \(3\) divides \(p\): write \(p = 3k\). Then \(9k^2 = 3q^2\), so \(3k^2 = q^2\), so \(3\) divides \(q^2\), so \(3\) divides \(q\). Both \(p\) and \(q\) divisible by \(3\) — the fraction was not in lowest terms. Contradiction, and \(\sqrt{3}\) is irrational. The census of denominators to \(10{,}000\) finds no \(p, q\) with \(p^2 = 3q^2\) — the wall's local shape, confirmed; and the same proof runs for any prime replacing \(3\), which is Exercise 5's engine.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the primes that refuse, three at a time

Prove that there are infinitely many primes of the form \(4k + 3\) — the primes \(3, 7, 11, 19, 23, \ldots\) that leave remainder \(3\) on division by \(4\). Euclid's machine from Example 1 needs a modification, and finding it is the exercise.

Hint

Suppose \(3, 7, 11, 19, \ldots, p_k\) were all of them, and consider \(N = 4 \cdot (p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1\). What remainder does \(N\) leave on division by \(4\)? And what is the remainder of a product of numbers each leaving remainder \(1\)?

Worked answer

Suppose the list \(p_1 = 3, p_2 = 7, p_3 = 11, p_4 = 19, \ldots, p_k\) contained every prime of the form \(4k + 3\), and set \(N = 4 \cdot (p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k) - 1\). Then \(N \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and \(N > 1\), so \(N\) has a prime factor. No \(p_i\) divides \(N\) — each leaves remainder \(-1\), not \(0\). Could every prime factor of \(N\) leave remainder \(1\) or \(2\) on division by \(4\)? Not remainder \(2\): one factor of \(2\) would make \(N\) even, and \(N\) is odd. Not all remainder \(1\): a product of numbers each \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is itself \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — since \((4a+1)(4b+1) = 16ab + 4a + 4b + 1\) — but \(N \equiv 3\). So some prime factor of \(N\) is \(\equiv 3 \pmod 4\), and it is none of the \(p_i\): the complete list was missing one. Contradiction. Run the machine concretely: with the first four such primes, \(N = 4 \cdot (3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 19) - 1 = 17555 = 5 \times 3511\), and \(3511 = 4 \cdot 877 + 3\) is prime — exactly the promised kind, and not on the list. The census confirms the machine for the first six list lengths, every run producing a fresh \(4k+3\) prime. Note the honest asymmetry: the same argument fails for \(4k+1\) primes, whose infinity needs a different engine — obstructions are family-specific, which is P12's opening theme.

Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

The claim on trial: "\(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is irrational." Three students hand in proofs. Student A: "Irrational plus irrational is irrational; \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(\sqrt{3}\) are both irrational; done." Student B: "Suppose \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is rational. Then \(s^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\), so \(\sqrt{6} = \frac{s^2 - 5}{2}\) would be rational; write \(\sqrt{6} = \frac{a}{b}\) in lowest terms and \(a^2 = 6b^2\) forces \(3 \mid a\) and \(3 \mid b\) — contradiction." Student C: "Suppose \(s\) is rational. Then \(\sqrt{3} = s - \sqrt{2}\), so \(3 = s^2 - 2s\sqrt{2} + 2\), so \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{s^2 - 1}{2s}\) is rational — contradiction." Referee all three: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

For A: test the inference form itself on \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(2 - \sqrt{2}\). For B: check the squaring \(s^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) numerically, then audit the corner: why does \(a^2 = 6b^2\) force \(3 \mid a\), and then \(3 \mid b\)? For C: check the algebra numerically, and ask what happens if \(s = 0\) — may it be?

Worked answer

Student A: rejected — the inference form is false. Irrational plus irrational is not always irrational: \(\sqrt{2}\) and \(2 - \sqrt{2}\) are both irrational, and their sum is exactly \(2\). A's conclusion happens to be true, and that is the trap: truth rescued by a broken inference is luck, not proof, and the same form "proves" that \(\sqrt{2} + (2 - \sqrt{2})\) is irrational — false. Student B: upheld. The squaring is exact: \((\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3})^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) — the receipt \(9.898979485566\) matches \(5 + 2\sqrt{6}\) to twelve digits. If \(s\) were rational, \(s^2\) and then \(\frac{s^2 - 5}{2}\) would be rational, making \(\sqrt{6}\) rational. The corner is Exercise 2's lemma twice over: \(a^2 = 6b^2\) makes \(3 \mid a^2\), so \(3 \mid a\); write \(a = 3m\), and \(9m^2 = 6b^2\) gives \(3m^2 = 2b^2\), so \(3 \mid 2b^2\), and since \(3\) divides neither \(2\) nor shares a factor with it, \(3 \mid b^2\), so \(3 \mid b\). Both \(a\) and \(b\) divisible by \(3\) contradicts lowest terms — so \(\sqrt{6}\) is irrational, so \(s\) cannot be rational. B's proof is a correct contrapositive-with-contradiction: it transfers \(s\)'s supposed rationality to \(\sqrt{6}\), then breaks it on a proven wall. Student C: upheld, with one audit. The algebra checks: \(\sqrt{3} = s - \sqrt{2}\) squares to \(3 = s^2 - 2s\sqrt{2} + 2\), so \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{s^2 - 1}{2s}\) — and the receipt agrees, \(\frac{s^2 - 1}{2s} = 1.414213562373\) at \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\). But the formula divides by \(s\), so the proof owes one line: \(s = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \geq 1.4 + 1.7 > 0\), so the division is legal. With that line, C's proof stands — §5's smuggled-step audit, passed in advance. The referee's ledger: one broken form, one clean transfer, one clean transfer with its division certified — three verdicts, and only the receipts distinguish the honest two from the lucky one.

Exercise 5 ◐ — the hunt, the boundary, and the general wall

Compute first, conjecture second, prove last. For every \(n\) from \(2\) to \(100\): is \(\sqrt{n}\) rational? Tabulate the successes — there are exactly nine — and notice what the successful \(n\)'s have in common. Conjecture the full boundary, then prove it by contradiction: \(\sqrt{n}\) is rational exactly when \(n\) is a perfect square. Then explore outward: what is the corresponding statement for cube roots, and does your proof adapt?

Hint

For the proof direction that matters: suppose \(\sqrt{n} = \frac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms and \(n\) is not a perfect square. P05's wardrobe dresses \(n\) as \(n = a^2 b\) with \(b > 1\) squarefree — no square factor left in \(b\). What does \(p^2 = n q^2 = a^2 b q^2\) force on \(p\), and then on \(q\)?

Worked answer

The census first: the nine successes are \(\sqrt{4}, \sqrt{9}, \sqrt{16}, \ldots, \sqrt{100}\) — the perfect squares, roots \(2\) through \(10\) — and the ninety failures resist a direct search: for every non-square \(n \le 100\) and every \(q \le 200\), no \(p\) makes \(p^2 = n q^2\); that is eighteen thousand checks, all misses, and the detector never turns up a single success outside the squares. The proof: clearly a perfect square has a rational root. Conversely, suppose \(\sqrt{n} = \frac{p}{q}\) in lowest terms with \(n\) not a perfect square. Dress \(n\) by P05: \(n = a^2 b\) with \(b\) squarefree, and \(b > 1\) — the leftover non-square. Then \(p^2 = a^2 b q^2\). First, \(a \mid p\): in \(p^2\) every prime appears an even number of times — twice its count in \(p\) — and \(p^2\) contains all the copies that \(a^2\) carries, so if \(a\) carries \(e\) copies of a prime \(r\), then \(p\) carries at least \(e\) of them. Write \(p = a p_1\); the equation slims to \(p_1^2 = b q^2\). Now pick any prime \(r\) dividing \(b\) — there is one, since \(b > 1\). The lemma of Exercise 2 gives \(r \mid p_1^2\), so \(r \mid p_1\); write \(p_1 = r p_2\). Because \(b\) is squarefree, \(b = r c\) with \(\gcd(r, c) = 1\), and substituting turns \(r^2 p_2^2 = r c q^2\) into \(r p_2^2 = c q^2\). The left side is a multiple of \(r\), so \(r \mid c q^2\); since \(\gcd(r, c) = 1\), the lemma applies once more: \(r \mid q^2\), so \(r \mid q\). But \(r\) divides \(p\) too — \(p = a r p_2\) — so \(r\) divides both \(p\) and \(q\), and the fraction was not in lowest terms. Contradiction: no non-square has a rational square root. The cube-root exploration: the census finds rational cube roots exactly at the perfect cubes \(1, 8, 27, 64, 125\) up to \(200\), and the proof adapts word for word — dress \(n = a^3 b\) with \(b\) cubefree, and the same transfer forces \(b \mid p\) and \(b \mid q\). The general wall stands: \(\sqrt[\,m]{n}\) is rational exactly when \(n\) is a perfect \(m\)-th power — one engine, every root, and the engine is this page's.
