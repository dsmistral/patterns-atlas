The Patterns Atlas · B07 · bridge page, group B · before P16

Directions a matrix leaves alone

A matrix is a machine that eats points and returns points. Most directions it knocks clean off their line; a chosen few it only stretches. Those are the machine's own directions — eigen is German for "own" — and this page hunts them with one equation, files them as a scheme, and watches the machine become a pure stretch inside it.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it is the third page of group B — the coordinates block. B05 built the measuring scheme itself: a basis is a pair of directions, a reading is a pair of numbers. B06 built the translation between two schemes: the dictionary, its reverse, and the determinant as the exchange rate for areas. This page turns the question around. Every dictionary so far translated between two schemes that surveyors chose freely; now a machine — a table that eats points and returns points — answers for itself: which scheme does it prefer? The answer is the directions it only stretches, the machine's own directions, and hunting them is a two-step craft run on tools you already own. B08 then reaps the harvest: in the machine's own scheme its repeated action falls apart into a pair of one-number stretches. All four stand behind P16, the pattern that solves a problem by re-dressing it — because the best scheme to re-dress into is the one the machine itself prefers. You already own the floor from B06: tables, columns, the reverse dictionary and exactly when it breaks, the determinant's jobs. Take a pencil; you will feed a machine four arrows and catch its two favourites before you meet a single formula.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

The island is back, and so is the harbourmaster. Last page he filed dictionaries between surveyors; today he has a different contraption on his desk. The storm redrew the coastline, and his office now runs a distortion machine: feed it any map point, and it returns a new map point — the same map, the same school numbers in and out, one fixed table doing the moving. The table is

\[M \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.\]

What the table means is exactly what B06's columns always meant: the first column \((3,2)\) is what the machine does to one east-step, the second column \((1,2)\) what it does to one north-step. A machine's columns are its answers to the two smallest questions you can ask it. And a fed point is walked just as B06 taught — reading \((a,b)\) means \(a\) steps of the first column plus \(b\) of the second:

\[M\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} \;=\; a\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + b\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Task: feed the machine four arrows, by hand. An arrow is a map point seen from the lighthouse \(O\) — a step-direction, not a location. The harbourmaster wants to know what the storm did to directions on the island: did each direction survive, bent or straight? Feed it four and see. No formula beyond the walk above; do each one on paper:

  • Feed east, \((1,0)\): out comes \((3,2)\) — one step of the first column. Is the output still pointing east? No: \((3,2)\) is not a multiple of \((1,0)\). The machine knocked the arrow off its line.
  • Feed north, \((0,1)\): out comes \((1,2)\) — the second column alone. Not a multiple of \((0,1)\): knocked off its line too.
  • Feed the diagonal \((1,1)\): walk it, \((3,2) + (1,2) = (4,4)\). Stop and look: \((4,4)\) is \(4 \times (1,1)\). The output points exactly along the input — the arrow kept its line and just got stretched, four times as long.
  • Feed the cove arrow \((1,-2)\): walk it, \((3,2) - 2(1,2) = (1,-2)\). The machine returned the arrow unchanged — same line, same length, not a millimetre moved.

\[M\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad M\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Two feeds ended off the line; two feeds ended on it. That asymmetry is the whole phenomenon. Most directions the machine knocks sideways, mixing the two columns into something new; but some directions it treats as private property — it leaves their line alone and only stretches them, \((4,4)\) being \(4\) times its input, and \((1,-2)\) being \(1\) times its input. Look at Fig B07.1 and see the two kinds with your eyes before naming anything: the grey arrows leave their lines; the coloured ones slide along themselves.

What the pencil just showed. A machine does not treat directions equally. There exist special directions — the machine's favourites — whose fed image never leaves their own line: the machine's answer to such an arrow is just the arrow itself times a number. Two questions now demand their turn. Which directions are the favourites — the island has infinitely many arrows, and feeding them all is not a plan? And: we found \((1,1)\) and \((1,-2)\) by luck of the harbourmaster's four choices; what law finds them? The next section names the phenomenon, and the name comes with a hunt that never fails.

2 · The idea, named

First the floor, restated from B06 in one breath: a matrix is a table whose columns are its answers to the two unit-step questions; feeding a reading means walking the columns with the reading's step-counts; the determinant is the health certificate — and a table crushes some nonzero arrow to the origin exactly when its determinant is zero (B06's Claim 3, the broken dictionary). That is all this page needs; everything new is filed structure on top of it.

Box B07 — the machine's own directions. Let \(M\) be a machine and \(\vec v \ne (0,0)\) an arrow. If the machine's answer to \(\vec v\) is the arrow itself times one number,

\[M\vec v \;=\; \lambda\,\vec v,\]

then \(\vec v\) is an eigenvector of \(M\) — a direction the machine leaves alone, its own direction — and the number \(\lambda\) is that direction's eigenvalue, the stretch factor. In words: an eigenvector is a direction the machine cannot knock off its line; its answer slides along the input's own line, and the eigenvalue says how far. The line, not the arrow, is what the machine owns — \((2,2)\) and \((1,1)\) are the same eigenvector-direction with the same eigenvalue, and \((0,0)\) is excluded on purpose: the zero arrow never moves, so it would flatter every stretch factor at once.

Box B07.A — the hunt (the characteristic equation). An arrow \(\vec v \ne (0,0)\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda\) exactly when

\[(M - \lambda I)\,\vec v \;=\; \vec 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \det(M - \lambda I) \;=\; 0,\]

because a table crushes a nonzero arrow to the origin exactly when its determinant vanishes — B06's Claim 3, one letter renamed. For a two-by-two machine the determinant on the right expands into one polynomial in \(\lambda\), the characteristic polynomial

\[\lambda^2 \;-\; (\operatorname{tr} M)\,\lambda \;+\; \det M,\]

and the hunt is two steps, always the same two: (1) solve \(\det(M - \lambda I) = 0\) for the stretch factors \(\lambda\); (2) for each \(\lambda\), solve \((M - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0\) for the direction \(\vec v\). The hunt never fails to answer: the verdict is two distinct factors, one doubled factor, or none — and each verdict is honest, as section 4 proves.

Four words get defined here, at first use:

  • New word: eigenvector. The arrow-direction the machine leaves alone, \(M\vec v = \lambda\vec v\) — Box B07's whole content in one noun. Eigen is German for own (the history note below tells how late the word arrived): the direction is the machine's own property, not the surveyor's choice. Every nonzero arrow on that line counts as the same eigenvector; the machine owns lines, not lengths.
  • New word: eigenvalue. The stretch factor \(\lambda\): how many times its own length the machine returns an arrow on the line. \(\lambda = 1\) means returned unchanged (the cove arrow), \(\lambda = 4\) means stretched four times (the diagonal), and \(\lambda = 0\) would mean crushed to the origin — possible only for a broken machine, since Box B07.A with \(\lambda = 0\) demands \(\det M = 0\).
  • New word: characteristic polynomial. The one-polynomial summary \(\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} M)\lambda + \det M\): its roots are exactly the machine's eigenvalues, no more and no fewer. The name goes back to Cauchy's racine caractéristique, the root of the characteristic equation — the history note below.
  • New word: trace. The diagonal sum \(\operatorname{tr} M = a + d\) — the two entries the machine answers its own unit-steps with, added. It appears in the characteristic polynomial with its sign flipped, and (Claim 2) the stretch factors always sum to it.

Examples and non-examples, one per line, each checkable against the boxes:

  • The distortion machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\): characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 4)\); favourites \((1,-2)\) with \(\lambda = 1\) and \((1,1)\) with \(\lambda = 4\) — Lecture 0's luck, now found by law (section 3).
  • Identity and triple, \(I\) and \(3I\): \((1 - \lambda)^2\) and \((3 - \lambda)^2\), and step two of the hunt dissolves — every direction is an eigenvector, stretched \(\times 1\) and \(\times 3\). The machines that never knock. Worth the P03 glance: the identity is the floor every special case gets checked against.
  • The shear \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\): \((1 - \lambda)^2\) again — but step two returns east alone: the doubled factor owns exactly one direction. Two distinct roots would have promised two rails; one doubled root keeps its promise, barely.
  • The quarter-turn \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\): \(\lambda^2 + 1\), no real roots, no eigen-direction at all. The honest non-example: a machine can own nothing it leaves alone, and the hunt says so before a single arrow is fed.

The names carry a history worth one paragraph, none of it load-bearing. The phenomenon is older than every one of its names: Euler, in the eighteenth century, analysed rotating bodies and found they spin most smoothly about certain axes — the principal axes; Lagrange showed those axes are exactly the eigen-directions of the table of inertia. Cauchy, in 1829, gave the roots their lasting name — racine caractéristique, the root of what is now called the characteristic equation — generalized the idea to any number of dimensions, and proved that a real symmetric table always has real roots; Hermite widened the guarantee in 1855. Sylvester — the same law-office Sylvester who named the matrix in B06's history note — coined latent roots in 1883, a name still heard in engineering. The German prefix arrived last and won: Hilbert, in 1904, made eigen ("own") the standard usage; English texts before him said proper value, and the odd proper survives here and there. None of this carries the page's weight — hunt, file, verify — but the law-office thread continues: the machines are named, and the names were earned after hours.

3 · Worked by hand

Three workings, simplest first, each carried to the end. Under all of them run Boxes B07 and B07.A.

(a) The hunt, end to end, on the distortion machine

Run both steps of Box B07.A on \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), with a pencil. Step one, the stretch factors. Write the characteristic equation and expand honestly:

\[\det(M - \lambda I) \;=\; \begin{vmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 2 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \;=\; (3-\lambda)(2-\lambda) - 2 \;=\; \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4 \;=\; (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 4).\]

Roots: \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = 4\) — and both numbers were already on the desk in Lecture 0: the cove arrow came back unchanged (\(\times 1\)), the diagonal came back four times as long (\(\times 4\)). Luck found them; the law finds them. While the expansion is warm, glance at the coefficients: the roots sum to \(5 = 3 + 2\), the machine's trace, and multiply to \(4 = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 2\), its determinant. Claim 2 says that is no coincidence — it is the hunt's built-in check, and it never costs more than a glance.

Step two, the directions. Each stretch factor owns its rail; find it by solving \((M - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0\). For \(\lambda = 4\):

\[(M - 4I)\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad -a + b = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec v = (1,1).\]

For \(\lambda = 1\):

\[(M - I)\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2a + b = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec v = (1,-2).\]

In each little table both rows say the same equation — that collapse is what a determinant-zero table looks like from the inside, and it is why the answer is a whole line of arrows rather than one point. Feed both to confirm: \(M(1,1) = (4,4) = 4 \times (1,1)\) and \(M(1,-2) = (1,-2)\) — the rails, found by law and verified by feeding, the return trip every claim on this page takes. Fig B07.2 draws the two rails and adds the census's eye: of the grid's 48 arrows — every \((a,b)\) with entries from \(-3\) to \(3\), not both zero — exactly 8 sit on a rail: six on the red (\(\pm(1,1)\), \(\pm(2,2)\), \(\pm(3,3)\)) and two on the blue (\(\pm(1,-2)\); the further multiples \(\pm(2,-4)\) fall outside the census's grid). Everything else is knocked off its line, east \((1,0)\) included: its answer \((3,2)\) lies on neither rail.

(b) Three special machines, and what the census says

P03's discipline, run on machines instead of numbers: before trusting a general claim, feed the identity, the shear, and the turn — the special cases are where a young hunt lies. Each gets the same two steps as \(M\).

The machines that never knock: \(I\) and \(3I\). Hunt the identity: \(\det(I - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2\), so \(\lambda = 1\) doubled. Step two: \(I - 1 \cdot I\) is the zero table, and the zero table crushes every arrow to the origin — every direction solves, every direction is an eigenvector, each returned exactly as it went in. The triple \(3I\) repeats the story one size up: \((3 - \lambda)^2\), \(\lambda = 3\), every direction stretched \(\times 3\). The doubled verdict is not always poverty: a pure stretch owns every direction there is.

The shear: \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\). It slides the whole plane east, keeping every horizontal line — so its census can be guessed before it is counted. The hunt: \(\det(S - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)^2\), \(\lambda = 1\) doubled, and step two says \(b = 0\): the east line alone. Count over the 48 grid arrows: exactly 6 stay on their line — \(\pm(1,0)\), \(\pm(2,0)\), \(\pm(3,0)\), every one of them fixed, \(\lambda = 1\). The doubled factor kept its promise, but barely: one rail, not two. Hold this machine tight — it is the standing warning that two roots promise two rails but one root doubled can leave a scheme with a missing direction, and the scope note returns to it.

The quarter-turn: \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\). It turns every arrow a quarter-circle, keeping every length. The hunt: \(\det(Q - \lambda I) = (0 - \lambda)(0 - \lambda) - (-1)(1) = \lambda^2 + 1\) — no real roots at all. By Box B07.A no arrow can stay on its line, and the census agrees to the last arrow: 0 of the 48. Fig B07.3 draws why no pencil will ever find a rail: every arrow on the circle comes back rotated a quarter, sliding along nothing. This is the hunt's honest empty-handed verdict, and the page does not repair it in secret: over the real numbers the turn owns nothing it leaves alone; the completion that gives it rails lives over the complex numbers, is named here, and is deferred (scope note, section 4).

(c) The machine's preferred scheme

The hunt returned two directions, and they are plainly not parallel — \((1,1)\) is not a multiple of \((1,-2)\). By B05's health test they form a scheme: the machine's own measuring scheme, the eigen-scheme. File its dictionary the B06 way — columns are the scheme's two directions read in school numbers, first column the \(\lambda = 1\) rail, second the \(\lambda = 4\) rail:

\[P \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \det P \;=\; 1 \cdot 1 - 1 \cdot (-2) \;=\; 3 \;\ne\; 0, \qquad P^{-1} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;\;\text{(Box B06.A)}.\]

Now ask the question this block exists to ask: what does the machine look like inside its own scheme? Read a point in eigen-readings, let the machine act, translate back — the round trip \(P^{-1}MP\), computed honestly:

\[P^{-1}MP \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 8 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 12 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}.\]

A diagonal table. Inside its own scheme the machine is a pure stretch — \(\times 1\) along the first column's readings, \(\times 4\) along the second, and the two readings never mix. That is what "the machine prefers this scheme" means, exactly: column by column, \(MP = PD\), where \(D\) is the stretch table — the first column of \(P\) is the \(\times 1\) direction, the second the \(\times 4\) direction, and the stretch factors file themselves along the diagonal. The equality \(P^{-1}MP = D\) is B08's harvest in one line; this page earns it, B08 spends it.

Feed a point both ways and watch the scheme earn its keep. Directly: \(M(3,3) = (12,12)\). By the scheme's three moves: read \((3,3)\) in eigen-readings, \(P^{-1}(3,3) = \frac{1}{3}(3 - 3,\; 6 + 3) = (0,3)\); stretch, \((0 \cdot 1,\; 3 \cdot 4) = (0, 12)\); translate home, \(P(0,12) = (12,12)\). Agreement — and the reading \((0,3)\) deserves the pause: it says the point sits entirely on the second rail, zero first-column content, which is visibly true, since \((3,3)\) is three diagonal steps. A point off both rails reads mixed: \((1,0)\) reads \(\left(\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}\right)\) — fractional step-counts, Elena's honesty from B06, and no less exact for being fractions.

Two closing checks. The stretch factors' product is \(1 \cdot 4 = 4 = \det M\) — Vieta's product reading, one floor deeper; the stretch table's determinant is the machine's determinant, because the round trip \(P^{-1}MP\) is a composition and B06's multiplication law says compositions multiply determinants: \(\det(P^{-1}) \cdot \det(M) \cdot \det(P) = \det(M)\). The machine and its stretch table agree on the one number that prices areas — and on everything else the pair of rails can say. One feed is a stretch; what a hundred feeds do inside the scheme is B08's question, and it is one dictionary change away.

4 · Why it is true

Four claims carry this page — the hunt is honest in both directions, the characteristic polynomial really is \(\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} M)\lambda + \det M\), two distinct stretch factors give two non-parallel directions (hence a scheme), and one number, the discriminant, foretells the whole census. All get complete proofs; the first leans on B06's Claim 3 rather than repeating it.

Claim 1 — the hunt is honest, both ways. For any machine \(M\), any number \(\lambda\), and any nonzero arrow \(\vec v\): \(M\vec v = \lambda\vec v\) exactly when \(\det(M - \lambda I) = 0\). Proof: subtract the right side, and the statement becomes \((M - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0\) — "the table \(M - \lambda I\) crushes a nonzero arrow to the origin" — and B06's Claim 3 proved that a table crushes some nonzero arrow exactly when its determinant is zero: the witness arrow \((s, -q)\) exhibits it when the determinant vanishes, and the read-once failure forbids it when the determinant does not. One letter is new, \(\lambda\); the proof is B06's, leaned on and cited, not repeated. Two honest footnotes: \(\lambda = 0\) is a legal verdict — an arrow crushed flat, possible only when \(\det M = 0\) — and the zero arrow is barred from being an eigenvector on purpose, since it never moves and would flatter every \(\lambda\) at once (Box B07 said so upfront).

Claim 2 — the characteristic polynomial, and Vieta's two readings. For \(M = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}\):

\[\det(M - \lambda I) \;=\; \begin{vmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{vmatrix} \;=\; (a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) - bc \;=\; \lambda^2 - (a + d)\lambda + (ad - bc).\]

The expansion does all the work: \((a - \lambda)(d - \lambda) = ad - (a + d)\lambda + \lambda^2\), then \(bc\) subtracts off the constant. Reading the coefficients, \(\operatorname{tr} M = a + d\) and \(\det M = ad - bc\), the polynomial is \(\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr} M)\lambda + \det M\). Now Vieta's two readings, both worth saying aloud: the roots of a monic quadratic sum to the negative of the middle coefficient and multiply to the last — so if the hunt finds roots \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\), then always

\[\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \;=\; \operatorname{tr} M, \qquad \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \;=\; \det M,\]

a check the hunt gets for free on every machine it meets: \(M\)'s roots \(1, 4\) sum to \(5\) and multiply to \(4\); the symmetric machine of Exercise 1 sums \(1 + 3 = 4\), products \(3\); the shear's doubled root obeys both readings at once, \(1 + 1 = 2\), \(1 \cdot 1 = 1\). And the empty-handed verdict is printed by the polynomial too: the turn's \(\lambda^2 + 1\) has trace-coefficient \(0\) and constant \(1\) — the equation itself says "no real roots", which is exactly the census's 0 of 48 read off a line of algebra. (The two readings hold for the turn's complex roots as well, \(i\) and \(-i\) — but that is the deferred completion, named in the scope note, not part of this page's real rails.)

Claim 3 — two distinct stretch factors give a scheme. If \(M\vec v = \lambda\vec v\) and \(M\vec w = \mu\vec w\) with \(\lambda \ne \mu\), then \(\vec v\) and \(\vec w\) are not parallel — so they pass B05's health test, and the eigen-scheme of section 3(c) exists. Proof, two lines, by contradiction: suppose \(\vec w = t\vec v\) for some \(t \ne 0\). Then \(M\vec w = t\,M\vec v = \lambda t \vec v = \lambda \vec w\); but also \(M\vec w = \mu \vec w\); subtract, \((\lambda - \mu)\vec w = \vec 0\); \(\vec w \ne \vec 0\), so \(\lambda = \mu\) — contradiction. So the two directions are independent, their dictionary has \(D \ne 0\), and two columns make a scheme. This is the door to B08: whenever the hunt finds two distinct factors, the machine owns a scheme in which it is a pure stretch. The doubled case is the door that can stick — the shear owns one direction, not two, and no eigen-scheme — and the scope note below says exactly where that boundary stands.

Claim 4 — the discriminant foretells the census. The characteristic equation is a monic quadratic; completing the square at \(\operatorname{tr} M / 2\) gives its roots and their whole story in one number:

\[\lambda \;=\; \frac{\operatorname{tr} M \,\pm\, \sqrt{(\operatorname{tr} M)^2 - 4\det M}}{2}.\]

The quantity under the root, the discriminant \((\operatorname{tr} M)^2 - 4\det M\), decides everything before a single arrow is fed:

  • Positive — two distinct real factors, two rails. \(M\): \(25 - 16 = 9 > 0\), and the hunt found \((1,-2)\) and \((1,1)\). Claim 3 then guarantees the two directions are not parallel, so an eigen-scheme exists.
  • Zero — one doubled factor, \(\lambda = \operatorname{tr} M / 2\). At least one rail exists, by Claim 1 with the doubled root. And the count of directions is pinned exactly: if two non-parallel rails existed, both with the same factor \(\lambda\), then for their two-column dictionary \(P\), \(MP = \lambda P\), so \(M = \lambda P P^{-1} = \lambda I\) — the pure stretch, which owns every direction. So the doubled machine owns exactly one direction, like the shear (\(4 - 4 = 0\), east alone), or every direction, like the identity and the triple — nothing in between.
  • Negative — no real factors, no rails. The quarter-turn: \(0 - 4 = -4 < 0\), the census's 0 of 48, and the honest completion lives over the complex numbers, deferred.

The proof is the quadratic formula, which is the completed square read backwards — and an exhaustive census makes the verdicts' proportions concrete. Take all \(625\) machines with the four entries from \(-2\) to \(2\). Verdict by verdict: \(416\) come back two-rail, \(61\) own a doubled factor — five of them the pure stretches \(\lambda I\), which own every direction, and fifty-six shears, which own one — and \(148\) come back empty-handed. Nearly one machine in four owns nothing it leaves alone: the empty-handed hunt is not a freak of the turn, it is a standing verdict, and the discriminant prints it before any pencil moves.

Scope, honestly. Three boundaries. (i) This page's machines are two-by-two with real entries, because its island is the plane; three-space and beyond get bigger tables and the same two-step hunt, one floor up. (ii) The quarter-turn's empty-handed verdict has an honest completion over the complex numbers — stretch factors without real arrows to ride — named here and deferred, not repaired in secret; this page's rails are real rails only. (iii) The shear is the standing warning of the doubled case: one factor, one direction, and therefore no eigen-scheme — B05's health test detects the missing second direction exactly as it detected Daisy's collapse, and B08 handles the machines that pass while saying what to do about the ones that stick. What the page closes, it closes completely; what it defers, it names.

5 · Practice

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◐ open exploration. Hints and worked answers are collapsible. No ◆ exercise here — there is no pattern to diagnose on a concept page yet.

Exercise 1 ●○○

The symmetric machine \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\). (a) Read off its trace and determinant, write the characteristic equation, and solve it. (b) Run step two of the hunt: find the direction belonging to each stretch factor. (c) Feed each favourite and watch it slide along its own line. (d) The Vieta glance: do the roots sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant?

Hint

(a) The trace is the diagonal sum, the determinant the corner product — the equation assembles itself from them. (b) For each root \(\lambda\), subtract it from the diagonal and solve the crush equation; both rows will say the same thing, and that collapse is the direction. (c) Feeding is the return trip every claim takes. (d) One glance, two numbers.

Worked answer

(a) \(\operatorname{tr} A = 4\), \(\det A = 3\), so \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 3)\): roots \(\lambda = 1\) and \(\lambda = 3\). (b) For \(\lambda = 3\): \(A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(-a + b = 0\), direction \((1,1)\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(a + b = 0\), direction \((1,-1)\). (c) \(A(1,1) = (3,3) = 3 \times (1,1)\) — stretched on its line; \(A(1,-1) = (2 - 1,\; 1 - 2) = (1,-1)\) — returned unchanged. (d) \(1 + 3 = 4 = \operatorname{tr} A\) and \(1 \cdot 3 = 3 = \det A\): both readings hold, as Claim 2 promises.

Exercise 2 ●●○

A storm-damaged machine of unknown make, \(N = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), washes up at the harbourmaster's desk. (a) Full hunt: characteristic equation, roots, both directions. (b) Verify each favourite by feeding. (c) The census: of the 48 grid arrows — entries from \(-3\) to \(3\), not both zero — how many stay on their line? (d) The discriminant, \((\operatorname{tr})^2 - 4\det\): what does it promise, and does the census keep the promise?

Hint

(a) Two steps of Box B07.A, in order. (b) Two feeds, one per rail. (c) An arrow stays on its line exactly when it sits on one of the two rails — count the grid's multiples of each direction, and mind which multiples fall outside the grid's \(-3\) to \(3\) box. (d) Claim 4 connects the sign of one number to the count of rails — but note carefully what it promises: rails, not arrows.

Worked answer

(a) \(\operatorname{tr} N = 6\), \(\det N = 5\): \(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 5 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 5)\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(N - I = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(3a + b = 0\), direction \((1,-3)\). For \(\lambda = 5\): \(N - 5I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 3 & -3 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(-a + b = 0\), direction \((1,1)\). (b) \(N(1,-3) = (4 - 3,\; 3 - 6) = (1,-3)\), returned unchanged, \(\lambda = 1\); \(N(1,1) = (5,5) = 5 \times (1,1)\), stretched on its line. (c) Exactly 8 of the 48: six on the \((1,1)\) rail — \(\pm(1,1)\), \(\pm(2,2)\), \(\pm(3,3)\) — and two on the \((1,-3)\) rail — \(\pm(1,-3)\); the further multiples \(\pm(2,-6)\) fall outside the census's grid, so they simply do not count. (d) \(36 - 20 = 16 > 0\): two distinct real factors, two rails promised — and two rails found. The discriminant counts rails, not arrows; the census's 8 is two rails' worth of grid arrows, and the promise kept is the promise actually made.

Exercise 3 ●●●

The machine \(B = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), end to end. (a) Full hunt: equation, roots, directions. (b) Build the eigen-scheme's dictionary \(P\) — smaller stretch factor's direction first — health-check it, and write the reverse dictionary by Box B06.A. (c) Verify the diagonal claim: compute \(P^{-1}BP\) and read off what the machine does inside its own scheme. (d) Feed \((3,3)\) both ways: directly, and by the scheme's three moves — read, stretch, translate home. (e) The warranty: which of B06's claims guarantees the two answers agree, and why?

Hint

(a) The same two steps as section 3(a), new numbers. (b) Columns are the directions in school numbers; the determinant is the health check; the adjugate over the determinant is the reverse. (c) Multiply in the order written — reverse, machine, dictionary — and let the middle column of the first product do the deciding. (d) Direct is one feed; the scheme's route is three moves, and the reading may come out fractional — that is honest, B06's Elena showed why. (e) Ask what property makes "translate, act, translate back" equal "act in place".

Worked answer

(a) \(\operatorname{tr} B = 7\), \(\det B = 6\): \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 6)\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(B - I = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(2a + b = 0\), direction \((1,-2)\). For \(\lambda = 6\): \(B - 6I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\), so \(-a + 2b = 0\), direction \((2,1)\). (b) \(P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) — first column \((1,-2)\), the \(\times 1\) rail; second column \((2,1)\), the \(\times 6\) rail. \(\det P = 1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot (-2) = 5 \ne 0\), healthy; \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) by Box B06.A. (c) \[P^{-1}BP \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 12 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 30 \end{bmatrix} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix}.\] A pure stretch inside its own scheme: \(\times 1\) and \(\times 6\), no mixing — and the stretch factors' product \(6\) is \(\det B\), Vieta's second reading in the flesh. (d) Direct: \(B(3,3) = (15 + 6,\; 6 + 6) = (21,12)\). The scheme's route, three moves: read, \(P^{-1}(3,3) = \frac{1}{5}(3 - 6,\; 6 + 3) = \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{9}{5}\right)\); stretch, \(\left(-\tfrac{3}{5} \cdot 1, \tfrac{9}{5} \cdot 6\right) = \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{54}{5}\right)\); translate home, \(P\left(-\tfrac{3}{5}, \tfrac{54}{5}\right) = \left(-\tfrac{3}{5} + \tfrac{108}{5},\; -2 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{3}{5}\right) + \tfrac{54}{5}\right) = \left(\tfrac{105}{5}, \tfrac{60}{5}\right) = (21, 12)\). Agreement — and the fractional reading is Elena's honesty from B06: the eigen-scheme's steps are long, fine positions ask fractional counts, and the answer lands on whole numbers all the same. (e) B06's Claim 2, the dictionary carries the arithmetic: feeding a sum is summing the feeds, so the machine commutes with translation — translate out, act, translate back is the same machine as acting in place, and the agreement of (d) is that warranty exercised, not luck.
