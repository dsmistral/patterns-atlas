5 · When it fails

Three exhibits, one per promise. Each is a place the move genuinely breaks — not a hard problem it merely fails to solve, but a way its own logic goes wrong.

Exhibit A · The vacuous count — overflow proves nothing

Count the mono-triangle events on \(K_5\), both colors: \(\binom{5}{3} = 10\) triangles, each monochromatic with chance \(2 \cdot \frac{1}{8}\), so the expected count of bad events is \(10 \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 2.5\) — comfortably above \(1\). The method's ledger says: no conclusion. A beginner reads \(2.5\) as "the thing probably does not exist." The census says otherwise: exactly \(12\) of the \(1024\) colorings of \(K_5\) have no monochromatic triangle — the pentagon seating of \S2 and its eleven siblings. The failure is the argument's, never the object's. Worse, the failure has a mechanism: the union bound counts a coloring with three mono triangles three times, and a room full of overlapping crimes inflates the average without erasing the quiet corners. Overflow is ambiguity — the count cannot distinguish "crimes everywhere" from "crimes piled up in a few seats." When the count overflows, the honest move is to change the count, not to bury the object.

Exhibit B · The anonymous witness — existence without a name

In 1948, Claude Shannon proved that randomly built codebooks achieve channel capacity — nearly error-free communication at the fastest possible rate, a year after Erdős's seatings, and in the same dialect. What Shannon's argument does not give is the codebook itself: it certifies that good ones dominate the room while naming none of them. Coding theorists lived in that gap for sixty-one years — constructions existed, but none reached capacity — until Erdal Arıkan's polar codes (2009) finally wrote an explicit capacity-achieving code down. The Ramsey seatings are still in the gap: no explicit coloring of any \(K_n\) is known that matches what the coin certifies for \(n\) near \(2^{k/2}\), and decades of trying have not closed the distance. The honest nuance cuts the other way too: when the good fraction is overwhelming — the \(k = 10\) party, where at least \(1 - 0.0000037\) of the room is good — then sampling finds a witness almost immediately; flip the coin, check, done. Anonymous is not unreachable. What the move truly cannot do is force the witness to be simple, short, or human-readable — and for some theorems (Shannon's, until 2009) that absence lasted a working lifetime.

Exhibit C · The wrong experiment — the coin is a design choice

Off-diagonal Ramsey: \(R(3,4)\), avoiding a red triangle and a blue four-clique at once. Try the default experiment — fair coin — on \(n = 5\) people. Bad events: red triangles, \(10\) of them, chance \(p^3\) each; blue four-cliques, \(5\) of them, chance \((1-p)^6\) each. At \(p = \frac{1}{2}\): \(10 \cdot \frac{1}{8} + 5 \cdot \frac{1}{64} = 1.328125\) — overflow, and the method, tried once, says nothing about parties of five. Now bias the coin: red edges rare, \(p = 0.4\). The same sum reads \(10 \cdot 0.4^3 + 5 \cdot 0.6^6 = 0.64 + 0.233 \approx 0.873 < 1\) — witness exists, \(R(3,4) > 5\), and the experiment, not the theorem, is what changed. (The truth is \(R(3,4) = 9\): an eight-person seating avoids both — red edges form the cycle of eight plus the four opposite chords — and it has no red triangle among its \(56\) triples and no blue four-clique among its \(70\) four-groups; the lab runs both censuses.) The room was never the problem; the measure on it was. Treating "the probabilistic method" as one fixed recipe — fair coin, count, compare to one — is exactly the cargo cult this page exists to dismantle.

Scope and price of P19

Three boundaries, stated while the exhibits are still warm. (i) Existence is not construction. The move's output is a certificate that the room is not empty; Exhibit B prices the gap — sixty-one years between Shannon's random codebooks and Arıkan's named one, and the Ramsey gap still open. (ii) A successful count is a floor, never a ceiling. The \(k = 4\) row certifies \(R(4,4) > 4\) against a truth of \(18\); abundance says nothing about location, and quoting a probabilistic bound as if it approximated the truth is the classic misuse. (iii) The union bound wastes overlap, so overflow is ambiguous. Exhibit A's \(2.5\) hid twelve good colorings; when the count fails, redesign the experiment (Exhibit C) before abandoning the object. What this page defers, it names: the second-moment method — asking not just \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\) but how \(X\) concentrates — and the Lovász Local Lemma (1975), which finds good candidates even when the bad events are everywhere, and whose constructive rebirth by Moser and Tardos (2010) is the derandomization story of Example 2 at full power. The price tag, said once: P19 buys abundance cheaply and must never be cashed as if it bought a name.
