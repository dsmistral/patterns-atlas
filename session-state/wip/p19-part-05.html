3 · Name the move

P19 · Count the outcomes — prove something exists by proving a random pick finds it

When a problem jams in a way that smells like a missing witness — you can say exactly what would count as an answer, the room of candidates is honest and huge, and neither search nor census nor construction can reach inside it — do not hunt the witness. Name the room of all candidates and the precise definition of bad (a candidate that fails). Count the bad on average: run the honest thought experiment of picking a candidate at random, and sum, over every bad event, its individual chance — no independence required. Compare the average count to one: if the expected number of failures in a random candidate is less than \(1\), then some candidate has none, and the witness exists. Price the conclusion honestly: the proof licenses existence, not construction; the witness stays anonymous until someone earns it by other means.

In words: some objects are hard not because they are rare but because the room they live in is too big to search. The move has three working parts. First, the room and the crime must be nameable — every candidate counted exactly once, every bad event precisely defined, the coin flip understood as bookkeeping (a fraction of the room), never as a gamble. Second, the average must be computed by linearity — the expected count of bad events is the sum of the per-event chances, and this single-step sum is why the method survives sizes no enumeration can visit; independence is not needed and demanding it is the classic beginner's error. Third, the conclusion must be priced — what arrives is an existence claim with a quantitative floor (\S3, Box P19.A), not a name, and a count that overflows past \(1\) kills the argument, never the object. The move is the book's first that proves things about objects it cannot point at.

Box P19.A · The two readings of one inequality

Both readings certify existence, and they are the same computation wearing two costumes.

Expectation reading. Let \(X\) be the number of bad events in a random candidate: the sum of indicator variables \(X = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_m\), where \(X_i\) is \(1\) when bad event \(i\) occurs and \(0\) otherwise. Linearity of expectation gives \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[X_1] + \cdots + \mathbb{E}[X_m]\), and \(\mathbb{E}[X_i]\) is just the chance of event \(i\) — a sum of single-event chances, with no independence anywhere in sight (the ten-groups of §2 overlapped freely; the sum did not care). If \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\), then a candidate with \(X = 0\) must exist: if every candidate had \(X \ge 1\), the average of \(X\) over the room could not be below \(1\). The same step gives the quantitative floor: since \(X \ge 1\) on at most an \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)-fraction of the room, \(\Pr[X = 0] \ge 1 - \mathbb{E}[X]\) — for the \(k = 10\) party, at least a \(1 - 0.0000037\) fraction of all \(2^{496}\) seatings is good.

Probability reading. Bad events \(A_1, \ldots, A_m\); the union bound says \(\Pr[A_1 \text{ or } \cdots \text{ or } A_m] \le \Pr[A_1] + \cdots + \Pr[A_m]\) — the room covered by the bad events is at most the sum of the rooms they cover separately, because overlap is counted several times on the right and only once on the left. If that sum is below \(1\), the bad events cannot cover the whole room; the uncovered part is exactly the good candidates, and its fraction is again at least \(1 - \sum_i \Pr[A_i]\). Same inequality, two dialects: an accountant's average of crime counts, or a cartographer's bound on coverage. Read "probability" as "fraction of the room" and both dialects are pure counting — which is how Erdős's 1947 paper actually wrote them (\S7).

Reductio in disguise. The move is P11 wearing the room as its quantifier: assume every candidate is bad, average the crime count over the room, and the assumption forces an average of at least \(1\) — contradicting the computed \(\mathbb{E}[X] < 1\). The contradiction releases a fact about an unnamed object, and P11's price schedule applies unchanged: the stronger the assumption (all candidates bad), the harder it falls.

Box P19.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Ramsey theory: the §2 bound and its general form (\S4, Example 1), plus the Paley seating that makes \(R(4,4) > 17\) concrete. Graph theory: every graph hides a balanced cut holding at least half its edges (\S4, Example 2), with the derandomization walk as its deterministic echo. Analysis: Bernstein's 1912 proof of the Weierstrass approximation theorem, where the polynomial witness is the expectation (\S4, Example 3). Information theory: Shannon's 1948 random codebooks, whose witnesses stayed anonymous for sixty-one years (\S5, Exhibit B). Later doors: the Lovász Local Lemma (1975) and its constructive rebirth by Moser–Tardos (2010), and the 2015 Marcus–Spielman–Srivastava resolution of Kadison–Singer — the move's modern limit case, where the room is a polynomial family (\S5 names what it defers).

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the jam smells like a missing witness at a size beyond search and census — you can define the object, and nothing else about the room is within reach. Second, the room and the experiment are both design choices you own: name the room so every candidate is counted once, and pick the experiment — a fair coin is only the default, and a biased coin can succeed where the fair one overflows (\S5, Exhibit C). Third, the bad-event count splits into per-event averages computable without independence — one clean chance per crime, summed. One key alone is a guess; two keys, a hypothesis; all three, a plan.
