4 · Why it is true

Four claims carry this page, and all four get complete proofs: the hand-me-down (Box B03), the ending (termination), the strengthened guarantee (Box B03.A), and the speed engine (halving). The B02 conjecture's easy half follows as a corollary.

The hand-me-down, proved. Write the division as \(a = qb + r\). Suppose \(d\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\) — say \(a = d\alpha\), \(b = d\beta\). Then \(r = a - qb = d\alpha - q d\beta = d(\alpha - q\beta)\), so \(d\) divides \(r\) too: every common divisor of \((a, b)\) is a common divisor of \((b, r)\). Conversely, suppose \(d\) divides \(b\) and \(r\), say \(b = d\beta\), \(r = d\gamma\). Then \(a = qb + r = q d\beta + d\gamma = d(q\beta + \gamma)\), so \(d\) divides \(a\): every common divisor of \((b, r)\) is a common divisor of \((a, b)\). The two common-divisor families are identical — which is more than "the gcds are equal": the gcd claim of Box B03 follows because a greatest member of one family is automatically the greatest member of the other.

Termination. B01's division algorithm guarantees each leftover is a whole number with \(0 \le r < b\): strictly smaller than the number it replaces. So the dance produces a strictly decreasing sequence of nonnegative whole numbers — \(a > b > r_1 > r_2 > \dots \ge 0\) — and such a sequence cannot go on forever: if it did, its members would form a nonempty set of whole numbers with no smallest element, which B01's well-ordering principle forbids. The dance must end, and it ends only when a division comes out exact (leftover 0).

The output is the gcd — and more. Let \(g\) be the last nonzero leftover, and let the pairs of the dance be \((a, b), (b, r_1), (r_1, r_2), \dots, (r_k, g), (g, 0)\). The final division reads \(r_k = q \times g + 0\), so \(g\) divides \(r_k\), and \(g\) divides itself — \(g\) is a common divisor of the pair \((r_k, g)\). Now climb: by the hand-me-down, the common divisors of \((r_k, g)\) are exactly the common divisors of the pair above it, and those exactly the common divisors of the pair above that, all the way up to \((a, b)\). Repeating this observation up the finite chain (the formal name for such repetition is induction, P66 — here the chain is finitely long, so the climb is a finite list of identical steps, spelled out in the lab for every worked example), we get the whole story at once:

\[\text{common divisors of } (a, b) \;=\; \text{divisors of } g.\]

So \(g\) is a common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\), every other common divisor is at most \(g\) (it divides \(g\), so it cannot exceed it), and the greatest common divisor exists for every pair — the dance both computes the gcd and proves it exists. The equality above deserves boxing, because one of its halves is the fact the next bridge is built on:

Box B03.A — every common divisor divides the gcd. If \(d \mid a\) and \(d \mid b\), then \(d \mid \gcd(a, b)\).

In words: the gcd is not merely the biggest common divisor — it is a multiple of every one of them. For 1071 and 462: the common divisors are \(\{1, 3, 7, 21\}\), and each divides 21. Proof: \(d\) is a common divisor of every pair in the dance (the hand-me-down, downwards), in particular of \((g, 0)\) — and the common divisors of \((g, 0)\) are exactly the divisors of \(g\). The lab verifies the box over every pair \(a, b \le 120\): 23,344 common-divisor checks, zero failures.

The speed engine: two steps at least halve. Claim: after two divisions, the second member of the pair is less than half of what it was. Concretely: from \((a, b)\), the first division leaves \(r = a - qb\) with \(0 \le r < b\); the second leaves \(r' = b - q'r\) with \(0 \le r' < r\). Then \(r' < b/2\), in two cases. If \(r \le b/2\), then \(r' < r \le b/2\) — done. If \(r > b/2\), then \(r\) fits into \(b\) only once (\(2r > b\)), so the second division's quotient is \(q' = 1\) and \(r' = b - r < b/2\) — done. The lab sweeps every pair \(a, b \le 500\) for which both steps exist: 122,060 two-step cases, zero violations. This is the engine behind Lamé's theorem in section 3(d): halving every two steps means the dance's length grows with the number of digits of the inputs, not with their size — ten times bigger, about seven more divisions; a thousand times bigger, about twenty.

B02's conjecture, easy half. Claim: every invertible class mod \(n\) is coprime to \(n\). Proof: if the class of \(a\) is invertible, some class \(y\) has \(ay \equiv 1 \pmod{n}\) — meaning \(n\) divides \(ay - 1\), so \(ay - 1 = nk\) for some whole number \(k\). Let \(d\) be any common divisor of \(a\) and \(n\). Then \(d\) divides \(ay\) (it divides \(a\)) and \(d\) divides \(nk\) (it divides \(n\)), so \(d\) divides their difference \(ay - nk\). But \(ay - nk = ay - (ay - 1) = 1\). So \(d \mid 1\), meaning \(d = 1\): the only common divisor is 1, which is the definition of coprime. Complete. The lab re-checks the census data of B02's Exercise 4 through this lens: mod 7, 10 and 12, the invertible classes \(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), \(\{1,3,7,9\}\) and \(\{1,5,7,11\}\) are exactly the classes coprime to the modulus, on the nose — 14 classes, every one passing the test.

Scope, honestly. The hard half of B02's conjecture — coprime classes are invertible — is not proved on this page, and the page does not pretend otherwise. The proof needs the fact that the gcd is not just divisible-by-all but reachable as a combination \(ax + by\): that is Box B03.A's deeper use, and building it (the dance run backwards, turning the (2026, 1491) chain into an equation that exhibits 1321 instead of brute-forcing it) is B04's entire job, together with Euclid's lemma — the debt P05's page named when it reduced fractions. Default until then: the dance tests coprimality; it does not yet certify inverses.
