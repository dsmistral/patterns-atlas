Polynomial identity power

Two students expand the same product and disagree on one coefficient; nobody wants to redo twenty-four multiplications. A rival claims a formula for the sum of the first \(n\) fifth powers, and testing it at every integer would take a lifetime. The move this page teaches refuses both chores: an identity — a claim that two polynomial expressions are the same object at every input — is certified not by expanding but by evaluating. The engine is pure school mathematics: a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) cannot vanish at more than \(d\) points, because every root donates a linear factor and the degree budget runs out. So agreement at \(d + 1\) points forces agreement everywhere — the room of places a false difference could hide is too small to hold \(d + 1\) crimes. This page cashes the certificate on a four-consecutive-integers surprise, buys Vandermonde's identity with four small sums instead of one heroic expansion, and hands a sixteen-variable determinant identity of Lewis Carroll to a coin — the very coin P19 taught you to flip, now certifying algebra instead of existence. The wreckage gets three exhibits: a five-hour clock where a complete census certifies nothing, a forgery that passes any test set you fixed in advance, and a parabola that agrees at infinitely many points and is still not zero.

New word: monomial. A monomial is a single term — a coefficient times a power product, like \(3x^2y\) or \(-7x^4\). A polynomial is a sum of monomials, and monomials are the room's census unit: the number of monomials of degree at most \(d\) in \(n\) variables counts exactly how many independent coefficients a polynomial of that shape can carry (\S3 counts this room twice). Class-12 algebra already owns the two facts this page leans on hardest: the factor theorem — \(P(a) = 0\) exactly when \((x - a)\) divides \(P(x)\) — and its census consequence, that a polynomial equation of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) roots. New word: identity. An identity is the claim that two expressions are the same object: their difference is the zero polynomial — every coefficient zero, so it vanishes at every input, always. An equation asks where a difference vanishes; an identity asserts the difference is nothing at all. The two feel similar and are worlds apart: solving an equation hunts through the room of inputs, while proving an identity is a claim about the whole room at once. New word: vanishing set. The vanishing set of a polynomial \(P\) is the set of inputs where \(P\) evaluates to \(0\) — the roots, gathered into one object. The school fact above, restated in the room language of P19: the vanishing set of a nonzero one-variable polynomial of degree \(d\) holds at most \(d\) points. The crimes a false identity can commit are few, and counting them — not hunting them — is the entire move.
