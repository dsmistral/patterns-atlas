The Patterns Atlas · P17 · the seventeenth move of the spine

Flip to the dual

Every problem about points has a secret twin that is a problem about lines — every counting question about vertices has one about faces, every statement about unions one about intersections. The twin is not far away in another subject, the way P16's solved sibling was; it is the same problem, stood on its head, with the roles of its objects traded. The seventeenth move is the trade itself, done honestly: write down the dictionary that swaps the roles, check that it carries the problem's relations — and notice that it carries some of them backwards — then solve whichever side is easier and read the other side's answer off for free. A theorem about points becomes a theorem about lines without proving anything new. A census of five solids becomes two pairs and a loner. Half of set algebra proves the other half. The flip is the cheapest machine mathematics owns for manufacturing theorems — and this page builds it, checks it, and shows exactly where its fine print bites.

New word: dual. The dual of a problem or statement is its mirror: the same question asked with the roles of the two kinds of object traded — points for lines, vertices for faces, unions for intersections. "Dual" is an adjective hunting for its dictionary: calling a statement a dual licenses nothing until the trade is written down and checked, which is this page's whole business. New word: incidence. Incidence is the bare relation between points and lines: a point lies on a line, a line passes through a point. Nothing about distance, order, or size — just the on/through fact. Incidence is the relation the point-line flip carries, and it is all it carries. New word: involution. An involution is a flip that comes home: doing it twice returns exactly what you started with. Complementing a set twice gives the set back; flipping a point to a line and back gives the point back. The double flip is the third promise a dual must keep, and the failures of that promise are the most instructive wreckage on this page.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far, one breath: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on, P13 convicted whole families by manufacturing ever-smaller criminals, P14 built the one object no list can contain, P15 trapped the unwritable between two computable walls, and P16 licensed whole problems to change clothes. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P16, because the flip is the dictionary move's mirror twin: P16 translated a problem into a solved problem's clothes, and this page discovers that some problems carry their own twin inside them — the dictionary here runs from a problem to its own roles-swapped image, and every one of P16's three promises (one-for-one, relations carried, both directions) is reused as-is. P09, because a flip that changes the sign of a quantity leaves its zero untouched — the determinant ahead flips from \(21\) to \(-21\), and "equals zero" survives the flip the way invariants survive everything. And P03, because a flipped statement is a claim, and claims are attacked: the noun-swapping version of the flip produces falsehoods on demand, and the hostile cases are where the honest version earns its keep.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Every geometry book states theorems about points and then, mysteriously, also states a second theorem that is the first one with the words swapped — one theorem about three lines meeting at a point for every theorem about three points lying on a line, a face-counting formula for every vertex-counting formula. The mysterious double bookkeeping is not a coincidence of subject matter; it is a machine, and the machine has a shape you can check. The good news: nothing new needs building. The flip's dictionary is one line long, its checks are P16's checks with the words traded, and the harvest — theorems arriving free, censuses halving, half an algebra proving the other half — is the largest in the book per line of work spent. The bad news, paid up front: the machine's fine print is where all the danger lives. The naive version — swap the nouns and hope — is a lie generator, and even the honest version carries exactly the relations it was checked to carry, backwards, and nothing else. The fine print gets three exhibits, and the exhibits are the page's real content.

2 · The same question, asked twice

Two questions, cold. Question one. Do the points \((2,5)\), \((3,9)\), and \((7,4)\) lie on a single line? You know how this goes — P02's table work and P09's determinant, expanded along the first row:

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 3 & 9 & 1 \\ 7 & 4 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 2(9-4) - 5(3-7) + 1(12-63) = 10 + 20 - 51 = -21. \]

Three points lie on one line exactly when this determinant vanishes; here it is \(-21\), so they do not — no single line through all three, and the figure's left panel shows the triangle they form instead.

Question two. Do the lines \(y = 2x-5\), \(y = 3x-9\), and \(y = 7x-4\) pass through a single point? The same determinant test exists for lines — write each line as \(px - y = q\) and take the determinant of the coefficient rows \([p,\,-1,\,-q]\):

\[ \begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 & -5 \\ 3 & -1 & -9 \\ 7 & -1 & -4 \end{vmatrix} = 2(4-9) + 1(3\cdot(-4)-7\cdot(-9)) - 5(3-7) = -10 + 51 + 20 = 21. \]

Three lines pass through one point exactly when this one vanishes; here it is \(+21\), so they do not. But stop and look at what you just computed. Question one's answer was \(-21\). Question two's answer was \(+21\). Same magnitude, opposite sign — and not because I chose the examples to force it. Take any triple, say the points \((1,2)\), \((2,3)\), \((3,4)\): the point determinant is \(0\) (they sit on the line \(y = x+1\)), and their partnered lines \(x - y = 2\), \(2x - y = 3\), \(3x - y = 4\) have line determinant \(0\) too, meeting all three at the single point \((1,-1)\). Vanishing together, non-vanishing together, always the same number up to sign. These two questions are not two questions. They are one question wearing two uniforms — the exact phrasing belongs to P16.

Here is the dictionary doing the tailoring. To each point \((p,q)\) attach the line \(px - y = q\), that is \(y = px - q\). To each line \(y = mx + c\) attach the point \((m,-c)\). Point \((2,5)\) becomes the line \(y = 2x - 5\); that line becomes the point \((2,5)\) again. Flip twice, arrive home — the involution from the onramp, checked on one example, proved in §4. The three points of question one become, under the dictionary, exactly the three lines of question two; that is why the answers match. One dictionary, one line long, and a whole family of question-pairs collapse into single questions.

Now the trap, because there is one, and it is the reason this page exists as a move rather than a curiosity. The oldest true statement of geometry is: any two distinct points lie on exactly one line. Flip it by swapping the nouns, the way you would flip any sentence: any two distinct lines pass through exactly one point. That flipped statement is false — the lines \(y = x\) and \(y = x+1\) never meet. Hostile case, one line of arithmetic, and the naive flip is dead on arrival. Now flip the same statement through the dictionary, and watch the difference: two distinct points \((p_1,q_1)\) and \((p_2,q_2)\) become two lines with slopes \(p_1\) and \(p_2\), and those lines meet exactly when the slopes differ. When the two points share a first coordinate, their duals are parallel and the flipped statement fails — for the same reason \(y = x\) and \(y = x+1\) fail. The dictionary does not erase the exception; it moves it. Points stacked vertically (equal \(p\)) trade places with parallel line families (equal slope), and the repair that heals one heals the other — §4 names it. What the dictionary does that noun-swapping cannot: it keeps the bookkeeping exact, so the exception arrives labeled, as a visible clause about equal first coordinates, instead of hiding inside an English sentence that quietly claimed any two lines. The naive noun-swap erased the fine print and paid for it with a falsehood. This is the tension of §1 in miniature: the flip is a machine, the machine has fine print, and the difference between executing the flip through a checked dictionary and performing it as wordplay on English is the difference between a theorem and a lie.

A skeptic should now object, and the objection is the right one: two matched examples and one worked observation do not make a machine, the same way one matched pair of cities did not make P16's dictionary a theorem. What is the dictionary certified to carry? Where is it defined, and where does it tear? Which relations does it preserve, and which does it reverse? And why would anyone bother — what does the flip buy? The next three sections answer in order: name the move and its promises (\S3), watch it work and certify the fine print (\S4), then study the wreckage where it fails (\S5). The pattern to hold onto is the one P16 established: a correspondence is only as good as its checks, and the checks are the content.

3 · Name the move

P17 · Flip to the dual — solve the mirrored problem instead

When a subject's objects come in two paired kinds — points and lines, vertices and faces, unions and intersections — write the dictionary that trades their roles, and certify it the P16 way: one-for-one on objects, relations carried, both directions honest. Then read every theorem, every census, and every unsolved problem through it. A proof on one side is a proof on the other; a count on one side is a count on the other; a hard problem on one side is worth a glance at its twin before you pay full price. The flip carries the relations it was checked to carry — some of them forwards, some of them backwards — and it carries nothing else, which is a promise and a warning at once.

In words: some problems are left-handed and some are right-handed, and for whole families of them the two versions are mirror images with the same skeleton. You can prove the left-handed version, close the notebook, and hand the right-handed version away for free — if the mirror is a real, checked dictionary and not a vibe that the statements rhyme. The move has three working parts. First, write the trade explicitly: which object becomes which, one line, no hand-waving — the dictionary \((p,q) \mapsto (px - y = q)\) is the entire cost of admission for half of plane geometry. Second, certify what the trade carries: incidence? yes, and symmetrically; order and distance? no; containment? yes, but reversed — a flip that preserves containment is a broken flip, the way a mirror that does not reverse your handedness is a window. Third, collect: theorems, counts, and reductions arrive in pairs, and you only ever pay for one.

Box P17.A · The three promises, mirrored

These are P16's three promises, restated for a dictionary that runs from a subject to its own roles-traded image — each one a checkable requirement, not a metaphor.

Promise 1 — the trade is explicit and comes home. The dictionary is written down, object by object, one-for-one; and flipping twice returns the original object exactly. \((p,q) \mapsto y = px - q \mapsto (p,q)\). A set and its complement; a solid and its swapped \((q,p)\). If the double flip lands somewhere else, or only lands home "up to" hand-waving, you do not have a dual — you have an analogy wearing one.

Promise 2 — the relations are carried, some of them backwards. Every relation the flip was checked on translates — but direction-having relations reverse. Incidence is symmetric: \(P\) lies on the dual of \(A\) exactly when the dual of \(P\) passes through \(A\). Containment is not: \(A \subseteq B\) flips to \(B' \subseteq A'\), the two ends trade places. Anything with an arrow — inclusion, implication, "goes into" — comes out pointing the other way. That reversal is the signature of the honest flip; a dictionary that carries arrows unchanged is preserving something, not dualizing it.

Promise 3 — a proof on one side is a proof on the other. No theorem about points needs re-proving for lines. This is the harvest that makes the move worth its fine print, and it is also the audit trail: every "free" theorem you claim must be traceable to a checked dictionary carrying a checked relation. Free theorems from an unchecked dictionary are not free; they are unpriced.

Box P17.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Points and lines (this page, from 1639). Vertices and faces of polyhedra — \((p,q) \mapsto (q,p)\) swaps the census of corners for the census of faces, §4. Unions and intersections of sets — complement each side of a set identity and read off its twin, §4. "For all" and "there exists" — negate a quantifier and the other kind appears, P11's native country. Products and coproducts at the frontier (P31), a function and its Fourier pair (P24), a curve and its tangent-line family (Legendre, §7), a field extension and its Galois group — inclusion reversed there too. The flip is one move wearing many uniforms, which is itself P16's lesson.

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the subject has two paired kinds of object, and the pairing is grammatical: on/in, corner/face, union/intersection — the roles sound traded even in plain English. Second, the relations are listable: you can write the subject's axioms and run the flip through each one, because an unchecked flip is a lie generator (§2 already killed one). Third, the double flip makes sense: trade the roles twice and you expect to come home. If you cannot even say what trading twice does, there is no dictionary, only a rhyme — and rhymes do not carry proofs.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · Points and lines — certifying the dictionary

Moves used: P02 (the census), P09 (the determinant test), P16 (the certification protocol).

Time to pay for the promises of §3, in public, with the checks written out. The dictionary, its domain, and its inverse:

ObjectBecomesCheck
point \((p,q)\)line \(px - y = q\), i.e. \(y = px - q\)slope \(p\), intercept \(-q\)
line \(y = mx + c\)point \((m,-c)\)the same rule, read backwards
line \(x = c\) (vertical)—the gap: no \(m\) exists

Promises 1 and 2, checked. The double flip comes home exactly: \((p,q) \mapsto y = px - q\), which is \(y = mx + c\) with \(m = p\), \(c = -q\), which maps to \((m,-c) = (p,q)\). One line of arithmetic, involution certified, on every point and on every non-vertical line — the vertical lines are the honest gap, flagged in the table and repaired below. Incidence carries, symmetrically: the point \(P = (a,b)\) lies on the dual line of \(A = (p,q)\) precisely when \(pa - b = q\); the dual line of \(P\) is \(ax - y = b\), and it passes through \(A\) precisely when \(ap - q = b\). Rearranged, both say \(ap = b + q\) — the same equation, so each incidence holds exactly when the flipped one does. The relation is carried, and carried without even reversing; §4's set-theoretic twin will show the reversing kind.

The determinant theorem. §2's observation is now a theorem: for any three points, the line determinant of their duals is the negative of the point determinant. Proof in four moves on the point matrix with rows \([p,\; q,\; 1]\): multiply column 2 by \(-1\) (determinant multiplied by \(-1\)), multiply column 3 by \(-1\) (multiplied by \(-1\) again — net \(+1\)), obtaining rows \([p,\; -q,\; -1]\); swap columns 2 and 3 (multiplied by \(-1\)), obtaining rows \([p,\; -1,\; -q]\) — exactly the line matrix. The column scalings cancel, the swap pays a single minus sign: \(L = -P\), always. It is P09 standing on its head: the determinant is not an invariant here, but its zero is, and "the zero is what the theorem needed" is a lesson P09 already taught. Collinear points, concurrent duals, one test, one theorem, proved never to be a coincidence.

The repair, named. The gap is real and has a shape. Points with the same first coordinate — stacked vertically — map to parallel lines (same slope, different intercepts), and parallel lines meet nowhere the affine plane can see. Vertical lines are nobody's dual at all. The repair, which took mathematics until 1639 and Desargues: extend the plane by one point at infinity per direction. Parallel lines of slope \(p\) "meet" at the infinity-point of direction \(p\); the vertical lines all pass through the infinity-point of the vertical direction. In the extended plane every two distinct lines meet exactly once, the gap closes, and the flipped statement of §2 becomes true without exceptions — at the price of a new geometry, the projective plane, which §5 prices honestly. The repair is not decoration; it is the census's missing ledger entry.

The census. Take all sixteen points \((p,q)\) with \(p, q \in \{0,1,2,3\}\) — a \(4 \times 4\) lattice — and ask P02's question: of the \(\binom{16}{3} = 560\) triples, how many are collinear? Count by slope: 4 horizontal lines × \(\binom{4}{3} = 4\) triples each gives 16; 4 vertical lines give 16; the slope-1 lines (the main diagonal, the two flanking diagonals of length 3) give \(4 + 1 + 1 = 6\); slope \(-1\), by the same mirror argument, gives 6. Total: \(16 + 16 + 6 + 6 = 44\) collinear triples out of 560 — and no other slope can fit three lattice points in a window four wide. Now flip the census through the dictionary. The 28 triples on non-vertical lines dualize to 28 triples of lines concurrent at an ordinary point (the dual of the line they shared — the determinant theorem guarantees it). The 16 triples on vertical lines dualize to 16 families of parallel lines — concurrent only at infinity. The two ledgers match exactly: 44 collinear point-triples, 44 "concurrent" line-triples once the repair is paid for, 28 ordinary and 16 at infinity on each side. A census that would take an afternoon by hand on the line side is read off the point side for free, and the fine print is not an embarrassment — it is the most informative part, because the 16 is exactly the size of the gap.

The flagship harvest: Ceva and Menelaus. The oldest one-two punch in the subject. Menelaus's theorem (second century, in a book on spheres): take a triangle, mark one point on each sideline; the three marks are collinear precisely when the product of directed ratios around the triangle equals \(-1\). Ceva's theorem (1678): take a triangle, draw one line through each vertex; the three lines are concurrent precisely when the product of directed ratios equals \(+1\). Look at what the second statement is: points-on-sidelines traded for lines-through-vertices, collinear traded for concurrent — the first theorem, flipped, found and proved 1,578 years later. On the projective side of the repair they are one theorem; the sign difference is the affine fine print, directed lengths, not a difference of content. Concretely: in triangle \(A(0,0)\), \(B(6,0)\), \(C(0,4)\), the cevians through \(P(2,1)\) meet the opposite sides with ratios \(BD/DC = 3/4\), \(CE/EA = 5/3\), \(AF/FB = 4/5\) — product \(1\), so the lines are concurrent, and indeed they all pass through \(P\). The transversal \(x + y = 3\) meets the same sidelines with ratios \(1\), \(1/3\), and \(-3\) — product \(-1\), so the three meeting points are collinear, and indeed they all sit on \(x + y = 3\). Two tests, two signs, one machine: the flip certified in §4 is the reason a proof of either statement is a proof of both.

Example 2 · Solids — the census that halves

Moves used: P02 (the census), P03 (the boundary case), P08 (the twin swap). Euler's formula is taken as given.

Regular solids come with two numbers stitched in: \(p\), the sides of each face, and \(q\), the faces meeting at each vertex. Not every pair is possible — at every vertex, the \(q\) angles of a regular \(p\)-gon must fall short of a full turn: \(q(p-2)\cdot 180/p < 360\), which rearranges to \(1/p + 1/q > 1/2\). Census all \(p, q \ge 3\) against that inequality and exactly five pairs survive: \((3,3)\), \((4,3)\), \((3,4)\), \((5,3)\), \((3,5)\) — tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron. The first hostile case to die at the boundary is \((3,6)\): three hexagons at a vertex total exactly \(360^\circ\), not less — and that flat case is not a failure of the census, it is the hexagonal tiling of the plane, the boundary between solid and floor.

Now count. Each face carries \(p\) edges and each edge lies on 2 faces: \(2E = pF\). Each vertex touches \(q\) edges and each edge serves 2 vertices: \(2E = qV\). Together with Euler's \(V - E + F = 2\) these pin every count from \((p,q)\) alone: \(E = 2pq/(2p+2q-pq)\), \(V = 4p/(2p+2q-pq)\), \(F = 4q/(2p+2q-pq)\). The ledger:

\((p,q)\)Vertex sumVEFSolid

(4, 3)270°8126cube

(3, 5)300°123020icosahedron

And here is the flip, free of charge: the dictionary \((p,q) \mapsto (q,p)\). Promise 1 — it is an involution, swapping twice returns \((p,q)\). Promise 2 — look at what it carries: \(V\) and \(F\) trade places (the formulas for \(V\) and \(F\) are the same expression with \(p\) and \(q\) traded), \(E\) stays fixed (its formula is symmetric), and the census itself carries, because the inequality \(1/p + 1/q > 1/2\) is symmetric too. So the flip of the cube \((4,3)\) is the octahedron \((3,4)\) — \(8, 12, 6\) becomes \(6, 12, 8\), corners traded for faces — the flip of the dodecahedron \((5,3)\) is the icosahedron \((3,5)\), and the tetrahedron flips to itself, the one self-dual solid. The harvest is exactly the halving of §3's promise: to census the regular solids you enumerate only the cases with \(p \le q\) — three survivors, \((3,3)\), \((3,4)\), \((3,5)\) — and the flip hands you the other two without a single new angle computation. Five solids in the universe, and you paid for three. The census even explains the lonely one: the tetrahedron is the only self-dual solid because it is the only survivor with \(p = q\).

Example 3 · Sets — the algebra that proves its other half

Moves used: P11 (negation as an operation), P02 (the exhaustive census), P16 (the certification protocol).

Fix a universe \(U\) and flip every set to its complement: \(S \mapsto S'\), everything in \(U\) that is not in \(S\). Promise 1 first, and it is one line: \((S')' = S\) — the complement of the complement is the set itself. The flip comes home. Promise 2, the carried relation: membership flips to non-membership, \(x \in S \iff x \notin S'\), and — here is the reversal — inclusion turns around: \(A \subseteq B\) if and only if \(B' \subseteq A'\). Everything outside the bigger set is inside the smaller set's complement; the arrow trades ends. This is Promise 2's signature from Box P17.A, live: incidence in Example 1 carried symmetrically, containment here carries backwards, and the reversal is not a defect but the very mark that the flip is a dual rather than a copy.

The carried laws now pay rent. De Morgan — element by element, two lines: \(x \in (A \cup B)'\) means \(x \notin A \cup B\), so \(x \notin A\) and \(x \notin B\), so \(x \in A'\) and \(x \in B'\), so \(x \in A' \cap B'\); every step an iff. Now take the distributive law you already own, \(A \cap (B \cup C) = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap C)\), and flip both sides through the complement, pushing De Morgan through the syntax: the left side's complement is \(A' \cup (B' \cap C')\); the right side's complement is \((A' \cup B') \cap (A' \cup C')\). Equals complement to equals — and since complementing is an involution, un-flip by trading every set for its complement (a bijection on sets, Promise 1 again): \(A \cup (B \cap C) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup C)\). The second distributive law, the "other" law in every textbook's table, is the first one run through the machine — proved in this paragraph without a single new element-chase. Half of the identity tables in set algebra are the other half, flipped; the quantifier laws say the same thing, \(\lnot\forall = \exists\lnot\), which is P11's home turf: negation itself is a flip.

And the census, exhaustive because the universe is small: with \(U = \{1,2,3,4\}\) there are 16 subsets, hence 256 ordered pairs \((A,B)\) — the machine in this page's lab runs all of them, and De Morgan plus the inclusion reversal hold in every single one — and 4,096 triples \((A,B,C)\), enough to confirm both distributive laws by brute force. The flip's fine print is thinner here than in Example 1: the complement is defined on every subset, no gaps, no points at infinity needed. When the dictionary is total, the fine print shrinks to a sentence; when it is not, the fine print is the story. Both facts are worth knowing, and §5 is where the difference earns its keep.

5 · When it fails — three exhibits of fine print

The checks of §4 are the whole license. Here is what happens at the edge of the license, in three exhibits — each one a way the flip gets used beyond what was paid for, each one with its wreckage on display.

Exhibit A · Relations the certificate never mentions. Take the points \((1,1)\), \((2,2)\), \((3,3)\), sitting on the line \(y = x\), equally spaced, with \((2,2)\) between the other two. Flip them: the duals are \(y = x-1\), \(y = 2x-2\), \(y = 3x-3\), three lines through the point \((1,0)\) — incidence carried, concurrency certified, exactly as §4 promised. Now ask what else traveled. Betweenness: the certificate cannot even state it — "lies on" is the only relation it was checked on, and "between" is not incidence. The slopes of the three dual lines come out \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), in order — a tempted reader will call that the dual of the point order, and for this dictionary it even follows from the formula, since slope is the first coordinate. But it is a fact about the formula, not a certified carry, and facts you derive off-license must be re-derived and re-checked, never assumed. Distance is blunter: the \(\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{2}\) spacing of the points has no dual counterpart at all — three concurrent lines have no notion of "spacing" for the dictionary to produce, because the certificate has no distance clause. The lesson is the one P16 taught about dictionaries that carry: the checklist is exhaustive. What is not on it does not travel, even when it looks free, even when a worked example happens to rhyme.

Exhibit B · The flip performed by wordplay. §2 already killed the naive noun-swap once; here is the autopsy. Wordplay flips are seductive precisely because they often work — "three points lie on one line" flips cleanly to "three lines pass through one point," and the dictionary backs both. The failure only surfaces when a parallel pair slips in, and by then the habit is formed and the lie is already on the page. The repair is not to abandon the flip but to pay its full price: extend the plane with one point at infinity per direction, so parallel lines of slope \(p\) meet at the infinity-point of direction \(p\), vertical lines included. The flipped statement of §2 becomes true without exceptions, the 16 parallel families of the §4 census get their missing ledger entry, and the cost is a genuinely new geometry — the projective plane, with its own axioms, its own theorems, and its own centuries of development. This page uses the repair, names it, and does not pretend it is free: building it properly is another book's work.

Exhibit C · The equally-priced twin. The flip's economics fail when both sides cost the same. The point determinant and the line determinant are the same arithmetic with one sign changed — flip a collinearity problem and you receive a concurrency problem of identical difficulty, which is why §2's observation charmed but did not help. The flip pays when the two sides genuinely differ: when one census is half of another (Example 2), when one proof is already written (Example 3), when one side is where your tools live. The honest price tag: before paying for a hard problem, glance at its twin and check the twin's price too — the glance is nearly free, and it is the default habit of §6. And the deepest version of the trap: Desargues's theorem is self-dual — flip it and you get the same theorem back, so the flip contributes nothing, and its proof needs the 3D lift of the next page. A dual that maps a problem to itself is a mirror showing you your own face: worth knowing, worth nothing as a shortcut.

Scope and price of P17

What the move gives. A checked dictionary between two paired kinds of object; theorems, censuses, and reductions arriving in pairs; direction-having relations carried backwards as a signature; halves of identity tables proved by the other half.

What it does not give, honestly. First, no dictionary means no flip: statements that merely rhyme ("for every even number, two primes" trading "sum" for "difference") are not duals, and Exercise 4 makes you watch one fail. Second, the certificate is exhaustive: only checked relations travel, and Exhibit A shows what happens to the ones nobody checked. Third, the repaired point-line dictionary lives in the projective plane, a new geometry named here and not built here. Fourth, the twin is not always cheaper — Exhibit C — and the far doors (the Fourier pair at P24, Legendre's point-tangent dictionary in §7, the inclusion-reversing Galois correspondence) are signposts, not content: each is its own page, someday, and none of them is free by analogy.

6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P18: Desargues's theorem is self-dual, Exhibit C's mirror that gives nothing back, and its natural proof lifts the whole configuration out of the plane into three dimensions — the projective plane's own repair pointing at a dimension above, which is the next move of the spine. The second door is P24: a function and its Fourier transform are a dictionary pair, and every fact about time-limited signals carries to a fact about band-limited ones, backwards — the same machine, running on functions instead of points, with the same fine print and far bigger harvest. The farthest is P31: at the frontier, "product" and "coproduct" are defined as mirror-image universal properties, two paired kinds of object whose duality is an axiom of the subject rather than a theorem about it — the flip, promoted to a load-bearing wall.

The default habit

When a subject's objects come in two paired kinds — points and lines, vertices and faces, unions and intersections, "for all" and "there exists" — write the dictionary explicitly, one line, no hand-waving. Check the double flip comes home. Check each relation on the certificate list, and note which ones come out reversed. Then price the twin before paying for the problem: if the twin is cheaper, or already solved, or half of a census, collect the harvest. If the twin is the same problem wearing your own face, close the notebook — the flip told you something true anyway: that the problem stands alone.

7 · Where it came from

The flip is old, and its history is a sequence of mathematicians noticing that their subject's bookkeeping ran twice. Girard Desargues, an architect from Lyon, self-published a pamphlet in Paris in 1639 — the Brouillon projet — that introduced points at infinity and treated points and lines as trading partners; the profession was not ready, the pamphlet vanished, and the flip was rediscovered a century and a half later. Blaise Pascal printed a single page in 1639 or 1640 about a hexagon inscribed in a conic; its dual — six lines tangent to a conic meeting three at a time on one line — waited until 1806, when Charles Brianchon, a student at the École Polytechnique, published it, 167 years after the original. Giovanni Ceva's 1678 book De lineis rectis announced his concurrency theorem and, in the same covers, republished the collinearity theorem of Menelaus of Alexandria, second century, from the Sphaerica — the dual pair reunited in one binding without either author knowing they were twins. Euler's 1750 letter to Goldbach (printed in 1758) recorded \(V - E + F = 2\), a formula that under the vertex-face flip becomes \(F - E + V = 2\): the equation is its own dual, which is why it survives the flip untouched. Adrien-Marie Legendre, in a 1787 memoir on minimal surfaces, used the transform that trades points for tangent lines — the grown-up version of this page's dictionary, with \((f^*)^* = f\) as its Promise 1. The 19th-century logician Augustus De Morgan gave the complement laws their modern form. Joseph Gergonne stated the principle of duality as a general policy in 1825 (Jean-Victor Poncelet reached it independently), turning two centuries of paired theorems into a printing press. And in the fall of 1947, John von Neumann's typescript Discussion of a Maximization Problem — the linear-programming dual, where every optimization problem arrives with a mirror problem whose optimum must agree — put the flip to work in the age of machines; the first published version is due to Gale, Kuhn, and Tucker, 1951. Every one of these was the same discovery: the dictionary was checkable, the checks were cheap, and the theorems came in pairs.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 (●○○). (a) Compute the point determinant of \((1,4)\), \((2,6)\), \((3,11)\), write down their three dual lines, and compute the line determinant of those duals. What does the determinant theorem predict, and does the prediction hold? (b) Do the same for \((0,1)\), \((1,3)\), \((2,5)\) — and this time also find the exact point where the three dual lines meet.

Hint

The dual of \((p,q)\) is the line \(px - y = q\), and the theorem of §4 says the two determinants are negatives of each other. For part (b), remember what the dual of the line \(y = mx + c\) is.

Worked

(a) Point determinant: \(1(6-11) - 4(2-3) + 1(22-18) = -5 + 4 + 4 = 3\). Duals: \(x - y = 4\), \(2x - y = 6\), \(3x - y = 11\). Line determinant of rows \([p, -1, -q]\): predicted \(-3\), and direct expansion gives \(-3\). Not collinear, not concurrent, one sign apart. (b) Point determinant: \(0(3-5) - 1(1-2) + 1(5-6) = 0\): the three points lie on \(y = 2x+1\). Their duals are \(y = -1\), \(y = x - 3\), \(y = 2x - 5\), with line determinant \(0\). The dual of the line \(y = 2x + 1\) is the point \((m, -c) = (2,-1)\), and all three lines pass through it: \(-1 = 2 - 3 = 4 - 5 = -1\). Collinear points, concurrent duals, meeting exactly where the dictionary says.

Exercise 2 (●●○). The regular-solid census of §4 has a boundary case: \((3,6)\), three hexagons at a vertex. (a) Show \((3,6)\) fails the angle bound — and explain why the failure is a tiling rather than a solid. (b) List the five surviving pairs \((p,q)\). (c) Compute \(V\), \(E\), \(F\) for \((4,3)\) from the formulas alone, name the solid, and name its flip.

Hint

The angle bound is \(q(p-2)\cdot 180/p < 360\), equivalent to \(1/p + 1/q > 1/2\). The formulas: \(E = 2pq/(2p+2q-pq)\), \(V = 4p/(2p+2q-pq)\), \(F = 4q/(2p+2q-pq)\).

Worked

(a) At a vertex of \((3,6)\): \(q(p-2)\cdot 180/p = 6 \cdot 1 \cdot 60 = 360\), equality where the bound demands strict inequality — equivalently \(1/3 + 1/6 = 1/2\), not greater. The vertex lies flat, so the configuration tiles the plane instead of closing into a solid: the hexagonal tiling. (b) The inequality \(1/p + 1/q > 1/2\) with \(p, q \ge 3\) leaves exactly \((3,3)\), \((4,3)\), \((3,4)\), \((5,3)\), \((3,5)\). (c) For \((4,3)\): \(2p+2q-pq = 8+6-12 = 2\), so \(E = 24/2 = 12\), \(V = 16/2 = 8\), \(F = 12/2 = 6\) — the cube, \(8, 12, 6\). Its flip \((3,4)\) is the octahedron, \(6, 12, 8\): \(V\) and \(F\) traded, \(E\) untouched, Euler still \(8 - 12 + 6 = 6 - 12 + 8 = 2\).

Exercise 3 (●●●). In the triangle \(A(0,0)\), \(B(6,0)\), \(C(0,4)\), take the point \(P(3,1)\). (a) Find where each cevian \(AP\), \(BP\), \(CP\) meets its opposite side, compute the three directed side-ratios, and check Ceva's product. (b) Now take the transversal \(x + y = 2\): find its three meeting points with the sidelines, compute the signed ratios, and check Menelaus's product. (c) One paragraph: why is part (b) part (a), flipped?

Hint

Parametrize each side: \(AB\) is \(y = 0\), \(BC\) is \(2x + 3y = 12\), \(CA\) is \(x = 0\). On a side parametrized as "start plus \(t\) times (end minus start)", the directed ratio is \(t/(1-t)\); a meeting point beyond the end makes \(t > 1\) and the ratio negative.

Worked

(a) \(AP\) is \(y = x/3\); on \(BC\), \((6-6t, 4t)\) gives \(4t = (6-6t)/3\), so \(t = 1/3\), meeting at \((4, 4/3)\), ratio \(BD/DC = (1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2\). \(BP\) hits \(CA\) (\(x = 0\)) at \((0,2)\): \(CE/EA = 2/2 = 1\). \(CP\) hits \(AB\) (\(y = 0\)) at \((4,0)\): \(AF/FB = 4/2 = 2\). Product \(1/2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 1\): concurrent, at \(P\). (b) \(x+y=2\) meets \(AB\) at \((2,0)\): \(AF/FB = 2/4 = 1/2\); meets \(CA\) at \((0,2)\): \(CE/EA = 1\); meets \(BC\) where \(x = 2-y\) and \(2(2-y)+3y = 12\), so \(y = 8\), \(x = -6\), the point \((-6,8)\) — on the \(BC\) parametrization \(t = 2\), ratio \(BD/DC = 2/(1-2) = -2\), beyond \(C\) as the sign announces. Product \(1/2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-2) = -1\): collinear, on \(x+y=2\). (c) Part (a) asks about three lines through the vertices and answers with points on the sides; part (b) asks about points on the sidelines and answers with a line. Points on sides traded for lines through vertices, concurrency traded for collinearity: each is the other read through the dictionary of this page, which is why the two products are the two signs of one test.

Exercise 4 (◆). Goldbach's conjecture says every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes. A student proposes its "dual": every even number is the difference of two primes. Diagnose the proposal. (a) What dictionary, if any, was written? (b) Prove that no dictionary of the integers can carry "sum" to "difference" — that is, no bijection \(f\) of the integers can satisfy \(a + b = c \iff f(a) - f(b) = f(c)\) for all \(a, b, c\). (c) What, honestly, is the status of the student's statement?

Hint

For (b): addition is commutative and subtraction is not. Feed the same sum in both orders through the supposed dictionary and watch the two outputs collide. For (c): a statement with no dictionary earns nothing from a cousin it cannot reach.

Worked

(a) No dictionary was written: "sum" became "difference" by wordplay, §2's lie generator. (b) Suppose such a bijection \(f\) exists. Since \(2 + 3 = 3 + 2 = 5\), the carried equations give \(f(2) - f(3) = f(5)\) and \(f(3) - f(2) = f(5)\). Subtracting: \(2(f(2) - f(3)) = 0\), so \(f(2) = f(3)\), contradicting injectivity — \(f\) is a bijection, so it cannot merge two inputs. No such \(f\) exists; the flip is not merely unverified, it is impossible as a dictionary. (c) The student's statement is a different conjecture with its own evidence — it has been checked enormously far by machine, and no proof is known in general; it is a cousin of de Polignac's 1849 conjecture on prime differences, which itself stands unproven — but it is a rhyme, not a dual: Goldbach's (partial) results transfer to it by exactly nothing. The diagnosis is the move's negative space: when there is no dictionary, the flip licenses nothing, and the honest answer is "interesting question, wrong machine."

Exercise 5 (◐). Five statements: (S1) any two distinct points lie on exactly one common line; (S2) any three distinct points lie on at most one common line; (S3) a line is determined by any two of its points; (S4) there exist three points with no common line; (S5) any two distinct lines meet in at most one point. (a) Flip each statement through the §4 dictionary, writing the flip precisely enough to check. (b) Verify each flipped statement, and say for which of the five the equal-first-coordinate exception bites. (c) Flip the same five by noun-swapping and mark which survive. (d) State the pattern: which kind of statement flips for free, and which kind needs the projective repair?

Hint

Flip a statement by trading every object through \((p,q) \mapsto (px - y = q)\) and every relation through the certificate — "lies on" trades with "passes through" symmetrically. Then check each claim against small cases, the way §2 checked the two-question game.

Worked

(a) The flips: (S1) the duals of two distinct points — lines of distinct slopes, or parallel lines when the first coordinates agree — pass through exactly one common point, or meet only at the infinity-point of their direction once the §5 repair is paid. (S2) the duals of any three distinct points pass through at most one common point. (S3) a point is determined by any two of the lines that pass through it — dually: the point dual to a line is pinned by any two of the duals of that line's points. (S4) there exist three lines with no common point. (S5) any two distinct points lie on at most one common line — which is the mirror of S1 with "exactly" weakened, and indeed S5's own dictionary-flip is S1's at-most cousin. (b) All five flips are true: three concurrent lines share at most one point (two shared points would force the lines to coincide); two distinct lines through a point determine it; three duals of non-collinear points, say the duals \(y = 0\), \(y = x\), \(y = -1\) of \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\), have no common point. The exception bites only in (S1): points like \((1,2)\) and \((1,5)\) share a first coordinate and their duals \(y = x-2\), \(y = x-5\) are parallel — the repaired reading handles them at infinity. (c) Noun-swapped: S1 becomes "two distinct lines meet in exactly one point" — false, witness \(y = x\) and \(y = x+1\); S2, S3, S4, S5 survive as true statements. Four out of five, and the one casualty is exactly the one the dictionary had flagged. (d) The pattern: "at most one" statements flip for free, because zero common objects (parallel lines, or points off a line) is a coincidence the statement never forbids; "exactly one" statements promise existence as well as uniqueness, existence is where the affine plane has gaps, and those are the ones the projective repair must underwrite.
