5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

Every failure below is a ledger failure. The symmetry itself is usually real; what breaks is the accounting around it.

  • The uneven-orbit division (the divide-by-\(n\) trap). Thread six beads on a necklace, three red and three blue: \(6!/(3!\,3!) = 20\) linear arrangements. Divide by 6 rotations? \(20/6 \approx 3.33\) — not even an integer, and the move itself is confessing: the orbits are not all size 6. The arrangement \(RBRBRB\) returns to itself after just two rotations, so its family holds only 2 members, not 6. The honest census: one family of size 2, three families of size 6 — so \(2 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 3 = 20\), and the necklace count is \(4\), not \(3.33\) and not \(3\). Mitigation: census the orbit sizes on small cases before dividing (P02's habit — the \(n = 3\) table would have caught this instantly). Price: the census is a scan, and for a one-off small problem it can cost more than the division saves. Default: all objects distinct and the symmetry move has no fixed cases — divide freely; repeats or self-twins present — census first, divide never.
  • The phantom symmetry (the move is not a symmetry of the question). Solve \(x + y = 10\) in positive integers with \(x < y\), and the tempting half-division — "solutions come in swap pairs, so count half" — quietly destroys the problem: the constraint \(x < y\) changes under the swap, so \((3,7)\) is a solution and \((7,3)\) is not, and the twins you divided by do not exist. Half of nothing is still nothing; the halved count reports solutions the problem forbids. Mitigation: before trusting any symmetry, apply the move to every clause of the question and watch what breaks. Price: the clause-by-clause check is strict bookkeeping, and it occasionally kills a symmetry you were fond of. Default: the move must fix the full statement — data, constraints, and goal — not just the picture you drew of it.
  • The question breaks the symmetry (the marked chair). Same eight relatives, same round table — but now one chair is the head seat, nearest the door, and the question asks who sits where. The door is a mark on the frame: rotations move people relative to the door, so the rotation twinning is gone, and every seating is its own family. The count is the full \(8! = 40{,}320\) — the division you were owed a minute ago has evaporated. Marks like the door, an origin, a labelled axis, or the word "first" pin the frame and kill frame symmetries. Mitigation: find the largest symmetry the question actually keeps, and divide only by that. Price: sometimes the honest answer is that nothing survives — full price. Default: hunt the pinning mark first; the division you may keep is the one that moves the mark onto itself.
  • The hidden symmetry (waste, not wrongness). Missing a symmetry does not produce wrong answers — it produces honest ones at retail price: computing \(x^2 + y^2\) separately for \((3,4)\) and \((4,3)\), or scanning all \(36\) dice pairs by hand when \(3\) representatives and a census close the ledger. Mitigation: run the three-key diagnosis (block 3) before brute force, and let a small scan show the twins arriving in families. Price: none — this is the rare failure whose repair is a discount. Default: one small scan, every time, before committing to the long count.
  • Symmetry locates, verification proves. Example 3's square was located by symmetry but proved by the identity \((a-10)^2 \ge 0\). Stop at the location and you have a candidate, not a theorem: a problem can be symmetric and still have no winner at all, or several tied winners sitting off the mirror line. Mitigation: treat every symmetry conclusion as a fork that still needs its certificate — usually one line of algebra. Price: the certificate line. Default: symmetry prunes, the proof confirms; ship both.
Scope, honestly. This page runs the school cases: distinct-object rotations, swap twins, fixed points, self-twins. When orbits get truly uneven — many sizes at once, colors in complicated patterns — the honest census has a machinery (averaging over the symmetry moves themselves, the result often mis-attributed to Burnside), and that machinery is P36's, far up the ladder. The proof-side phrase for "one representative, argued honestly for its whole orbit" — without loss of generality — is important enough to be its own pattern, and it is P68's. The general language for all of it — groups, actions, orbits — exists, is beautiful, and is beyond this page; nothing here secretly used it.
