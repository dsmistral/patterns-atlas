2 · The idea, named

First the floor, restated from B07 in one breath: the hunt finds the stretch factors by solving \(\det(M - \lambda I) = 0\), then the rails by solving \((M - \lambda I)\vec v = \vec 0\); two distinct factors give non-parallel rails, hence a scheme (B07's Claim 3); and the eigen-scheme's dictionary \(P\) — rails as columns — was health-checked, reversed, and run through the round trip on this very machine, where \(P^{-1}MP\) came out the stretch table \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\). That one computed line is the new idea; what remains is to name it, and to reap what it buys.

Box B08 — diagonalization (decoupling by relabelling). A machine \(M\) is diagonalizable when some healthy dictionary \(P\) relabels it into a diagonal table:

\[P^{-1} M P \;=\; D \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix},\]

the stretch table: first reading stretched by \(\lambda_1\), second by \(\lambda_2\), no mixing. In words: to diagonalize a machine is to re-measure the plane in the machine's own directions, where the machine's coupled action reads as two independent one-number stories. The coupling never disappears — it lives in the dictionary and is paid once at each end of the trip; inside the scheme, the action itself is pure stretching.

Box B08.A — the harvest law. For a diagonalizable machine, for every whole number \(n\) of feeds:

\[M^n \;=\; P\,D^n\,P^{-1}, \qquad D^n \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^n & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^n \end{bmatrix}.\]

In words: \(n\) feeds cost one read, \(n\) one-number multiplications per reading, and one translate-home — the interior dictionaries annihilate each other pairwise (\(P^{-1}P = I\) at every seam), so a hundred coupled feeds collapse into two uncoupled stretch-counts. The law is also a closed form: the answer to "where after \(n\) feeds" written directly in \(n\), with no ladder of intermediate feeds to climb.

Four words get defined here, at first use:

  • New word: diagonal (table). A table whose off-corner entries are zero: it answers each unit-step question with a multiple of the same step, so it never mixes the two readings. \(\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}\) is the shape, and the name is plain description — the nonzero entries run down the corner-to-corner line, the diagonal. A diagonal table is two one-number stories filed side by side.
  • New word: diagonalizable. A machine is diagonalizable when some healthy relabelling makes it diagonal. For a real two-by-two machine that happens exactly when it owns two non-parallel rails — or already is a pure stretch \(\lambda I\) (section 4's Claim 3 pins the "exactly"). The adjective carries its own health warning: not every machine qualifies, and the failures are named below, not hidden.
  • New word: power of a machine. \(M^n\) is \(n\) feeds composed — the machine applied \(n\) times in a row. As a definition that is the end of the story; as a computation it is a tangle that grows with every year, until Box B08.A collapses it.
  • New word: closed form. A formula that answers a "what after \(n\) steps" question directly in \(n\). Lecture 0's year-by-year walks were the ladder; the harvest law is the closed form standing at the top. Closed forms are what the relabelling buys: not just cheaper arithmetic, but the whole future in one line.

Examples and non-examples, one per line, each checkable against the boxes:

  • The distortion machine \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\): diagonalizable, with \(D = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\); section 3 prints its tenth power both ways, and a general formula for every power at once.
  • Identity and triple, \(I\) and \(3I\): diagonal already, in every scheme at once — for any healthy \(P\), \(P^{-1}(\lambda I)P = \lambda P^{-1}P = \lambda I\). The machines that need no relabelling; the tool honestly declines work that is already done.
  • The shear \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\): not diagonalizable — one rail, so no two-column dictionary exists to file. Its year-after-year story is still honest, and section 3(c) tells it without any dictionary.
  • The quarter-turn \(\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\): no real rails at all — nothing to relabel into. The complex completion stays exactly where B07 left it: named, and deferred.
  • The Fibonacci machine \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\): diagonalizable with irrational stretch factors — the harvest travels past integer machines, and Exercise 3 reaps from it the most famous closed form in the book.

The names carry a history worth one paragraph, none of it load-bearing. The most famous reaping predates matrices by six centuries: Fibonacci — Leonardo of Pisa — posed the rabbit problem in the Liber Abaci of 1202, and its monthly counts \(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \ldots\) are Exercise 3's machine in disguise. The closed form for those counts is called Binet's formula, after Jacques Binet, who published a proof in 1843 — but the formula was old news even then: Daniel Bernoulli had proved essentially the same result in 1726, Abraham de Moivre had it by 1730 in the first systematic treatment of linear recurrences (Donald Knuth traces the trail), and Euler published his own account in 1765 — an attribution late enough to star in any telling of Stigler's law of eponymy. And Binet deserves better than a borrowed name: he was one of the quiet founders of the very matrix algebra this page runs on, before Cayley — B06's law-office lawyer — wrote its rules. None of this carries the page's weight; the harvest works whether or not its history is flattering.
