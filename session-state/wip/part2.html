Task two: the map must reach Ben. The harbourmaster also works in the other direction: a supply drop is planned at the map point \((4,3)\), and Ben — who navigates by road and fence — needs it in his numbers. From B05 you know the drill: solve. A reading \((a,b)\) reaches \((4,3)\) when \(a + 2b = 4\) and \(a + b = 3\); subtract to get \(b = 1\), then \(a = 2\). Two more map points and the pattern generalizes on its own:

\[(1,1) \to (1, 0): \;\; a + 2b = 1,\; a + b = 1; \qquad (9,5) \to (1, 4): \;\; b = x - y = 4,\; a = 2y - x = 1 \;\;\text{(the well, backwards)}.\]

Every reverse translation runs the same two equations, so the harbourmaster files this direction too — as a pair of formulas, one per unknown: \(b = x - y\) and \(a = 2y - x\), where \((x,y)\) is the map point. Filed as a table (watch how the formulas become the rows):

\[M_{\text{school} \to \text{Ben}} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 + 6 \\ 4 - 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.\]

So the wreck, remember, is \((2,1)\) in Ben's book and \((4,3)\) on the map — and now you can see the two events as one: the wreck never moved; the two reports are related by a fixed, reusable table in each direction.

Task three: honesty checks, and one broken dictionary. Two tables are now in play; hold both to B05's standard. First, the round trip: a Ben reading, translated to school and back, must come home —

\[(3,2) \;\xrightarrow{\;M_{\text{Ben} \to \text{school}}\;}\; (7,5) \;\xrightarrow{\;M_{\text{school} \to \text{Ben}}\;}\; (3,2). \qquad \text{Check the arithmetic: } (-1 \cdot 7 + 2 \cdot 5,\; 7 - 5) = (3, 2).\]

and the map point \((1,1)\) goes \((1,1) \to (1,0) \to (1,1)\). No drift, no loss: the two tables are mutual inverses, each undoing the other. Second — and this is the check that matters — Daisy. Her scheme was road \((1,1)\) and half-road \((2,2)\), and her would-be dictionary is

\[M_{\text{Daisy} \to \text{school}} \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.\]

Try the round trip through Daisy. Her reading \((3,0)\) translates to \((3,3)\)... but so does her reading \((1,1)\): check both, \(3 \times (1,1) + 0 \times (2,2) = (3,3)\) and \(1 \times (1,1) + 1 \times (2,2) = (3,3)\). Two different readings, one map point. So the reverse question "which of Daisy's readings produced \((3,3)\)?" has no honest answer — and no reverse table can exist, because a table must answer it. You have seen this failure before, in B05's pencil: it is her double-reading defect, the failure of read-once, wearing a table. The two columns of a healthy dictionary are a basis; a broken dictionary has parallel columns and \(D = 0\), and it collapses the whole plane onto a line as it translates.

What the pencil just showed. Translation between schemes is not a new act of measurement — it is a fixed table of four numbers, two per direction, built once from the scheme's own step-vectors and reused forever. The table's columns are the new scheme's units, spelled out in the old scheme's numbers. A table exists in both directions exactly when its columns pass B05's health test \(D \ne 0\); when \(D = 0\) the table collapses readings and no reverse is possible. And one question waits: the harbourmaster now owns a table per surveyor — what happens when a report must travel through two schemes at once? That is section 3's story, and it is where the tables start behaving like an algebra of their own.
