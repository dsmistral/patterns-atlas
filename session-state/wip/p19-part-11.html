7 · Where it came from

The counting engine is older than the coin. Pafnuty Chebyshev's inequality — the split that powers Example 3 — circulated from the 1860s, with Irénée-Jules Bienaymé holding an 1853 priority the name never recorded; either way the point is the same: a distribution's average and spread alone bound its tails, no shape assumptions wanted. Sergei Bernstein put that split to existential work in 1912, in a paper whose full title is its own abstract — Démonstration du théorème de Weierstrass fondée sur le calcul des probabilités, published in two pages by the Kharkov Mathematical Society — proving the approximation theorem by averaging over coin flips and handing over the polynomial as the average itself. Paul Erdős, in 1947, aimed the same machinery at Ramsey numbers in "Some remarks on the theory of graphs" (Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 53, 291–297) — and here is the historical nuance this page's \S2 dressed in coins: the 1947 paper is pure counting, written in the language of averages over color classes without a probability word anywhere; the probabilistic dialect the page uses was adopted later, in the collaboration with Alfréd Rényi that built random graph theory at the end of the 1950s. Claude Shannon, in 1948, proved coding capacity by averaging over random codebooks in A Mathematical Theory of Communication — two rooms, two subjects, one move, one year apart. The method's internal frontier has a fifty-year arc: the Lovász Local Lemma (Erdős and László Lovász, 1975) rescued witnesses from rooms where the bad events almost cover everything; Robin Moser and Gábor Tardos, in 2010, made its witnesses constructible — the derandomization walk of Example 2 grown to full power; and Adam Marcus, Daniel Spielman, and Nikhil Srivastava, in 2015, resolved the Kadison–Singer problem by running the whole method on a family of polynomials — the move's modern limit case. Noga Alon and Joel Spencer's The Probabilistic Method is the canon's codification, the book this page's §3 boxes are miniature of. And Erdős himself priced the whole subject in the anecdote this page will not need: if aliens land and demand \(R(5,5)\) or the planet burns, he said, compute the count and hand it over — but if they demand \(R(6,6)\), start negotiating. The anecdote is color, not load-bearing; the boundary it draws is real: counting the outcomes has limits it knows by name.
