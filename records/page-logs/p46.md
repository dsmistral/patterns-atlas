**Lineage sources (§7).** Jean Leray, captured June 1940 as a French officer and held at Oflag XVII-A in Austria for nearly five years; by his own account he rebuilt his mathematics in deliberate abstraction to avoid topics that might serve German military interests; the two 1946 Paris notes (CRAS) introduced the sheaf, sheaf cohomology, and the spectral sequence in one stroke. Jean-Louis Koszul (1947) gave the algebra its modern form within two years. Henri Cartan's seminar rebuilt the theory for its first great customer; **Cartan–Eilenberg**, *Homological Algebra* (1956) wrote it down as homological algebra's first systematic account. Jean-Pierre Serre's thesis (1951) — he was 24, and it made him the inaugural Fields medalist of 1954 — used spectral sequences to compute the homology of fibered spaces (the page's Encounter 3 sketch) and turned homotopy theory into a computational discipline. Alexander Grothendieck's Tôhoku paper (1957) re-founded the subject so the page-turning followed from axioms. J. Frank Adams (1958) built the sequence bearing his name for the stable homotopy groups of spheres. Pierre Deligne, *La conjecture de Weil II* (1974; Fields 1978) ran weighted pages deep enough to settle the Riemann-hypothesis-shaped questions of arithmetic geometry. The folklore quip attributed to Adams — spectral sequences are like exact sequences, only harder — half a joke and half Exhibit C.
