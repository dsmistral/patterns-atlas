The Patterns Atlas · P41 · the sixth move of tier 4

The mountain pass

Minima are found by descending and maxima by climbing, but the number most questions actually want is neither: it is the worst moment of the best route. Price every crossing by its highest point, take the cheapest, and the terrain itself must pay that budget — with a still point that is neither floor nor ceiling.

New word: saddle. A resting point of a landscape is a place where every direction is flat — the gradient vanishes. Floors and ceilings are the familiar resting points: minima, which every direction climbs away from, and maxima, which every direction falls away from. A saddle is the third kind: flat at rest, yet falling along some directions and rising along others — the seat of a riding saddle, rising toward pommel and cantle while it falls away to either side, or the lowest point of a ridge between two valleys. Walk the ridge and the pass is the ridge's own minimum; walk from valley to valley and it is the crossing's maximum. Neither descending nor ascending will ever find it, because half the directions betray each search — P29's one-way books point downhill, and a saddle is exactly the resting point they slide past. This page's whole task is to make the terrain hand one over anyway: not by searching, but by pricing.

1 · Before we begin

P21 taught the habit that a bounded monotone book must land: nested intervals closing on a limit point no one ever sees directly, and the extreme value theorem's promise that a continuous height on a closed, bounded terrain is actually attained. P29 taught the ledger: pair the field with a single number the dynamics only push one way, and read verdicts — settling, uniqueness, corridors, deadlines — out of the books. P26 taught the writing of equations of change, and P24 taught the splitting of a field into waves — whose first arch will carry this page's opening vehicle.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P29's ledgers answer questions about where things go: does it settle, how wrong can it be, when does it blow. But the oldest question an equation can be asked is more primitive — does it have a solution at all? — and the ledger cannot answer it, because the obvious tool for existence is descent ("seek the floor of the energy"), and for the equations most worth asking the energy has no floor: scale any candidate up and the books plunge without end. Descending finds nothing, climbing finds nothing, and no closed form is coming. What is needed is a resting point that is neither floor nor ceiling — invisible to both searches. The certificate that finds it is a new kind of question: stop asking which point is best and start asking what any complete route must pay. The worst moment of the best route is a number the terrain itself fixes; and when the terrain closes honestly — P21's limit point, spent one last time — that budget is paid at a still point exactly on the level: a saddle. The budget over routes, the cheapest route, and the still point are three views of one fact, and §2 watches all three agree.

2 · Meet the problem

A landscape small enough to hold in one hand: \(E(x,y) = (x^2-1)^2 + y^2\) — two valleys with floors at \((\pm 1, 0)\), a wall between them. A traveler at \((-1,0)\) wants to reach \((1,0)\), and asks the only question this page cares about: what is the least height that any route must touch? The straight line looks efficient; it crosses the wall at the origin and pays exactly \(1\) — its worst moment, at the midpoint. Detours look clever; but every route, straight or crooked, must cross the line \(x = 0\) at some latitude, and the wall's readings there are \(E(0,y) = 1 + y^2 \ge 1\). Any crossing pays at least the wall's cheapest reading — and the cheapest reading is \(1\), at \(y = 0\) alone. Two hundred random broken-line routes confirm the geometry: every one peaks at \(1.0002\) or above, and none beats the wall. A flood agrees: fill the landscape from below, and at water level \(0.99\) the two lakes are separate; at \(1.001\) they are one. The joining level is exactly \(1\). So the answer is forced (every route pays at least \(1\)), witnessed (the straight route pays exactly \(1\)) — and the census adds the surprise. At \((0,0)\) the landscape is still: the gradient vanishes. But the second derivatives read \(-4\) and \(+2\) — down toward the floors, up along the wall. The budget is paid at a resting point that is neither a floor nor a ceiling.

Now a landscape with more character: \(E(x,y) = (x^2-1)^2 + y^4 - y^2\) — two wells strung along the \(x\)-axis, each well split into two trenches by the \(y\)-term (four floors at \((\pm 1, \pm 1/\sqrt2)\), height \(-1/4\)), and a dome at the origin at height \(1\). Two hikers make the same trip, from the southwest trench \((-1, -1/\sqrt2)\) to the northeast trench \((1, +1/\sqrt2)\). Hiker A trusts the ruler and walks the straight line: it crosses the wall at the dome's shoulder, latitude \(y = 0\), and pays \(1.000000\). Hiker B reads the map — down to the southern trench, along its latitude, up the far side — and pays \(0.750000\). A twenty-five percent discount for reading the map.

Can anyone beat B? The question is about the wall again. Every route between the wells crosses \(x = 0\), and the wall's height along that line is \(g(y) = 1 + y^4 - y^2\). Search it: the gap sits at \(y = -1/\sqrt2 = -0.70711\), level \(0.750000\) — and by symmetry a second gap at \(+1/\sqrt2\), the same level. No route between the wells pays less than \(0.75\), and B's route pays exactly that. The flood agrees twice over: at level \(0.70\), each well's two trenches have already merged — their divider is a free crossing, a saddle at height \(0\) that costs nothing — but the two wells are still separate lakes; at \(0.76\) they are one lake, joined through the wall's gap. The grid map reads the joining level at \(0.750100\): its own cheapest wall cell, one ten-thousandth above the truth, an honest confession of resolution (the pass point falls between grid rows). And the census of resting points says exactly nine: four floors at \(-1/4\), two free floor saddles at \(0\), two wall saddles at \(3/4\), one dome at \(1\).

Look at what the answer turned out to be. Not a floor: nothing was minimized, and the two wall saddles sit far above the valley floors. Not a ceiling: nothing was maximized, and they sit far below the dome. The number \(0.75\) is a property of the terrain — every route pays it, one route attains it — and the terrain pays it back as a still point at exactly that level (Fig P41.1). The question was about routes; the answer is a place. That exchange — a budget over routes traded for a resting point on the level — is the move this page names. It works just as well when the landscape is an infinite-dimensional space of functions and the question is whether an equation has a solution (§4), or when the landscape is a graph of camps and ridges (§4 again).

3 · Name the move

§2 traded a budget over routes for a still point on the level. The move, named:

Pattern P41 — The mountain pass. When the object you need is a threshold between two basins — the least any crossing must pay, a critical point that is neither a floor nor a ceiling, a solution that no descent and no ascent will ever find — do not optimize a path's total, and do not search point by point. Pose the family of complete routes from one basin to the other, price each route by its worst moment, and take the best: \[c \;=\; \min_{\text{routes}}\;\; \max_{\text{along the route}} \; E.\] Then close the books: if the terrain is honest — if a sequence of routes forced toward the level \(c\) must actually land somewhere — the level is attained, and the terrain pays it with a still point at exactly height \(c\): a saddle. The budget, the cheapest route, and the still point are three views of one fact, and none of the three can be seen by minimizing or maximizing alone.

Or, in words: don't optimize the path — optimize the worst moment of the path. The best worst-moment belongs to the terrain, not to you; and if it sits strictly above both your endpoints, the terrain owes you a still point at exactly that level.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — two basins, genuinely separated. The two endpoints of the story sit below the wall that divides them — a rim of terrain strictly above both — so that every complete route must climb. Jam-mode: the basins never separated, and the "wall" is a bookkeeping fiction — §5's Exhibit C shows a landscape where the certificate computes a level and certifies nothing, because the two "valleys" were hilltops all along.

Key 2 — the question is about the worst moment, not the total. The objective that matters is the maximum along the route — the flood level a route admits, the highest ridge on the way, the peak cost of the plan — because the failure you are pricing happens all at once, at the worst moment. Jam-mode: what you actually need to minimize is a sum — total length, total cost, total time — and then this is the shortest-path problem, a different move with a different algorithm; §8's diagnosis exercise prices exactly this confusion on a real map.

Key 3 — the terrain must close. A sequence of routes squeezed toward the level \(c\) — each flatter, each closer — must have a limit point, and the limit point must sit at the level: closed, bounded landscapes do this by force, and infinite-dimensional ones only if an explicit compactness condition holds. Jam-mode: the rim runs out — §5's Exhibit A shows a level that is real, priced by every route, witnessed in the limit by no route at all: the water rises to it and never stops rising.

Box P41.A — the pass, in three obligations. Every mountain-pass argument is earned the same three-step way, and each step carries its own proof duty:

1 · Pose the endpoints and the routes. Name the two basins — two points of a landscape, two camps on a ridge map, two states of a physical system — and the family of complete routes between them: continuous paths, graph walks, deformations of shapes. Duty: verify both endpoints sit strictly below the level you are about to compute. A pass priced at (or below) your endpoints' own height is not a pass; it is the data you started with, wearing a certificate (§5's Exhibit C).

2 · Price a route by its worst moment; take the best over routes. The price of a route is its maximum height; the flood finds the best price without enumerating anything — raise the water, and the joining level is the minimax \(c\). Duty: witness the level with one explicit route that attains it. A level with no witness is a definition, not an answer — and Exhibit A's level is exactly that, an infimum no route pays.

3 · Cash the compactness. The level must stop somewhere: a still point at height exactly \(c\), which is a saddle — falling into the basins along some directions, rising along the wall in others. Duty: name what closes the terrain before promising the still point — a closed bounded window in the plane, a compactness condition in a function space. This is where P21's habit is spent: the limit point the books force but no one sees, promoted from sequences of numbers to sequences of routes.

Box P41.B — where the move appears. Existence for boundary-value problems — a solution nobody can write down, bought as a pass between the trivial solution and a deep valley (§4's equation: the founding costume, and the reason the move matters). Graph bottlenecks — the water level that joins two camps, the cheapest crossing of a ridge map, and its dual, the cut that certifies no route does better (§4's map: the same level read as a route, a flood, and a cut). Games — von Neumann's minimax: the value of a two-person zero-sum game is the best worst case, a saddle in strategy space (§7). Ground states — the cheapest corridor through an energy wall turns out to be the ground state itself, and the crest law that prices every corridor is a one-line calculus (§4). The failure signatures are §5's: the rim that runs out (Exhibit A), the shelf that lies flat (Exhibit B), the basins that never separated (Exhibit C).

4 · The move at work

Three workings, one per costume of Box P41.B: a landscape (the pass derived by calculus, by enumeration, and by flood), an equation (existence bought without a closed form), and a map (one level read three ways — route, flood, cut). Every number below is exact arithmetic — identities to machine precision, searches carried to convergence — and each is re-derivable by hand where a line of algebra suffices.

Example 1 (the landscape) — the pass, derived three ways

The census. §2's richer landscape has exactly nine resting points, and the catalog is worth writing out, because every one will be used: four valley floors \((\pm 1, \pm 1/\sqrt2)\) at \(E = -1/4\); two floor saddles \((\pm 1, 0)\) at \(E = 0\) — the free dividers inside each well; two wall saddles \((0, \pm 1/\sqrt2)\) at \(E = 3/4\); one dome \((0,0)\) at \(E = 1\). Newton's method, launched from a grid of forty-nine seeds, finds precisely these nine and nothing else — the landscape has no hidden still points.

By calculus, in one line. The wall's height profile is \(g(y) = 1 + y^4 - y^2\), and it factors: \[g(y) \;=\; \tfrac34 \;+\; \bigl(y^2 - \tfrac12\bigr)^2.\] That single line is the entire wall search. The gaps sit where the square vanishes, \(y = \pm 1/\sqrt2\); the level there is exactly \(3/4\); the shoulder at \(y = 0\) re-reads as \(g(0) = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1\), the dome's latitude. The second derivative \(g''(y) = 12y^2 - 2\) classifies all three latitudes in the same breath: \(g''(0) = -2\), a hump; \(g''(\pm 1/\sqrt2) = +4\), two dips. A ternary search on \(g\) confirms the calculus to eight decimals — the gap at \(-0.70710678\), level \(0.7500000000\) — but the factorization is the proof, and the search is only the compass.

By enumeration. Any route from one well to the other crosses \(x = 0\), so the crossing argument prices every route at the wall's cheapest reading: no route pays less than \(0.75\), and the valley-floor route of §2 pays exactly that. The diagonal pair — southwest trench to northeast trench — pays the same level as the straight-across pair, to the last digit of the search: same wall, same gap; the route is longer, the level is not. That indifference is the move's signature: the budget belongs to the terrain, not to the itinerary.

By flood. Raise the water. At level \(0.70\), each well is already one lake — its two trenches merged through the free floor saddle at height \(0\), a crossing that costs nothing because it dips below both endpoints — but the wells are still separate. At \(0.76\) they are one lake, joined through a wall gap at \(3/4\). The joining level, the cheapest crossing, and the wall's argmin are the same number three times over; and at that exact level, on the wall, the terrain stands still — the two saddles of the census. Within one well, the same story runs in miniature: the two trenches divide at a saddle of height \(0\), and the water joins them for free. Passes come in families, and the free ones are as instructive as the priced ones.

Moves used: P21 the joining level, caught as the flood closes · P15 the wall's gap, bracketed and squeezed by ternary search · P25 the nine-point census and the lake levels, read off by machine.

Example 2 (the equation) — existence, bought as a crossing

The question. Take the boundary-value problem \(-u'' = u^3\) on \([0,1]\), with \(u(0) = u(1) = 0\): P26's move gives the equation; the zero function solves it trivially. Does another solution exist? Multiply by \(u\) and integrate by parts — P29's pairing, pointed at a landscape instead of a flow — and solutions of the equation become resting points of the energy \[E(u) \;=\; \tfrac12\int_0^1 (u')^2\,dx \;-\; \tfrac14\int_0^1 u^4\,dx.\] The question is now geographic: does the landscape of \(E\), over the space of all functions with \(u(0)=u(1)=0\), have a resting point other than zero?

Why the obvious tools die. Minimization dies first: \(E\) has no floor. Scale any candidate \(u\) by \(s\) and the books read \(E(su) = \tfrac{s^2}{2}\!\int u'^2 - \tfrac{s^4}{4}\!\int u^4\), which plunges to \(-\infty\) as \(s\) grows — the quartic beats the square, and no bottom exists to seek. Maximization dies too: the terrain has no ceiling either, because the crest law's \(Q^2/(4R)\) grows without bound as profiles steepen (§8's exploration watches it grow like the fourth power of the wave count). And a closed form is not something an existence certificate should have to wait for. What survives is the geometry: two basins and a wall.

The two basins. One endpoint is the zero function, \(E(0) = 0\). The other can be any function deep in the valley below: take six copies of P24's first arch, \(e = 6\varphi_1\) with \(\varphi_1(x) = \sin \pi x\). Its energy is \(E(6\varphi_1) = 9\pi^2 - 121.5 = -32.6736\) — deep below the rim, below the start, verified by Simpson's rule to \(4 \times 10^{-15}\). Between the two basins stands the wall, and it is real for a clean reason: a route is a continuous deformation, so it must leave every small sphere of functions around its own start — and while profiles are small, the two-term book's quadratic wins (\(E\) stays positive on a small sphere around the origin; the quartic is too weak there to drag it below zero). The price of a route is the highest \(E\) it touches, and the wall is the cheapest price over all routes.

The obvious corridor. Walk straight out along \(\varphi_1\)'s ray: \(E(s\varphi_1) = \tfrac{\pi^2}{4}s^2 - \tfrac{3}{32}s^4\), which crests at \(\pi^4/6 = 16.234849\), at the scale \(s = 2\pi/\sqrt3 = 3.6276\). The crest is scale-free — six copies of \(\varphi_1\) pay the same crossing as one, because the crossing price is fixed by the ray's direction, not by how far down the far valley you are going. So the obvious corridor prices the wall at \(16.234849\). Can any route do better?

The gallery of corridors. Along any ray \(v\) from the origin, the same two-term book applies with \(Q = \int (v')^2\) and \(R = \int v^4\): \[E(sv) \;=\; \tfrac{s^2}{2}\,Q \;-\; \tfrac{s^4}{4}\,R, \qquad\text{crest} \;=\; \frac{Q^2}{4R} \;\;\text{at the scale}\;\; s = \sqrt{Q/R}.\] That is the crest law — two lines of calculus, and it prices every corridor in the gallery at once. The crest scale \(s = \sqrt{Q/R}\) is where the rising square term and the falling quartic term balance; a bisection finds it without calculus: on the ray toward \(e\), the balance \(t^2 Q - t^4 R\) — with \(Q = 18\pi^2\) and \(R = 486\) for the six-fold wave — reads \(+18\pi^2 t^2\) at the rim and \(-486\,t^4\) at \(e\), and one intermediate-value bisection lands at \(t^* = \pi/(3\sqrt3)\) — with \(6t^* = 2\pi/\sqrt3\), exactly the obvious corridor's crest point. The wall crossing and the corridor crest are the same event, one dimension up.

The discovered corridor. The gallery says: to cross cheaper, find a ray with smaller \(Q^2/(4R)\) — a profile that is economical in slope and rich in quartic at once. Shoot the equation instead: launch \(u(0) = 0\), \(u'(0) = a\), integrate \(-u'' = u^3\) by Runge–Kutta with step \(1/4000\), and bisect on \(a\) until \(u(1) = 0\). The shot lands at \(a^* = 9.72298103\), with the first integral \(u'^2 + u^4/2 = a^{*2}\) holding to \(2.6 \times 10^{-12}\) along the entire flight — and the profile it traces is a solution of the equation. Its peak height is \(M = 3.7081493546 = \Gamma(\tfrac14)\sqrt{\pi}/(\sqrt2\,\Gamma(\tfrac34))\), and \(a^* = M^2/\sqrt2\) to \(3.4 \times 10^{-14}\): amplitude and launch speed are one number. This is the ground state.

What the ground state's ray costs. Multiply the equation by \(u\) and integrate — the parts identity \(\int u'^2 = \int u^4 = 63.024240\) (to \(3.5 \times 10^{-14}\)) — so the ground state's own height is \(E(u^*) = \tfrac14\int u^{*4} = 15.756060\), and the closed form agrees: \(E(u^*) = \sqrt2\,M^3\,\mathrm{B}(\tfrac54, \tfrac12)/8 = 15.75606001\), to \(3.4 \times 10^{-14}\). The crest law prices the ground state's ray: \(E(s\,u^*)\) peaks at exactly \(s = 1.0000\) with value \(E(u^*)\) — its Nehari balance \(s = \sqrt{Q/R}\) sits at \(s = 1\) precisely because \(\int u'^2 = \int u^4\). And no tried corridor crosses cheaper: eight random four-wave profiles pay crests of \(183.7\) and up, and the obvious corridor pays \(0.4788\) above the pass (Fig P41.2).

The exhibited route, and what it bought. One route caps the level: out along the ground state's ray to \(u^*\) (the leg peaks at \(15.756060\)), on to \(2u^*\) (energy \(-126.0485\), deep below), across the valley floor to \(e\) (the leg never rises above \(-32.7\), below zero). Every leg is priced, the route's worst moment is exactly \(E(u^*)\), and the first two obligations of Box P41.A are visible: the endpoints sit at \(0\) and \(-32.6736\), both strictly below the level; the level is witnessed by the route itself. What the certificate bought, with no closed form asked and no minimum found: a nonzero solution of \(-u'' = u^3\) exists — priced at \(15.756060\), witnessed by a route, and paid at a still point, the ground state, which is a saddle of the energy: along its own ray the books fall away toward both basins (down to \(0\) on the near side, \(-126.0485\) on the far side), while every orthogonal direction climbs. The gamma functions came later, as a bonus; the existence came first, from the pass.

Moves used: P26 the equation, written and shot · P29 the energy, inherited and read as a landscape · P24 the first wave, the obvious corridor's vehicle.

Example 3 (the map) — one level, read three ways

The map. Ten camps on a ridge system, sixteen ridges with whole-number heights, one connected landmass: \(0\!-\!1\) at \(8\), \(0\!-\!2\) at \(5\), \(0\!-\!3\) at \(3\), \(1\!-\!2\) at \(9\), \(1\!-\!4\) at \(7\), \(2\!-\!4\) at \(6\), \(2\!-\!5\) at \(4\), \(3\!-\!5\) at \(9\), \(3\!-\!6\) at \(2\), \(4\!-\!7\) at \(1\), \(4\!-\!8\) at \(5\), \(5\!-\!6\) at \(8\), \(5\!-\!8\) at \(3\), \(6\!-\!9\) at \(7\), \(7\!-\!9\) at \(4\), \(8\!-\!9\) at \(5\). Camp \(0\) must reach camp \(9\) as the flood comes up, and the question is the page's question: what is the least ridge height that any route must touch?

Reading 1 — the route. Enumerate every simple route from \(0\) to \(9\): there are \(41\). Price each by its worst ridge. The cheapest peak is \(5\), attained by exactly two routes — \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!9\) and \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!4\!-\!7\!-\!9\) — which leave together through the level-\(5\) ridge \(0\!-\!2\), then split into the two level-\(5\) arms \(8\!-\!9\) and \(4\!-\!8\). The full multiset of route prices runs from the two \(5\)'s up through a wall of \(8\)'s to the \(9\)'s of the summit-crossers: the landscape's whole story in one sorted list.

Reading 2 — the flood. Sort the ridges by height — \(1, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 9, 9\) — and raise the water one ridge at a time, joining camps as dry land connects (Kruskal's flood tree). Camps \(0\) and \(9\) join when the water reaches \(5\): the \(8\)th ridge processed, the \(4\!-\!8\) arm, with all three level-\(5\) ridges arriving together at steps \(7\), \(8\), \(9\) — the pass is a flat shelf of three ridges, which is why the enumeration found two optimal routes instead of one. The flood tree carries its own route \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!4\!-\!7\!-\!9\) with worst ridge exactly \(5\): the staircase's joining level, the enumeration's cheapest peak, one number (Fig P41.3).

Reading 3 — the cut. Now run the move's third face: exhibit a cut — a set of camps \(S = \{0, 3, 6\}\) containing the start, excluding the destination. Every route from \(0\) to \(9\) must leave \(S\) at least once, and the ridges crossing out of \(S\) read \(8, 5, 9, 8, 7\): the cheapest is \(5\), so every route pays at least \(5\) — a one-line certificate that no enumeration could beat, dual to the exhibited route that pays exactly \(5\). Among all \(256\) cuts, the best holds its cheapest crossing at \(5\): the maximum over cuts of the cheapest crossing equals the minimum over routes of the highest ridge. This is the minimax equality — P17's flip, worn by a landscape — and the pair of exhibits (route at \(5\), cut at \(5\)) is its proof by squeeze.

Sensitivity — what the level answers to. Raise the never-used ridge \(1\!-\!2\) from \(9\) to \(12\): the level stays \(5\). Raise the low spur \(4\!-\!7\) from \(1\) to \(2\): still \(5\). Raise one arm, \(8\!-\!9\), from \(5\) to \(6\): still \(5\) — the other arm takes the traffic. Raise the other arm: still \(5\). Raise both arms, or the source crossing \(0\!-\!2\), and the level moves to \(6\). The budget is carried redundantly through the shelf of level-\(5\) ridges, and answers only to the crossing all routes share or to the whole shelf rising at once — the same structural honesty §2's two-gap wall showed, now on a map you can hold in one hand.

Moves used: P17 the dual — route and cut, flipped · P21 the joining level, caught as the flood closes · P25 the \(41\) routes and the \(256\) cuts, exhausted by machine.

5 · When the move fails

Every certificate fails somewhere; the honest page prices its failures. Three exhibits, one per jam-mode of Box P41.A, and each was run, not imagined — the numbers are exact arithmetic.

Exhibit A — the rimless wall

Take §2's two-well landscape and remove the rim: \(E(x,y) = (x^2-1)^2\,(0.25 + 0.75\,e^{-y^2})\). Two trenches still run at \(x = \pm 1\) at height \(0\); the wall between them still stands — but its height now fades with latitude, toward exactly \(0.25\) as \(y\) runs north. The level: at water \(0.25\) exactly, the lakes never join; at \(0.25 + \varepsilon\) they join — far north. The latitude law is the whole tragedy: crossings sit at \(y = 1.45,\; 2.10,\; 2.60\) for \(\varepsilon = 0.1,\; 0.01,\; 0.001\), against the law \(\sqrt{\ln(0.75/\varepsilon)} = 1.4195,\; 2.0779,\; 2.5730\) — each power of ten of slack buys a crossing latitude that grows ever more slowly. The route walks north forever, and never finishes. And nothing in the terrain pays the level: the only critical heights are \(0\) (the trench lines) and \(1\) (the dome at the origin); nothing rests at \(0.25\), and the wall's own points slide — \(|\partial E/\partial y| = 0.24\) at \((0, 1.5)\) — so no limit of improving routes stops there either. A windowed map reads the "pass" at \(0.2500000012\): its own edge reading, \(0.25 + 0.75\,e^{-4.5^2}\) — the confession of a window, not a resting point.

What broke: the rim — Key 3. The terrain never closes; \(c\) is an infimum no route attains, and the compactness clause is a promise the landscape cannot keep. Mitigation: two honest retreats. Window the terrain — then a pass exists, but it reads the window's edge: a boundary artifact, priced as such. Or keep only the infimum statement — "for every \(\varepsilon\) a route pays \(c + \varepsilon\)" — which is true, useful for pricing, and delivers no still point. Price: without the still point there is no existence claim; the level prices routes forever and pays for nothing. Default habit: name what closes the terrain before promising a pass; a minimax level quoted without its rim is a budget, not a place.

Exhibit B — the flat shelf

Now a landscape with too many answers: \(E(x,y) = (x^2-1)^2 + x^2\sin^2(\pi y)\). Two trenches again, and a wall whose height along \(x = 0\) is exactly \(1\) at every latitude — the entire \(y\)-axis is critical at height \(1\). The water level pins at \(1.0000000000\), and it is attained — by a whole line. Three hikers climb from the valley side, starting at latitudes \(0.4\), \(1.6\), \(2.8\); each follows the steepest way up and stops at rest, at latitudes \(0.481\), \(1.519\), \(2.638\): three different addresses, one height. The budget exists; the address does not.

What broke: isolation — the pass exists but is a continuum, because the terrain is periodic in \(y\) (\(E(x, y+1) = E(x,y)\)): a flat direction is a symmetry confessing itself, and the shelf is the symmetry's fixed set. Mitigation: quotient — treat latitude modulo the period, and on the quotient terrain the shelf is a single pass, honest again; or perturb the terrain to break the flatness, and accept the perturbation's price. Never report one latitude as the answer. Price: any numerical search stops somewhere arbitrary on the shelf — reproducibility dies, and two honest hikers return different addresses for one level. Default habit: before trusting a computed pass, check for flat directions; a pass that moves when the start moves is a shelf, and a shelf wants a quotient, not a report.

Exhibit C — the missing valley

The quietest failure: the bowl \(E(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\), with endpoints \((-1, 0)\) and \((1, 0)\). Run the machinery: the water level between them reads \(1.0000000000\). Attained? Yes — trivially: it is the endpoints' own height, and the level is witnessed by any route that never dips. Critical at the level? Nothing: the bowl's only resting point is its bottom, height \(0\), and the optimal route dips — through the bottom — rather than crest. The "pass" between the two "valleys" certifies nothing, because the valleys never were: both endpoints are hilltops on a bowl, and there is no wall between them at all.

What broke: Key 1 — the basins never separated, so obligation 1 of Box P41.A fails and the level, though computed, is the input data wearing a certificate. Mitigation: none inside the problem — the check \(E(\text{start}) < c\) and \(E(\text{end}) < c\), strictly, is part of the statement, not decorum. Price: vacuous certificates still close — the level is attained, the books balance — and only the verdict gives the flaw away: the answer you get back is the data you put in. Default habit: before cashing, verify the endpoints sit strictly below the level; a pass priced at the endpoints' own height is not a pass.

Three diseases, one machine-read symptom table. Exhibit A: level attained — no; resting point at the level — none. Exhibit B: attained — yes; resting set — a whole line. Exhibit C: attained — trivially, at the endpoints; resting point at the level — none. Three different failures, one lesson: the hypotheses — a rim that closes, a shelf that is not flat, valleys that actually dip — are load-bearing, and each one is exactly one key of §3.

Scope, honestly. What P41 gives: a budget that is a property of the terrain — the least any crossing must pay — witnessed by an explicit route, and, when the terrain closes, paid at a still point that is neither floor nor ceiling: an answer minimization and maximization cannot see, and for equations an existence proof that needs no closed form. What it never gives: the pass as a formula — the level is exact, but the still point is found by search, not solved for (§4's ground state was shot, then recognized); a unique pass — Exhibit B's shelf is a continuum of them, and §4's map carried three ridges at the level; a pass at all when the rim runs out — Exhibit A's infimum survives, the still point does not; or an interior answer when the basins never separated — Exhibit C returns your own data. The budget is the heart of the theorem, the still point is its promise, and the three obligations of Box P41.A are the terms, signed before the mathematics begins.

The default habit. When a question asks "what is the least that any attempt must pay" — the smallest budget that fails, the cheapest crossing, the tightest capacity, the value of a game — do not reach for a minimum of a sum. Pose the two basins, price routes by their worst moment, take the best over routes, and check what closes the terrain. The level you compute is a property of the terrain, not of your search; and if it sits strictly above both endpoints, the terrain owes you a still point at exactly that level. Like P29's one-way books, the whole method costs one question: what must every route pay?

7 · Lineage

The metaphor is old; the theorem is young. Courant, lecturing on Dirichlet's principle around 1950, described the heuristic — two valleys, a rising lake, and a saddle the water's level certifies — as a way to see critical points that no minimization would ever deliver. Von Neumann, in 1928, had already proved the finite, strategic version: the value of a two-person zero-sum game is each player's best worst case, \(\max\min = \min\max\), a saddle in strategy space — §4's route-cut equality is the same flip, worn by a landscape. Palais and Smale, in 1964, named the compactness condition that makes infinite-dimensional water stop rising — sequences whose height approaches the level and whose slope approaches zero must land — which is P21's limit point promoted to function spaces; without it, Courant's lake rises forever (§5's Exhibit A is the failure, run). Ambrosetti and Rabinowitz, in 1973, assembled the pieces into the mountain pass theorem (Journal of Functional Analysis 14, 349–381): two basins below a rim force a critical point at the minimax level, and nonlinear analysis has leaned on it since — §4's equation is its textbook case, dressed for a class-12 reader. And the discrete twin: Kruskal, 1956, the flood tree that sorts the ridges and joins the camps — §4's staircase is the same water, one graph — with Dijkstra, 1959, close behind for the other objective, the shortest path, priced by sums where the pass is priced by worst moments (§8's diagnosis exercise prices the confusion). The dates are anchors, not load-bearing: every claim above stands on this page's own mathematics.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — A landscape by hand. Take \(E(x,y) = (x^2-1)^2 + y^2\). (a) Find every resting point and classify it from the Hessian. (b) Prove that every route from \((-1,0)\) to \((1,0)\) touches height at least \(1\) — one line, no calculus of variations. (c) Exhibit a route that pays exactly \(1\), and say where the terrain pays the budget.

Hint

(b) Every continuous route from the left floor to the right floor crosses the line \(x = 0\) at least once. What does the wall charge there?

Worked answer

(a) \(\nabla E = (4x(x^2-1),\, 2y)\) vanishes at \(x \in \{-1, 0, 1\}\), \(y = 0\): three resting points. The Hessian is \(\operatorname{diag}(12x^2 - 4,\; 2)\): at \((\pm 1, 0)\) it reads \(\operatorname{diag}(8, 2)\) — both eigenvalues positive, two floors at \(E = 0\); at \((0,0)\) it reads \(\operatorname{diag}(-4, 2)\) — one negative, one positive: a saddle at \(E = 1\). (b) Any route crosses \(x = 0\) at some latitude \(y\), and \(E(0, y) = 1 + y^2 \ge 1\), with equality only at \(y = 0\): every route pays at least the wall's cheapest reading. (c) The straight route along \(y = 0\): \(E = (x^2-1)^2\) peaks at \(x = 0\) with exactly \(1\). The budget is paid at the saddle \((0,0)\) — the water joins there, at exactly level \(1\) (at \(0.99\) two lakes, at \(1.001\) one), and the census and the crossing argument agree to the digit.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The wall, in one line. Take the wall profile of §2's richer landscape, \(g(y) = 1 + y^4 - y^2\). (a) Show that \(g(y) = \tfrac34 + (y^2 - \tfrac12)^2\), and read off both gap latitudes and the pass level without any search. (b) Classify all three still latitudes of the wall — \(y = 0, \pm 1/\sqrt2\) — from the second derivative \(g''(y) = 12y^2 - 2\). (c) The diagonal hiker crosses the wall at the shoulder; the map reader pays \(\tfrac34\). Verify both prices, and explain why the factorization is the wall search.

Hint

(a) Expand the square. (c) The diagonal from \((-1, -1/\sqrt2)\) to \((1, +1/\sqrt2)\) crosses \(x = 0\) at \(y = 0\); the valley-floor route crosses at \(y = -1/\sqrt2\).

Worked answer

(a) \((y^2 - \tfrac12)^2 = y^4 - y^2 + \tfrac14\), so \(g = \tfrac34 + (y^2-\tfrac12)^2\) identically. The square vanishes exactly at \(y = \pm 1/\sqrt2 = \pm 0.70711\), where \(g = \tfrac34\): two gaps, one level. (b) \(g''(0) = -2 < 0\): the shoulder at \(y = 0\) is a hump of height \(g(0) = 1\). \(g''(\pm 1/\sqrt2) = 12 \cdot \tfrac12 - 2 = 4 > 0\): the two gaps are dips — minima of the wall profile — each at exactly \(0.75\). (c) The diagonal crosses the wall at the shoulder and pays \(1.000000\); the valley-floor route crosses at the southern dip and pays \(0.750000\) — a \(25\%\) discount. The factorization is the wall search because the crossing argument reduces the whole landscape question to the wall's minimum: price every route at its crossing latitude, then minimize \(g\) — and the identity gives the argmin and the level in one stroke, no ternary search required (the search confirms: gap at \(-0.70710678\), level \(0.7500000000\)).

Exercise 3 ●●● — Certificates, paired. Return to §4's map of ten camps and sixteen ridges. (a) Exhibit a route from camp \(0\) to camp \(9\) whose worst ridge is \(5\). (b) Exhibit a cut — a set \(S\) of camps containing \(0\), excluding \(9\) — every ridge crossing out of which reads at least \(5\), and list the crossing ridges. (c) Explain why the pair of exhibits proves the pass level is exactly \(5\), why the argument works on any map, and why it is the squeeze in miniature.

Hint

(c) One exhibit says some route pays \(\le 5\); the other says every route pays \(\ge 5\). For the general claim, compare any route with any cut: the route must cross the cut somewhere.

Worked answer

(a) \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!9\): ridges \(5, 4, 3, 5\) — worst \(5\). (Its sibling \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!4\!-\!7\!-\!9\) also pays \(5\).) (b) \(S = \{0, 3, 6\}\). The crossing ridges are \(0\!-\!1\) at \(8\), \(0\!-\!2\) at \(5\), \(3\!-\!5\) at \(9\), \(5\!-\!6\) at \(8\), \(6\!-\!9\) at \(7\): the cheapest is \(5\). (c) Any route from \(0\) to \(9\) must leave \(S\) — it starts inside and ends outside — so it uses a crossing ridge and pays at least the cheapest of them: every route pays \(\ge 5\) (the cut's certificate). Some route pays exactly \(5\) (the exhibit). So the level is exactly \(5\). On any map the same two exhibits squeeze every level: for every route \(R\) and every cut \(S\), the worst ridge of \(R\) is at least the cheapest crossing of \(S\) — the route must cross somewhere. The best route prices the level from below, the best cut from above, and when they meet, the meeting is the pass: \(\min_{\text{routes}} \max = \max_{\text{cuts}} \min\). On this map the squeeze is tight: all \(41\) routes against all \(256\) cuts satisfy the inequality, and the two optimal sides meet at \(5\).

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the claim (pattern-unknown). A route-planner writes: "Dijkstra's algorithm finds the optimal route, so I ran it on the ridge map from base camp to the village. My route's worst day of climbing is provably minimal — the algorithm is optimal." Diagnose. What is true in the claim? What is smuggled in it? What would you run instead — and what single question would you put to the planner?

Hint

Optimal for which objective? Dijkstra prices a route by the sum of its edges. The worst day is priced by the maximum. On §4's map, run both and compare the answers.

Worked answer

True: Dijkstra is optimal — for totals. On the ten-camp map, the shortest route from \(0\) to \(9\) is \(0\!-\!3\!-\!6\!-\!9\), total \(3 + 2 + 7 = 12\), and brute force over all \(41\) routes confirms no route is cheaper. Smuggled: that "optimal" for sums transfers to the worst day. It does not — that route's worst ridge is \(7\). The worst-day question is this page's: price routes by their worst moment. The pass level is \(5\), attained by \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!9\) (total \(17\)) and \(0\!-\!2\!-\!5\!-\!8\!-\!4\!-\!7\!-\!9\) (total \(22\)). The two objectives rank the routes differently, and neither is wrong: if the constraint is total effort, Dijkstra's route wins \(12\) to \(17\); if the constraint is the worst day, the pass routes win \(5\) to \(7\). Run the flood (or exhibit a route and a cut, per Exercise 3) instead of Dijkstra. The single question: "what are you minimizing — the sum of your days, or your worst day?" The claim is not an algorithm error but an objective error: the pass and the shortest path are different moves answering different questions on the same map.

Exercise 5 ◐ The crest law, explored (exploration). §4 priced every corridor out of the rim by the crest law: along a ray \(v\), \(E(sv) = \tfrac{s^2}{2}Q - \tfrac{s^4}{4}R\) with \(Q = \int (v')^2\), \(R = \int v^4\). (a) Derive the law by calculus: the crest sits at \(s = \sqrt{Q/R}\) and pays \(Q^2/(4R)\). (b) Verify it on the first wave \(v = \sin \pi x\): compute \(Q\) and \(R\), recover §4's corridor price \(\pi^4/6\). (c) Explore: price the corridors \(v = \sin(k\pi x)\) for \(k = 2, 3\), find the growth law, and explain — in one sentence each — why higher waves are hopeless corridors, and why the ground state's own ray crests at \(s = 1\).

Hint

(b) \(\int_0^1 \cos^2(\pi x)\,dx = \tfrac12\); \(\int_0^1 \sin^4(\pi x)\,dx = \tfrac38\). (c) For \(v = \sin(k\pi x)\), the derivative gains a factor \(k\pi\) and the fourth power does not budge: watch which of \(Q\), \(R\) moves.

Worked answer

(a) \(\tfrac{d}{ds}E(sv) = sQ - s^3R\), zero at \(s^* = \sqrt{Q/R}\) (and \(s=0\), the rim); \(E(s^* v) = \tfrac{Q}{2}\cdot\tfrac{Q}{R} - \tfrac{R}{4}\cdot\tfrac{Q^2}{R^2} = \tfrac{Q^2}{4R}\). (b) \(Q = \pi^2 \int \cos^2 = \pi^2/2 = 4.934802\), \(R = \int \sin^4 = 3/8\), so the crest is \(\tfrac{(\pi^2/2)^2}{4 \cdot 3/8} = \pi^4/6 = 16.234849\) — §4's corridor price, recovered from two integrals. (c) For \(v = \sin(k\pi x)\): \(Q_k = k^2\pi^2/2\) (Simpson agrees to machine precision: \(19.739209\) at \(k=2\), \(44.413220\) at \(k=3\)), \(R_k = 3/8\) always — the crest is \(k^4\pi^4/6\): \(16.234849\), \(259.757576\), \(1315.022729\), standing above the first wave by exactly \(16\), then \(81\) — the factor \(k^4\). Higher waves are hopeless corridors because they steepen without deepening: all the extra slope goes into \(Q\), none of the depth into \(R\), and the crest law charges \(Q^2\). And the ground state's ray crests at \(s = 1\) precisely because \(\int u'^2 = \int u^4\) along it — its Nehari balance \(s = \sqrt{Q/R}\) reads \(\sqrt1\) — which is the parts identity of §4, and the reason the ground state sits at its own crest while every other ray must be scaled to reach its.
