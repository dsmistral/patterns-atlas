Remainders and the division algorithm

Every division leaves exactly one honest leftover — and that leftover, not the quotient, is the number that keeps order in what follows.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern. The spine pages P05 (reduce to canonical form) and P09 (find the invariant) both lean on remainders far harder than the class-12 syllabus ever advertises — so the book teaches the concept slowly, here, first. Take a pencil. You will compute before you read.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Here is a box problem. You have 47 sweets and boxes that hold exactly 5 each. Fill boxes. How many boxes, and how many sweets are left over?

Do it with the pencil, not memory: 47, take away 5 — 42. Again — 37. Again — 32, 27, 22, 17, 12, 7, 2. Nine takes, 2 left, and 2 is too few to fill another box. So: 9 full boxes, 2 left over. Now the question that contains this whole page:

Why is 9 boxes and 2 over the right answer, and 8 boxes and 7 over wrong? Both describe the same 47 sweets — count them: 8 × 5 + 7 = 47 checks out. The difference is that 7 is not a leftover honestly earned: seven sweets would fill another box. A true leftover must be too small to matter — smaller than the box size. That single instinct, demanded of every division in mathematics, is this page.

2 · The idea, named

Box B01 — the division algorithm. For every whole number a and every positive whole number b, there is exactly one pair of whole numbers (q, r) with

a = b · q + r,    0 ≤ r < b.

The number q is the quotient (how many times b fits into a) and r is the remainder — the honest leftover, too small to fit b again. We write r = a mod b, read "a modulo b" or simply "the remainder of a on division by b".

Or, in words: dividing by b always leaves one leftover, and only one — and the leftover is always a number from the fixed shortlist 0, 1, …, b − 1. Not "some leftover": the leftover. Divisibility is the special case where the leftover is 0 — b divides a exactly when a mod b = 0.

Examples and non-examples, with the number 47 divided by 5:

  • Yes: 47 = 5 · 9 + 2. Here q = 9 and r = 2, and 0 ≤ 2 < 5. This is the one legal form.
  • No: 47 = 5 · 8 + 7. The sum is right, but r = 7 ≥ b = 5 — seven sweets fill another box. Illegal as a division-algorithm form, even though it is true arithmetic.
  • No: 47 = 5 · 10 − 3. Negative leftovers are banned: r = −3 < 0. True arithmetic, illegal form.

The point of the rules is uniqueness. Many decompositions a = bq + r exist as arithmetic; exactly one of them obeys 0 ≤ r < b. That is why "the remainder" can be spoken of with the definite article, and why a remainder is worth computing at all: it is a canonical answer — the same for everyone who computes honestly, however they got there. (Euclid's arithmetic books already compute this way; the idea is at least twenty-three centuries old and has not needed repair since.)

3 · Worked by hand

Three examples, simplest first, each carried to the end.

(a) 47 by 5 — the subtraction you already did

Lecture 0's pencil work was the algorithm: repeatedly subtract b, count the subtractions (q = 9), and what survives is r = 2. Your class-5 long division is this same procedure with the subtraction batched — you subtract 9 fifties at once instead of one at a time, but the landing spot is identical: 47 = 5 · 9 + 2.

(b) 2026 on division by 7

Seven times 280 is 1960; 66 remain. Seven times 9 more is 63; 3 remain. So 2026 = 7 · 289 + 3 — quotient 289, remainder 3. Two checks: the arithmetic reconstructs (7 · 289 + 3 = 2026 ✓) and the leftover is honest (0 ≤ 3 < 7 ✓). The lab runs this exact computation plus the failure next door: 7 · 288 + 3 = 2019, a different number — the quotient cannot be nudged without changing the sum.

(c) Weekdays — the remainder is the answer

Today is Wednesday. What weekday is it 47 days from now? Naively: list 47 days. With remainders: weeks are boxes of 7 days, so 47 = 7 · 6 + 5 — only the leftover 5 matters, because whole weeks land on the same weekday. Five days after Wednesday: Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday. The lab confirms it two ways: (3 + 47) mod 7 = 50 mod 7 = 1 = Monday, and a day-by-day walk of 47 single steps from Wednesday also lands on Monday. The quotient (6) told you nothing you needed; the remainder was the whole answer.

(d) Units digits — remainders in base 10

A number's units digit is nothing but its remainder on division by 10: 7⁴ = 2401, and 2401 = 10 · 240 + 1. The digit is the leftover. And leftovers of products can be computed digit-only: (a · b) mod 10 = ((a mod 10) · (b mod 10)) mod 10 — the lab tests this identity over all 10,000 pairs in [0, 99]² with zero mismatches, and it is the engine under Exercise 4.

4 · Why it is true

The claim has two parts — a leftover always exists, and it is unique — and both deserve real proofs. Both are short.

Existence (the well-ordering argument). Fix a and b, and consider the set S of all numbers you can reach by removing whole multiples of b from a without going negative: S = {a, a − b, a − 2b, a − 3b, …} ∩ {0, 1, 2, …}. S is not empty (a itself is in it, since a ≥ 0) and S is finite, so it has a least element — the smallest thing you can still reach. Call it r. Why is r the remainder? It is of the form a − bq for some count q, so a = bq + r. And r < b: if r were ≥ b, then r − b would still be a reachable non-negative member of S, and smaller than r — contradicting that r was the least. So 0 ≤ r < b, done. (For 47 and 5: S = {47, 42, 37, 32, 27, 22, 17, 12, 7, 2} and the least element is 2 — exactly Lecture 0's leftover. The lab enumerates S in full.) The "least element of a nonempty set of whole numbers exists" principle is doing the work; it will return as a full problem-solving pattern at P71.

Uniqueness (the size argument). Suppose two legal forms exist: a = bq₁ + r₁ = bq₂ + r₂ with 0 ≤ r₁, r₂ < b. Subtract: b(q₁ − q₂) = r₂ − r₁. The right side is strictly smaller than b in size — both remainders live in [0, b), so their difference is between −(b − 1) and b − 1. The left side is a multiple of b. The only multiple of b with size less than b is 0, so q₁ = q₂, and then r₁ = r₂. One form, always. (The lab tests uniqueness by brute force: over the full grid b ∈ [2, 13], a ∈ [0, 120], it counts how many q produce a legal remainder — exactly one, every time, 1,452 cases, zero violations.)

Scope, honestly. This page states and proves the division algorithm for whole numbers a ≥ 0 and b ≥ 1 — the range the consumer pages need. Dividing with a negative dividend also leaves a remainder (the lab probes −47 = 5 · (−10) + 3 and three friends), but the conventions there are a matter of taste that different books set differently; the book will pick its convention when it first needs one, not now.

5 · Practice

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◐ open exploration. Hints and worked answers are collapsible.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Divide 2026 by 7: write the full division-algorithm form a = bq + r, and verify both requirements of Box B01.

Hint

Batch the sevens: 7 · 280 = 1960, then handle the remaining 66.

Worked answer

2026 = 7 · 289 + 3. Requirement 1 — the arithmetic reconstructs: 7 · 289 + 3 = 2023 + 3 = 2026. Requirement 2 — the leftover is honest: 0 ≤ 3 < 7. (The lab records the neighbouring failure: 7 · 288 + 3 = 2019, so the quotient cannot be off by one and survive.)

Exercise 2 ●●○

Today is Wednesday. (a) What weekday is it 47 days from now? (b) 2026 days from now? Do not list days; extract what matters.

Hint

Weeks are boxes of 7. Compute each leftover, then walk the leftover.

Worked answer

(a) 47 = 7 · 6 + 5: five days after Wednesday is Monday. (b) 2026 = 7 · 289 + 3 — the very form of Exercise 1 — so three days after Wednesday: Saturday. The quotient disappears both times; the remainder is the answer. (Both routes — the leftover walk and the day-by-day walk — are machine-checked in the lab.)

Exercise 3 ●●●

A number n leaves remainder 3 on division by 7. Prove that n² leaves remainder 2 — two ways: by algebra, and by a small table. Then say which of the two is a proof, and what the other one is.

Hint

Write n = 7k + 3, square it, and redistribute into a multiple of 7 plus a leftover. For the table, take several numbers of the form 7k + 3.

Worked answer

Algebra: n = 7k + 3 gives n² = 49k² + 42k + 9 = 7(7k² + 6k + 1) + 2 — and 0 ≤ 2 < 7, so the remainder is 2, for every k at once. Table: 3 → 9 → 2; 10 → 100 → 2; 171 → 29241 → 2; the lab carries this exhaustively over all thirty remainder-3 numbers in [0, 209] (zero failures) and the algebra exactly over k ∈ [0, 30]. Verdict, in the book's honesty language: the table is a detector (P02), the algebra is the proof — the table can only ever witness finitely many cases, while the algebra covers every k in one stroke. One caution the table itself teaches: 172 also has a square ending in remainder 2 — but 172 = 7 · 24 + 4 is not a remainder-3 number; its square leaves 2 for a different reason (4² = 16 = 7 · 2 + 2). An observed leftover does not certify the hypothesis that produced it.

Exercise 4 ◐

Explore the units digits of the powers of 7: 7, 49, 343, 2401, 16807, … (a) Make the table of units digits for 7¹ through 7¹² and describe the pattern you see. (b) Predict the units digit of 7¹⁰⁰ and say how confident the table makes you. (c) Compute it honestly: tracking a single digit through 100 multiplications (the digit-only identity of section 3(d) makes this exact, not approximate — the lab runs the full 100 steps). (d) Say precisely what remains unproved: what would it take to know the pattern never breaks?

What you should find

(a) The units digits run 7, 9, 3, 1, 7, 9, 3, 1, … — a repeating cycle of length 4 (machine-verified for the first twelve powers). (b) 100 = 4 · 25 exactly, so the cycle should close on the last digit of the cycle-start, 1. (c) The digit-only track agrees: units digit 1. (d) Still unproved: that the cycle continues forever — a table is a detector, not a certificate, and 12 witnessed powers are not infinitely many. Proving it cleanly needs arithmetic that treats same-remainders alike — which is exactly the next bridge, B02.
