The Patterns Atlas · B02 · bridge page, group A · before P05 and P09

Same remainder, new arithmetic — congruence classes

Numbers that share a leftover on division by n are interchangeable for every question about leftovers — and the small stand-ins can do the arithmetic for the originals.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it directly extends B01, where every division acquired exactly one honest leftover. B01 ended with a promise to settle: a witnessed pattern (units digits of the powers of 7) that no table alone could certify. Settling it takes one new idea — that same-remainder numbers share arithmetic. Take a pencil; you will multiply before you read.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

B01's Exercise 4 ended on an honest debt. The units digits of the powers of 7 ran 7, 9, 3, 1, 7, 9, 3, 1 — witnessed twelve powers deep — but "the cycle never breaks" was left unproved: a table is a detector, not a certificate. Two pencil tasks, and the idea will be in your hand before it has a name.

Task one. \(7^5 = 16807\) ends in 7. B01's digit-only shortcut said: take the previous digit 1, multiply by 7, keep the digit: \(1 \times 7 = 7\). Why is throwing away the "680" legal? The 680 is 680 tens — and a stack of tens, times 7, can never disturb a units digit. Something about tens is quietly sharing the arithmetic.

Task two. What does \(47 \times 2026\) leave on division by 7 — without doing the big multiplication? B01 computed the leftovers for you: \(47 = 7 \cdot 6 + 5\) and \(2026 = 7 \cdot 289 + 3\). Try multiplying the leftovers: \(5 \cdot 3 = 15\), and \(15 = 7 \cdot 2 + 1\), so the guess is 1. Now the honest check, once, by real division: \(47 \times 2026 = 95{,}222 = 7 \cdot 13603 + 1\). It landed. The leftovers did the arithmetic for the originals — not by luck, as this page will prove.

Notice what the two tasks share: in both, a number was replaced by its leftover, the computation was done with small stand-ins, and the result matched the full computation's leftover exactly. That replacement is legal because of one fact, which we now name.

2 · The idea, named

Box B02 — congruence. For whole numbers a, b and a positive whole number n:

\(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\)  means  \(n\) divides \(a - b\).

Read "a is congruent to b modulo \(n\)". Equivalently: \(a\) and \(b\) leave the same remainder on division by \(n\) \((\operatorname{rem}(a, n) = \operatorname{rem}(b, n))\) — either test can serve as the definition, because each holds exactly when the other does. The shared-remainder numbers are collected into the residue class of \(a\), written \([a]\): all numbers \(a + nk\), for every whole number \(k\).

Or, in words: for division by \(n\), the whole numbers fall into \(n\) teams, and team membership is all that arithmetic of leftovers ever sees. \([2] \bmod 7\), inside 0–30, is 2, 9, 16, 23, 30 — step by 7 and you never leave the team. Two numbers from one team are interchangeable for every leftover question; two numbers from different teams never are.

Examples and non-examples, all division by 7 unless said otherwise:

  • Yes: \(47 \equiv 5\), since \(47 - 5 = 42 = 7 \cdot 6\). B01's leftover 5 now carries a team of infinitely many numbers with it.
  • Yes: \(2026 \equiv 3\), since \(2026 - 3 = 2023 = 7 \cdot 289\).
  • No: \(47 \equiv 4\) is false: \(47 - 4 = 43 = 7 \cdot 6 + 1\), and 43 is not a multiple of 7 (the leftovers are 5 and 4 — different teams).
  • Yes, and negative: \(-47 \equiv 3 \pmod{5}\), since \(-47 - 3 = -50 = 5 \cdot (-10)\). The definition asks only whether \(n\) divides the difference; negative numbers take care of themselves.

New word: equivalence relation. Congruence behaves like equality in three specific ways — reflexive (\(a \equiv a\) always: \(a - a = 0 = n \cdot 0\)), symmetric (if \(a \equiv b\) then \(b \equiv a\): negate the difference), transitive (if \(a \equiv b\) and \(b \equiv c\) then \(a \equiv c\): add the two differences, \((a - b) + (b - c) = a - c\)). These three properties are exactly what makes the classes behave as clean, non-overlapping teams.

The triple-bar notation ≡ is Gauss's, from his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) — the book that made arithmetic of leftovers a field of its own. The notation is deliberately equality-shaped: congruence is a version of equality, one wearing the fixed uniform "mod n".

3 · Worked by hand

Four examples, simplest first, each carried to the end. The engine under all of them is one rule, worth boxing before use.

Box B02.A — the working rule (reduce first). If \(a \equiv a'\) and \(c \equiv c'\) (mod \(n\)), then

\[a + c \equiv a' + c', \qquad a - c \equiv a' - c', \qquad a \cdot c \equiv a' \cdot c' \pmod{n}.\]

In words: team arithmetic is legal — replace each number by any teammate, or by its leftover (the smallest non-negative teammate), compute with the stand-ins, and the result lands in the correct team. Proof in section 4.

(a) The weekday walk, upgraded

B01 asked: 47 days from Wednesday? and answered by computing \(47 = 7 \cdot 6 + 5\), then walking 5. Congruence says the same thing with the mechanism showing: whole weeks are multiples of 7, and \(47 \equiv 5 \pmod{7}\), so adding 47 days lands on the same weekday as adding 5 days — Wednesday + 5 = Monday. The quotient (6 weeks) was discarded — not because it is irrelevant, but because for a division-by-7 question it contributes exactly 0 to the difference. That is the general shape: quotients are invisible mod \(n\); leftovers are everything.

(b) 47 × 2026, with the rule out loud

Reduce first: \(47 \equiv 5\) and \(2026 \equiv 3\) (mod \(7\)). By Box B02.A (product), \(47 \cdot 2026 \equiv 5 \cdot 3 = 15 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}\). The full product needs never be formed — but we did form it once, as the honesty check: \(95{,}222 = 7 \cdot 13603 + 1\). Leftover 1, both routes, agreement. What B01 did as a digit-only trick for units digits is now a licensed move for every modulus.

(c) 2¹⁰⁰ on division by 7 — cycles, found the honest way

What can the powers of 2 leave on division by 7? Compute small, before guessing anything (experimenting with small cases is a book-length move of its own, P02): \(2^1 \to 2\), \(2^2 \to 4\), \(2^3 \to 8 \equiv 1\), \(2^4 \equiv 2\), \(2^5 \equiv 4\), \(2^6 \equiv 1\). A cycle of length 3: 2, 4, 1 — and once a value repeats, the class arithmetic forces the whole future: from leftover 1, the next is \(1 \cdot 2 = 2\), then 4, then 1, forever. So \(2^{100}\), being \(100 = 3 \cdot 33 + 1\) steps in, leaves the cycle's first member: 2. Second route, as a check: reduce first at every one of 100 steps (\(r \to \operatorname{rem}(2r, 7)\)) — and both routes give 2.

(d) Which classes can squares leave? Division by 7, a complete census

Square each class member: \(0^2 \to 0\), \(1^2 \to 1\), \(2^2 \to 4\), \(3^2 \to 9 \equiv 2\), \(4^2 \equiv 2\), \(5^2 \equiv 4\), \(6^2 \equiv 1\). So \(x^2\) lands only in \(\{0, 1, 2, 4\}\) — no square leaves 3, 5, or 6 on division by 7. This is not a sample: by Box B02.A only the class of \(x\) matters, so the seven-row table is a complete census of infinitely many squares.

(e) B01's Exercise 4, finished

Now the debt. Claim: the units digits of the powers of 7 cycle 7, 9, 3, 1 forever. Units digit is leftover on division by 10, so work mod 10. Suppose \(7^k\) ends in digit \(d\) — that is, \(7^k \equiv d \pmod{10}\). Then \(7^{k+1} = 7^k \cdot 7 \equiv d \cdot 7 \pmod{10}\) by Box B02.A. Since the leftover of \(d \cdot 7\) on division by 10 is between 0 and 9, it is the next units digit: each next digit is forced by the previous digit alone. Starting from \(7^1 = 7\): \(7 \to 49 \to 3\) (from 63) \(\to 1\) (from 21) \(\to 7\) — the fourth step returns to the start, and since each step is a function of the one before, the loop can never break: 7, 9, 3, 1, forever. In particular \(7^{100} = 7^{4 \cdot 25}\) must end in the same digit as \(7^4\), namely 1. One formality is named and deferred, not hidden: writing "each step forced, starting from one start" as a proof for all \(k\) at once is the induction pattern, P66, which the book teaches on its own page. The engine of the proof — Box B02.A — is already proved below.

4 · Why it is true

Box B02.A is the load-bearing claim; it has two proofs, both complete and both short. Write the two given congruences as differences: \(a - a' = nk\) and \(c - c' = nm\).

Sum and difference. \((a + c) - (a' + c') = (a - a') + (c - c') = n(k + m)\) — a multiple of \(n\), so \(a + c \equiv a' + c'\). The difference version subtracts instead of adds: \(n(k - m)\). Done.

Product. This is the one that needs a trick — redistribute before concluding:

\[a \cdot c - a' \cdot c' = a(c - c') + (a - a') \cdot c' = a \cdot (nm) + (nk) \cdot c' = n(am + kc').\]

The middle step is just expanding and cancelling (expand \(a \cdot c - a \cdot c' + a \cdot c' - a' \cdot c'\) and watch \(a \cdot c'\) cancel); the last brackets out the shared factor \(n\). So the product difference is a multiple of \(n\), and \(a \cdot c \equiv a' \cdot c'\). Done. This "add and subtract the same thing" move is a cousin of B01's uniqueness proof — small redistributions, exactly accounted, are the coin of this arithmetic.

The reduce-first rule is a corollary: \(a \equiv \operatorname{rem}(a, n)\) and \(c \equiv \operatorname{rem}(c, n)\) by the division algorithm's own form, so Box B02.A gives \(\operatorname{rem}(a) + \operatorname{rem}(c) \equiv a + c\) and \(\operatorname{rem}(a) \cdot \operatorname{rem}(c) \equiv a \cdot c \pmod{n}\). B01's uniqueness (one honest leftover per number) then guarantees "the" leftover of the small computation is "the" leftover of the big one — that is precisely why Lecture 0's product guess landed.

Scope, honestly. Team arithmetic licenses addition, subtraction and multiplication — and nothing else. Division is not preserved: \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\) and \(2 \cdot 8 = 16\), and \(6 \equiv 16 \pmod{10}\), so \(2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 8\) — yet \(3 \not\equiv 8 \pmod{10}\). Cancelling the 2 is illegal; the equation survives it, the conclusion does not. When division is legal — the precise condition involves the greatest common divisor — is B04's question, and B03 builds the tool for it. Default until then: reduce, add, subtract, multiply freely; never divide a congruence.

5 · Practice

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◐ open exploration. Hints and worked answers are collapsible. No ◆ exercise here — there is no pattern to diagnose on a concept page yet.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Decide, with one subtraction each: (a) is \(100 \equiv 30 \pmod{7}\)? (b) is \(2026 \equiv 3 \pmod{7}\)? (c) is \(47 \equiv 4 \pmod{7}\)?

Hint

Box B02's test: subtract, then ask whether the difference is a multiple of the modulus.

Worked answer

(a) \(100 - 30 = 70 = 7 \cdot 10\) — yes. (b) \(2026 - 3 = 2023 = 7 \cdot 289\) — yes (B01's form of 2026 again). (c) \(47 - 4 = 43 = 7 \cdot 6 + 1\) — not a multiple, so no: the leftovers are 5 and 4, different teams.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Find every \(x\) in \(\{0, 1, \dots, 12\}\) with \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\), and prove your list is complete.

Hint

There are only thirteen classes, and by Box B02.A only the class of x matters — so trying each class once is not a shortcut, it is a complete case scan.

Worked answer

Work through the classes: \(5 \cdot 0 = 0\), \(5 \cdot 1 = 5\), \(5 \cdot 2 = 10\), \(5 \cdot 3 = 15 \equiv 2\), \(5 \cdot 4 = 20 \equiv 7\), \(5 \cdot 5 = 25 \equiv 12\), \(5 \cdot 6 = 30 \equiv 4\), \(5 \cdot 7 = 35 \equiv 9\), \(5 \cdot 8 = 40 \equiv 1\), \(5 \cdot 9 = 45 \equiv 6\), \(5 \cdot 10 = 50 \equiv 11\), \(5 \cdot 11 = 55 \equiv 3\) ✓, \(5 \cdot 12 = 60 \equiv 8\). Exactly one solution: \(x = 11\), since \(55 = 13 \cdot 4 + 3\). Completeness is by exhaustion — all thirteen classes were checked, none skipped. Why "exactly one" is no accident — why some equations like this have exactly one solution while \(2x \equiv 2 \pmod{4}\) has two — is B04 territory.

Exercise 3 ●●●

Find the last two digits of \(7^{10}\) without computing \(7^{10}\), then verify against the full number.

Hint

Last two digits are leftover on division by 100. Start like B01: what does 7⁴ = 2401 leave on division by 100?

Worked answer

Mod 100: \(7^1 \equiv 7\), \(7^2 \equiv 49\), \(7^3 \equiv 343 \equiv 43\), \(7^4 \equiv 43 \cdot 7 = 301 \equiv 1\) (indeed \(2401 = 100 \cdot 24 + 1\)). So \(7^{10} = (7^4)^2 \cdot 7^2 \equiv 1^2 \cdot 49 = 49 \pmod{100}\): the last two digits are 49. Verify: \(7^{10} = 282{,}475{,}249\) — ends in 49. Two routes: the ten-step reduce-first track [7, 49, 43, 1, 7, 49, 43, 1, 7, 49] and the shortcut; both land on 49. Notice the by-product: the mod-100 cycle of the powers of 7 has length 4 — the same shape as the units-digit cycle, one register up.

Exercise 4 ◐

Call a class \(a\) (mod \(n\)) invertible if some class \(y\) has \(a \cdot y \equiv 1 \pmod{n}\). Census the invertible classes for \(n = 7\), \(n = 10\), \(n = 12\). Then guess what invertibility has to do with \(n\), and say what remains unproved.

What you should find

Mod 7: every nonzero class is invertible — \(1 \cdot 1\), \(2 \cdot 4\), \(3 \cdot 5\), \(4 \cdot 2\), \(5 \cdot 3\), \(6 \cdot 6\) all \(\equiv 1\) — and the reason is visible in B01-style tables: each nonzero row of the mod-7 multiplication table is a permutation of 1…6. Mod 10: only \(\{1, 3, 7, 9\}\) — \(3 \cdot 7 \equiv 1\) and \(9 \cdot 9 \equiv 1\) — while 2, 4, 6, 8, 5 cannot reach 1 (an even class stays even). Mod 12: only \(\{1, 5, 7, 11\}\), each self-inverse (\(5 \cdot 5 = 25 \equiv 1\), \(7 \cdot 7 = 49 \equiv 1\), \(11 \cdot 11 = 121 \equiv 1\)); the witnesses against the rest are exact — \(2 \cdot y\) runs through 0,2,4,6,8,10 twice; \(3 \cdot y\) through 0,3,6,9; \(4 \cdot y\) through 0,4,8 — 1 never appears. The guess: a class is invertible exactly when \(a\) and \(n\) share no common factor beyond 1. That guess is correct, and its proof — with the algorithm that finds the inverse — is the whole business of B03 and B04; the conjecture itself is deferred, honestly.
