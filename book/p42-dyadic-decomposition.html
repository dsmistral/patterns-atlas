The Patterns Atlas · P42 · the fifth move of tier 4

Dyadic decomposition

When an object is complicated because it mixes sizes — terms across many magnitudes, detail at every zoom, mass in every decade — do not study the whole at once: cut it into doubling shelves, bound each shelf with one argument reused verbatim at every scale, and sum the per-shelf stories into a smaller series whose verdict is already known — then pay, honestly, for the three things the shelves never deliver: the exact value, the signed terms, and the final constant.

New word: shelf. In this page's world a shelf is a dyadic band — the stretch of an object between one power of two and the next: the terms from \(2^j\) to \(2^{j+1} - 1\), the frequencies from \(2^j\) to \(2^{j+1}\), the samples in one window. An octave is the ratio between a shelf's two ends, always exactly two; a shelf's story is what it carries — its sum, its bound, its dial; and the story-sum is the series you get by carrying one number per shelf, which is smaller than the object by a factor of two per shelf. The word matters because of what it forbids: a shelf gives a bound, not a reading. "This shelf carries between \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(1\)" is a shelf statement; "this shelf carries exactly \(1\)" is a fallacy wearing the shelf's clothes, and this page will catch it being wrong four times in the first two sections.

1 · Before we begin

P24 ended with a door and a promise: the wave rack stays, the room gets windows, and the local questions that page refused become answerable one scale at a time. This page walks through that door. The rack of global dials — Fourier's whole-room waves — was P24's instrument, and its Exhibit C recorded the instrument's blind spot honestly: a spike of height \(100\), invisible to every global dial, because its mass cancels against the rest of the room. The repair is not a better global dial. It is a different rack — windows that halve, halve, and halve again — and the mathematics that makes those windows a census rather than a mess is this page's move.

Two older habits come due at the same moment. P15 taught that an unknown is trapped between two computable walls, and that the walls' honesty is worth more than their precision: the verdict (diverges, converges) often needs only a bracket, and the bracket is exactly what a shelf provides. And P25 taught that a count worth trusting is a count audited by a second, independent engine: every number this page quotes — every shelf sum, every dial, every wall — was computed from scratch and recomputed by machines that share no code, and the two runs agree.

The tension ahead: the harmonic series \(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} + \cdots\) has been proved divergent since the fourteenth century, and the proof is five lines long; a modern machine can run it to a million terms in under a second and produce \(14.3927\) — a number, not a verdict, and no amount of further running will turn it into one. The cheapness of the shelf proof is the suspicious part. A method that settles a question in five lines that a week of computing cannot touch must be spending something, and it is: the shelves deliver the behavior (diverges, at what rate, against which walls) and permanently refuse the value. How that refusal is priced — where it bites, when it lies, and what residue it leaves on the table — is the difference between using the move and being used by it.

2 · Meet the problem

A friend bets you a month's coffee that the harmonic series \(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4} + \cdots\) never reaches \(20\). Three of your friends — call them the machine friend, the shelf friend, and the confident friend — each take the bet home, and each comes back with something different.

The machine friend runs it. A million terms, summed by the page's own machines: the partial sum at \(n = 10^6\) is \(14.3927\), and the millionth term itself contributed \(0.000001\) — a single step of one part in a million. At a billion terms the borrowed formula \(H_n \approx \ln n + \gamma\) puts the sum near \(21.3\) — the bet is probably lost — but the machine has produced no verdict and cannot: every run is a partial sum, and no partial sum, however long, distinguishes a series that creeps past \(20\) at \(n = 10^{17}\) from one that stalls at \(19\) forever. The machine's honest report is creeping; no verdict. It pinpoints values better than any other instrument in this book, and behavior not at all.

The shelf friend cuts the terms into doubling bands — shelves, the onramp's word: shelf \(j\) is the terms from \(2^j\) to \(2^{j+1} - 1\). Shelf \(0\) is \(\{1\}\); shelf \(1\) is \(\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}\}\); shelf \(2\) is \(\{\tfrac{1}{4}, \ldots, \tfrac{1}{7}\}\); shelf \(j\) carries \(2^j\) terms. Then the whole argument is five lines. Every term of shelf \(j\) is at least \(\tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}}\) and at most \(\tfrac{1}{2^j}\), so shelf \(j\) carries at least \(2^j \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\) and at most \(2^j \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^j} = 1\). Twenty shelves put the total past \(10\); forty shelves put it past \(20\) — the bet is lost — and the same five lines give walls around the whole sum: with \(k\) shelves plus the leftover term, \[ \tfrac{k}{2} + \tfrac{1}{2^k} \;\leq\; H(2^k) \;\leq\; k + \tfrac{1}{2^k}. \] At \(k = 20\) — a sum of over a million terms — the walls read \(10.0000 \leq H(2^{20}) \leq 20.0000\), and the machine confirms the truth between them: \(H(2^{20}) = 14.4402\). Verdict in five lines; walls into the machine's own territory; the bet settled by a method the machine could not have replaced with a year of running.

The confident friend also uses shelves — and poisons them. His version: "Each term of shelf \(j\) is at most \(\tfrac{1}{2^j}\), and there are \(2^j\) terms, so each shelf carries exactly \(1\); twenty shelves carry \(20\); the bet is lost by an exact count." Audit it the way this book audits everything — with one small case done in full. Shelf \(2\): \(\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{5} + \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{7}\). Common denominator \(420\): \(105 + 84 + 70 + 60 = 319\), so shelf \(2\) carries exactly \(\tfrac{319}{420} \approx 0.7595\) — not \(1\), not close to \(1\), and never \(1\) at any shelf past the first. The confident friend has committed the shelf's forbidden act: he turned a bound into a reading. The terms are at most \(\tfrac{1}{2^j}\), but only one of them touches the top; the rest are strictly smaller, and the deficit never goes away. His conclusion (the bet is lost) happens to be true, and his method still deserves to die, because the same fallacy, applied one line earlier, proves "each shelf carries exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\)" by the symmetric argument — every term at least \(\tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}}\), \(2^j\) terms — which would put the sum under \(10\) at a million terms and hand the bet to the wrong side. Bounds are walls; readings are poison; the audit table:

Every fraction in the table is exact — computed in integer arithmetic, then cross-checked against the machine's floating-point shelves to twelve places. And the table's last column is quietly announcing a discovery the walls never promised: the shelf stories are falling, monotonically, shelf after shelf for twenty shelves, toward a target the hand can already name. The one-octave growth \(H(2^{j+1}) - H(2^j)\) reads exactly \(0.5\) at \(j = 0\) and \(0.693147\) at \(j = 19\); the limit is \(\ln 2 = 0.6931\). The harmonic series does not just diverge — it diverges at a fixed rate per octave, the same rate as a logarithm, because the shelves have been quietly summing an integral all along.

Three cold questions, and the page is their answer. (1) How much does one shelf carry — can the walls be tightened into a reading? (2) Where does the divergence live — early, late, or everywhere at once? (3) What can the machine settle, and what can the shelves — and is there anything neither instrument reaches?

3 · Name the move

Pattern P42 — Dyadic decomposition (shelf, bound, sum the stories). When an object is complicated because it mixes sizes — terms spanning many magnitudes, detail at every zoom, mass in every decade — and the question you are asking is monotone in size (each term's behavior is controlled by which decade it lives in), then (1) cut by scale, not by position: partition the object into dyadic shelves — bands \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\), each a factor of two wide — so that every member lands on exactly one shelf, and members of one shelf differ by at most a factor of two; (2) run the one bound: prove a single argument, once, that bounds what any shelf carries — lower and upper — and note that the argument is verbatim the same at every scale, which is the entire point of choosing a doubling ruler; (3) sum the stories: one number per shelf — a sum, a bound, an energy — turns the object into a story-sum, a series over the shelves, typically geometric or harmonic, whose verdict is known or is one shelf-analysis away (P15's walls promoted from one unknown to a whole scale; P25's audit run shelf by shelf).

Or, in words: do not fight the whole object — file it by size, bound each file with the same one-line argument, and read the verdict off the filing system. The shelves are scaffolding: they are chosen so the argument can be reused verbatim, and they are discarded the moment the story-sum is on the table. What survives is a behavior — diverges, converges, grows by \(\ln\) — stated at the precision of a wall, which is often exactly the precision the question wanted.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the object mixes scales. The interesting mass lies at many different sizes at once: a series whose terms range over decades of magnitude, a signal with both trend and jitter, a function with rough patches of every size. The scale structure, not the position structure, is where the behavior lives. Jam-mode: the object lives at one scale — all terms comparable, all detail at one zoom. Then one bound answers directly, the shelves are ceremony, and the ceremony's costs (Exhibit C) are paid for nothing.

Key 2 — one bound covers a whole shelf. Within a shelf the members differ by at most a factor of two, so a single two-sided estimate holds for every member at once — the shelf-friend's five lines. The proof is done once, at an arbitrary shelf, and the scale-invariance of the dyadic ruler makes "once" mean "at every scale, forever". Jam-mode: the story changes inside a shelf — the object has structure at ratio finer than two (a spike at \(2^j\) and another at \(2^j + 1\), behaving differently), so the shelf bound is true but too coarse to answer. Then halve the ruler — the dyadic shelves nest, so the repair is one more level of the same filing system — or change ruler entirely (Exhibit A's \(q\)-ladder).

Key 3 — the story-sum is a series you can settle. One number per shelf, summed over shelves, must converge, diverge, or compare against a known series — geometric, harmonic, \(\sum 1/j^2\) — for the verdict to land. Jam-mode: the story-sum is itself boundary-case — as hard as the original. Then the shelves have not simplified the problem, they have restated it at one scale lower, and the honest options are recursion (shelf the story-sum: Example 1's log-ladder, where \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\) is settled by shelving its own shelves) or a different move entirely.

Box P42.A — the machine, in three obligations. (1) The ruler. A partition of the object: every member on exactly one shelf, no gaps, no double-counted endpoints. The dyadic choice \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\) is canonical but not compulsory — any fixed ratio \(q > 1\) runs the same machine (Exhibit A), and the dyadic case is preferred because the shelves nest, so a too-coarse shelf can be re-shelved without abandoning the filing system. (2) The shelf bound. Two inequalities, lower and upper, proved once, verbatim-identical at every scale — and stated as walls, never as readings. The confident friend's "exactly \(1\)" is the move's canonical poison, caught by the smallest full case: one shelf, computed exactly, checked against the claim. (3) The story-sum. The per-shelf numbers, summed: \(\sum_j (\text{story}_j)\), plus the honest statement of what the sum delivers — a wall-pair for the whole object, a rate, a verdict — and what it does not: the value. Box P15.A's walls, promoted: the story-sum is a squeeze whose jaws are two series, one per wall.

Box P42.B — where the move appears. Cauchy's condensation test (1821): a nonnegative decreasing series and its dyadic condensation \(\sum 2^j a_{2^j}\) live or die together — the shelf bound as a published theorem. Haar's wavelets (1910): the room cut into halving windows, one wave per window per scale, orthonormality checked by a census of dot products — the rack rebuilt on the dyadic tree (Example 2). Littlewood–Paley theory (1931): a function's frequencies shelved into dyadic bands \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\), the square function the story-sum in the currency of energy, the theorem \(\|S(f)\|_p \approx \|f\|_p\) the claim that the stories determine the object (Example 3). Calderón–Zygmund stopping (1952): halve a room until the average crosses a threshold, shelve the cubes that crossed — bad cubes whose averages sit in a factor-\(2^d\) wall, the shelf bound verbatim. Whitney's cubes (1934): an open set shelved by distance to its edge, side length pinned to a dyadic ladder. Doob's martingales: conditional averages on nested dyadic informations — shelves in time. Below mathematics: the organ builder's octaves (P24's room, registered by doubling), memory sizes (a kilobyte, then two, then four — hardware is a dyadic shelf with a price tag), and the amortized doubling of a dynamic array, where each regrowth costs its own size, sizes double, and the total cost per insert is a story-sum that comes out constant — the move, unnoticed, in every phone's every list.

4 · The move at work — three encounters

Three encounters, at three scales. First the shelf machine runs as a census — the boundary family of series around \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\), where convergence and divergence trade places one logarithm at a time, and the shelves read off each verdict with the machine cross-checking every block. Then the wave rack of P24 is rebuilt on halving windows — Haar 1910 — and the spike that defeated the global dials is not only seen but addressed. Finally the industrial scale: Littlewood–Paley bands, Calderón–Zygmund stopping, Whitney cubes, martingales — the shelf bound as a working theorem, in four of the hardest-working tools of twentieth-century analysis.

Example 1 · The condensation census: how slow is slow?

Moves used: P15 the walls, one honest pair per shelf · P25 the census, audited block by block.

The confident friend of §2 is gone; the question he fumbled survives him. The walls around a shelf were a factor of two apart — honest, but wide. What happens when the terms themselves carry structure at every scale, as \(\tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\) does? Run the move. Shelf \(j\) of \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\) carries terms each at most \(\tfrac{1}{2^j \ln 2^j} = \tfrac{1}{j \ln 2 \cdot 2^j}\) and at least \(\tfrac{1}{2^{j+1} \ln 2^{j+1}}\), so the shelf sits between \[ \frac{1}{2\,(j+1)\ln 2} \;\leq\; \text{story}_j \;\leq\; \frac{1}{j \ln 2}, \] and the story-sum is bounded below by a harmonic series in the shelf index — divergent. The verdict is one shelf-analysis long, and the story it tells is stranger than the harmonic's: the series diverges, but its shelves carry about \(\tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\) each, and a sum of \(\tfrac{1}{j}\)-sized stories grows like a logarithm of a logarithm. The machine confirms the walls first — shelf \(1\) carries exactly \(1.0248\), inside its walls \([0.3607, 1.4427]\) — and then delivers the speed receipt: summed to \(2^{20}\) — a million terms — the series stands at \(3.4239\). Under three and a half, after a million terms. The borrowed integral formula (\(\sum_{n \leq N} \tfrac{1}{n \ln n} \approx \ln \ln N + \text{constant}\), the constant about \(0.4\)) puts the sum under six even at \(N = 10^{100}\) — a googol terms, and it has not yet reached six. This is the slowest divergence you will ever hold: the shelves read the rate, not just the verdict, and the rate is one line of arithmetic.

One logarithm more and the verdict flips. Shelf \(j\) of \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln^2 n}\) is at most \(\left(\tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\right)^2\), so the story-sum is bounded above by \(\sum \tfrac{1}{j^2}\) — convergent. The machine: summed to \(2^{20}\), \(2.0376\), and the walls put the total near \(2.11\). The boundary between converging and diverging is not a line but a ladder — \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\), \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n \ln \ln n}\), each rung one logarithm slower — and the shelf machine walks the ladder one rung at a time, because each boundary series is settled by shelving its own shelves: Key 3's recursion, running quietly inside a two-line verdict.

The census's third family sits at the p-boundary, where the shelves read speed geometrically. The shelves of \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n^p}\) are geometric in \(j\) with ratio exactly \(2^{-(p-1)}\) — the machine confirms it to four places at \(p = 2\) (ratio \(0.5000\)), \(p = 1.5\) (\(0.7071\)), and \(p = 1.01\) (\(0.9931\)). Now watch the last one: a convergent series whose story-sum is a geometric with ratio \(0.9931\). The story-sum's total walls bracket the borrowed \(\zeta(1.01) \approx 100.58\) between \(71.6\) and \(144.3\) — wide walls, honest walls — while the machine's own partial sum at \(2^{20}\) reads \(13.52\), sitting inside its own octave walls \([9.27, 18.67]\). A million terms of a convergent series, and thirteen and a half collected of a hundred: convergence near the boundary is glacial, and the shelf machine has priced the glacier in one ratio.

Example 2 · Haar 1910: the rack rebuilt on halving windows

Moves used: P24 the wave rack, now one wave per window per scale · P16 the signal and its dials, one object in two coordinate systems.

P24's Exhibit C raised a spike of height \(100\) in an eight-sample room and recorded, honestly, that the global dials could not see it: every whole-room wave averaged the spike away against the quiet samples. The repair promised in that page's §6 is now due. Cut the room by scale instead of by frequency: eight windows of one sample, four of two, two of four, one of eight — the dyadic tree — and hang on each window the crudest wave that fits it: positive on the window's left half, negative on its right, zero elsewhere. Normalized to length one, these are Haar's functions (1910), and the rack built from the eight of them is the wave rack with windows installed.

First, the census P24's rack demanded and P25's discipline enforces: is the new rack orthonormal? Eight rows, sixty-four dot products, every pair checked — the machine counts \(8\) ones and \(56\) zeros, and the integer census underneath is even cleaner: before normalization the rows are pure \(\pm 1\) patterns, and the Gram matrix reads \(\mathrm{diag}(8, 8, 4, 4, 2, 2, 2, 2)\) — the window sizes, on the diagonal, and nothing anywhere else. Then the dials. Take the ramp \((1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8)\): its eight dials are \[ \left(12.7279,\; -5.6569,\; -2,\; -2,\; -0.7071, -0.7071, -0.7071, -0.7071\right), \] and before normalization, the integer census \((36, -16, -4, -4, -1, -1, -1, -1)\): pure counting, no square roots in sight. Parseval holds exactly — \(204 = 162 + 32 + 8 + 2\) — and the four summands are the ladder this page has been building all along: the ramp's energy per shelf is \(162\) at the whole-room scale, \(32\) at halves, \(8\) at quarters, \(2\) at the finest — each shelf carries exactly a quarter of the one above it. That is the smooth story: for a gentle object, the shelf energies die geometrically, by four per octave — the dyadic twin of P24's decay diagnostic, and the fact from which every real wavelet codec descends.

Now the spike — P24's blind spot, filed by size. The signal \((0, 0, 0, 0, 100, 0, 0, 0)\) against the same rack reads \[ \left(35.3553,\; -35.3553,\; 0,\; 50,\; 0,\; 0,\; 70.7107,\; 0\right), \] and everything P24's global dials refused to show is on the table at once. The spike is seen — its loudest dial is \(70.7107\), and it reads exactly twice as loud in its own two-sample window (\(100/\sqrt{2}\)) as in the whole room (\(100/\sqrt{8}\)): the loudness ratio \(2\), computed exactly. It is located — the dial signs walk the tree and read the spike's binary address: the halves dial is negative (right half), within it the quarters dial \(50\) is the second quarter's (right half again), within that the finest nonzero dial is the third pair, and its sign says the left member: position \(4\), which is binary \(100\), spelled out scale by scale by the dials themselves. And it is priced: the energy ladder runs \(1250, 1250, 2500, 5000\) — doubling per octave, the exact opposite of the ramp's quartering. That is the rough story, and it is why this census is a diagnostic and not just a change of coordinates: read the shelf energies of any eight samples and you have said, in four numbers, whether the object is smooth (energy dying by quarters), rough (energy doubling), or mixed (Example 3's exercise signal, below, is: \(120.1, 21.1, 9.25, 22.5\) — trend at the top, jitter at the bottom, and the digits of \(\pi\) in between).

Example 3 · The industrial scale: bands, stopping, cubes, martingales

Moves used: P15 the walls, now with constants named after people · P16 a function and its scale stories, formally the same object.

The miniature of Examples 1 and 2 is honest mathematics, but the move's real career has been at industrial scale, in four of the load-bearing tools of twentieth-century analysis. What follows names them, states each one's shelf structure exactly, and flags — as this book always must — which claims are borrowed on trust rather than proved here.

Littlewood–Paley bands (1931 on). Take P24's frequency room and shelve it: \(\Delta_j f\) is the piece of a function \(f\) whose frequencies live in the band \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\) — one shelf per octave, exactly Example 1's ruler applied to a Fourier transform. The square function \(S(f) = \left(\sum_j |\Delta_j f|^2\right)^{1/2}\) is the story-sum in the currency of energy — Example 2's per-shelf energy ladder, summed as the squares demand. At \(p = 2\) the shelves are exact bookkeeping: Parseval, which Example 2's census checked in miniature, says the stories carry the whole object, no more and no less. The deep borrowed fact is the wall-pair: for every \(1 < p < \infty\), two constants exist with \[ A_p \, \|f\|_p \;\leq\; \|S(f)\|_p \;\leq\; B_p \, \|f\|_p, \] and the wall's width — the constants, not the verdict — is the whole subject. P15's walls at research scale: the stories determine the object up to a factor that depends on \(p\) and not on \(f\), which is P16's isomorphism wearing its most useful clothes.

Calderón–Zygmund stopping (1952). Here the shelving runs top-down instead of bottom-up. To control an integral against a threshold \(\alpha\): average a room; if the average is at most \(\alpha\), halve the room and repeat; the moment a piece's average first exceeds \(\alpha\), stop and shelve that piece as bad. The shelf bound is verbatim Example 1's: a bad cube's average is at least \(\alpha\) (that is why it stopped) and at most \(2^d \alpha\) (its parent's average was still under \(\alpha\), and one child can carry at most all of the parent's mass over \(2^d\) children). The bad cubes' averages live in a factor-\(2^d\) wall — the confident friend's fallacy, answered in advance: between \(\alpha\) and \(2^d\alpha\), never "exactly" anything. From this wall, the entire Calderón–Zygmund theory of singular integrals descends — borrowed here as a named, standard, unproved-here structure.

Whitney's cubes (1934). An open set — a region with holes, folds, and fjords — is shelved by distance: cover it by dyadic cubes whose side length is comparable to the distance from the cube to the region's edge. Near a ragged boundary, small shelves; out in the open, large ones; the ruler chosen by the geometry itself, rather than fixed in advance. This is the decomposition behind extension theorems and, in modern dress, behind the modern proofs of the Littlewood–Paley-style estimates — the dyadic model of the twenty-first century, where difficult operators are replaced by model operators built on cube trees, and the model's walls are proven to control the original's. Borrowed, named, and by now the standard working method.

Doob's martingales (the 1940s, codified 1953). Run the halving tree of Example 2 in time instead of space: a sequence of coarser-and-coarser observations of a random quantity, each dyadic level an average of the level below, the finest level the truth. The conditional averages are the tree's box values; the increments are the split dials; and the maximal inequality — Doob's, borrowed here — is the shelf audit at probability's scale: a bound on how often the running average can climb far above its final destination, proven by shelving the climb's excursions by powers of two. Martingale theory is this page's move with the arrows reversed: information flowing up a dyadic tree rather than mass flowing down one.

Four tools, one filing system. The lesson of the industrial scale is not that the shelves become fancier — it is that they stay the same. Bands of two, stopped cubes averaging two-to-the-\(d\), distance-scaled cubes, dyadic time: the shelf bound is proved once, at a scale chosen by the problem, and reused verbatim everywhere, which is what "industrial" means for a mathematical idea.

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits

Shelf, bound, sum the stories — the pattern is only as strong as its weakest obligation, and each obligation has a famous failure. Exhibit A: the walls are walls, and no refinement of the ruler closes them all the way. Exhibit B: the shelf bound is a statement about size, and signs kill it. Exhibit C: the story-sum can be as hard as the object it was summoned to settle.

Exhibit A · The wall that never closes

The factor-two gap in the harmonic walls \([10, 20]\) at a million terms looks like an artifact of choosing ratio two, and in part it is: sharpen the ruler — shelves of ratio \(q\), the same machine with \(q\) in place of \(2\) — and the walls tighten. The machine has run the ladder at \(2^{20}\): ratio \(2\) gives \([10.00, 20.00]\); ratio \(\tfrac{3}{2}\) gives \([11.29, 17.30]\) across \(33\) shelves; ratio \(\tfrac{9}{8}\) gives \([12.81, 15.13]\) across \(104\) shelves; ratio \(\tfrac{17}{16}\) gives \([13.13, 14.77]\) across \(192\) shelves. The widths fall — \(10.00, 6.01, 2.32, 1.64\) — and the two walls close toward each other like a squeeze. But close onto what? Not onto the truth. The true value is \(H(2^{20}) = 14.4402\), and every lower wall in the ladder — even at ratio \(\tfrac{17}{16}\) — sits below \(\ln(2^{20}) = 13.8629\), while the upper walls close toward \(1 + \ln(2^{20})\). The ladder's two walls converge on the integral test's bracket \([\ln N, 1 + \ln N]\), and the gap between that bracket and the truth is exactly \[ H(2^{20}) - \ln(2^{20}) \;=\; 0.577216 \;=\; \gamma, \] the Euler–Mascheroni constant — matching \(\gamma = 0.577216\) to six places, and the match is no coincidence of this \(N\): \(H(N) - \ln N \to \gamma\) for every route to infinity. This is the shelf method's residue: a wall of width one-half — no, of width \(\gamma \approx 0.5772\) — that no \(q\), no refinement, no amount of shelving ever closes, because the shelves measure the object's scale structure and \(\gamma\) lives in the fine arithmetic of the integers themselves, one shelf per unit of \(\ln\), leaking \(\gamma\) across all of them. The mitigation is honest borrowing: when the value matters, call for the machine (Example 1's \(14.4402\)) or for the borrowed formula \(H_n \approx \ln n + \gamma\). The shelves give behavior and rate; the constant is someone else's department, and this page says so out loud rather than pretending the wall is a reading.

Exhibit B · The monotonicity hole: signs

A second friend of the confident species shelves the alternating harmonic series \(\sum \tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} - \cdots\) — "same shelves, same machine, and the shelves say each shelf of absolute values carries between \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(1\), so the series behaves like the harmonic." Audit with the smallest full case: the signed shelf \([4, 8)\) is \(\tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{5} + \tfrac{1}{6} - \tfrac{1}{7} \approx 0.0738\) — a shelf carrying about seven cents where the shelf obligation promised at least fifty. The shelf bound is a statement about magnitude, and inside a shelf the signs cancel: Key 2's "one bound covers a shelf" is false the moment terms carry signs, because the bound was proved for things that pile up, not things that cancel. The machine confirms both halves of the wreckage: the signed series, summed to \(2^{20}\), reads \(0.693147\) — it is converging, to \(\ln 2\), bracketed exactly as Leibniz brackets it (odd partials above, even below, steps shrinking as \(\tfrac{1}{n}\)) — while its shelf shadow \(\sum_j 2^j \left|a_{2^j}\right|\) reads \(20\) at the twentieth shelf and climbs by exactly \(1\) per octave: the same machine, fed the absolute values, truthfully reports divergence. Both reports are true; the poison was expecting a size instrument to speak about signs. The diagnosis names the repair: cancellation is handled by a different move — the bracket, monotone pairing, this book's P24 and the classical Leibniz discipline — and the shelf machine is only pointed at \(\sum |a_n|\) when a size verdict is genuinely what is being asked.

Exhibit C · The price: recursion at the boundary

The third failure is quiet and lives at Key 3. The shelves turn an object into a story-sum, and the move's economy depends on the story-sum being easier than the object. For \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\), the story-sum was a harmonic in the shelf index — easy. For the boundary family beyond it, the story-sum is another series of the same species: shelving \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n \ln \ln n}\) produces a story-sum that is essentially \(\sum \tfrac{1}{j \ln j}\) — the previous rung's problem, one level down. The move survives by recursion — shelf the story-sum too, and the ladder of logarithms descends one rung at a time (Example 1's ladder, walked rung by rung) — but the honest price stands: at the boundary, the five-line proof becomes an \(r\)-line proof, one line per logarithm, and a series with infinitely many nested logarithms in its term can stall the machine outright. And the walls, even when they land, can be coarse where precision matters: the \(p = 1.01\) census's total walls \([71.6, 144.3]\) around a borrowed \(\zeta(1.01) \approx 100.58\) are a factor of two wide — the same factor two that opened this page, still there at the close. The default habit this leaves behind: shelf it, bound each shelf, sum the stories — then ask, before celebrating, whether the story-sum is actually simpler, and say which constant the walls cannot reach.

Scope, honestly. What this page's own machines established, from scratch, in two independent implementations sharing no code: the harmonic shelf census — twenty shelves each in \((\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]\), strictly decreasing, the exact fractions \(1\), \(\tfrac{5}{6}\), \(\tfrac{319}{420}\), \(\tfrac{52279}{72072}\) and their decimal agreement to twelve places, the walls \(k/2 + 1/2^k \leq H(2^k) \leq k + 1/2^k\) for every \(k \leq 20\), the per-octave growth falling from exactly \(0.5\) to \(0.693147\); the values \(H(10^6) = 14.3927\) and \(H(2^{20}) = 14.4402\); the log-series verdicts — every block inside its walls, \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\) at \(3.4239\) and \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n \ln^2 n}\) at \(2.0376\) to \(2^{20}\), the geometric-block ratios \(0.5000, 0.7071, 0.9931\), the \(p = 1.01\) partial \(13.52\) inside \([9.27, 18.67]\); the alternating-harmonic bracket to \(0.693147\) and its divergent shadow at \(20\); the Haar census — all sixty-four dot products, the integer Gram \(\mathrm{diag}(8,8,4,4,2,2,2,2)\), both dial lists, Parseval at \(204\) and \(10000\), the energy ladders quartering and doubling, the binary address \(100\), the loudness ratio exactly \(2\); and the \(q\)-ladder's four wall pairs with their widths. Borrowed on trust: the Euler–Mascheroni constant and the formula \(H_n \approx \ln n + \gamma\) (standard, and the \(q\)-ladder's residue receipt matches it without proving it); the value \(\zeta(1.01) \approx 100.58\); the Littlewood–Paley theorem, the Calderón–Zygmund theory, the Whitney and martingale structures, and Haar completeness on the infinite room — Example 3's four tools, named and shelved here, proved nowhere on this page; and the historical dates of §7, from the published record, sources in the page log. The pattern's honest summary: the shelves carry the behavior; the constants are borrowed; and the difference between those two sentences is the move's price tag.

6 · The doors this opens

Door one, the working door: propagation of singularities — P52, microlocal analysis. This page's shelves are the coarse draft of that theory's central act: a singularity is located not just in space but in the directions and scales of a function's frequency content — a dyadic census of bands, one per octave — and the theorem says that singularities travel along explicit curves through that census. The Littlewood–Paley bands of Example 3 are the door's hinge, and this page is the hinge's prereq: P52's catalog row names this page as one of its two entry requirements. The next step up the ladder makes the shelf a coordinate system for roughness.

Door two, the far door: rescaling for asymptotic geometry — P56. This page's ruler was fixed and dyadic; the far move asks what happens when the rescaling itself is the instrument — zoom out, renormalize, watch a complicated object converge to a smooth limit, with the shelves of this page as the zoom's individual frames. The questions P15 trapped between walls, and this page shelved by scale, both return there promoted from tactics to a whole geometry.

Door three, the widening door: the shelf habit, everywhere. You now own a question that costs nothing to ask and disciplines everything it touches: what are the shelves, what does each one carry, and does the story-sum close? It files a series (condensation), a signal (wavelet energies), a budget (amortized doubling — each regrowth a shelf, the story-sum constant), a database index, a music practice schedule (octaves), and an unstructured problem that has resisted three direct attacks — the last because "resisted three direct attacks" is often the smell of an object mixing scales that the attacks each treated at one scale only. The habit's honesty clause, learned in Exhibit A, must travel with it: the shelves return behavior and walls, and the moment you find yourself quoting a shelf's value, you have left the method and should say so.

7 · Where the move came from

The oldest shelf proof in the record predates the words for it by six centuries. Around 1350, in the Questiones super geometriam Euclidis, Nicole Oresme grouped the harmonic series' terms by doubling bands — \(1\), then \(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3}\), then \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) through \(\tfrac{1}{7}\), then a band he could already see carrying "more than one half" — and concluded the series diverges: the first shelf argument in European mathematics, filed under a manuscript about Euclid, two and a half centuries before logarithms existed to name what the shelves were summing. Pietro Mengoli printed the first published proof in his Novæ quadraturæ arithmeticæ (1650), and Jakob Bernoulli gave the argument its modern home in the Tractatus de seriebus infinitis (1689) — the family trade: Jakob credits the proof to his brother Johann, and the brothers' rivalry is the book's first reminder that a five-line proof can be worth fighting over.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy made the shelves a theorem — the condensation test of the Cours d'analyse (1821): a nonnegative decreasing series and its dyadic condensation \(\sum 2^j a_{2^j}\) converge or diverge together, which is exactly this page's Box P42.A with the story-sum promoted to an equivalence. Then the shelf crossed from series to rooms. Alfréd Haar's 1910 dissertation (published in Mathematische Annalen) built the first orthonormal system on the halving tree — Example 2's rack; Joseph Walsh's 1923 system of \(\pm 1\) waves gave the tree its arithmetic-est register. In 1931 Littlewood and Paley began the frequency shelving (three papers, 1931 to 1938, in the Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society), extended through the 1930s by Marcinkiewicz and Zygmund and carried to higher dimensions by Stein; in 1934 Whitney's extension theorem shelved open sets by distance; in 1952 Calderón and Zygmund published the stopping decomposition in Acta Mathematica; Joseph Doob built the martingale theory across the 1940s and codified it in his 1953 monograph Stochastic Processes; and in 1972 Charles Fefferman and Elias Stein's work on \(H^p\) spaces of several variables (Acta Mathematica 129) fused the threads — bands, square functions, maximal functions — into the modern theory that the twenty-first century then rebuilt on explicitly dyadic model trees. The dates are the page's seasoning, not its load-bearing walls: every verdict above stands on the shelves and the machines, and the history is the story of how many different rooms noticed the same filing system at once.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ · Run the census by hand

(a) Compute the stories of shelves \(0\) through \(4\) of the harmonic series as exact fractions — \(1\), then \(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3}\), then \(\tfrac{1}{4} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{7}\), then the \(8\)-term shelf, then the \(16\)-term shelf — and check each against the wall-pair \((\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]\). (b) The confident friend of §2 claims each shelf carries exactly \(1\), and his twin claims each carries exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). Your five fractions: which claim do they kill, and how many times? (c) A third twin proposes shelves \([2^j, 2^{j+1}]\) — endpoints included on both ends. What does the census principle (Box P42.A, obligation 1) say about this ruler, and what does it do to the double-counted terms?

Hint

For (a), the common denominators are small: \(6\) for shelf \(1\), \(420\) for shelf \(2\). For (c): a term sitting on two shelves is not a rounding error — it is a broken partition, and the machine's wall-check will not be checking the object you think it is checking.

Worked answer

(a) Shelf \(0\): \(1\). Shelf \(1\): \(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{5}{6}\). Shelf \(2\): \(\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{5} + \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{7} = \tfrac{105 + 84 + 70 + 60}{420} = \tfrac{319}{420}\). Shelf \(3\): \(\tfrac{1}{8} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{15} = \tfrac{52279}{72072} \approx 0.7254\). Shelf \(4\): a fraction whose reduced denominator has fourteen digits, \(\approx 0.7090\). All five sit strictly inside \((\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]\), with only shelf \(0\) touching the top wall. (b) Each fraction except the first kills the "exactly \(1\)" claim — four tombstones — and none of the five reaches down to \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the "exactly \(\tfrac{1}{2}\)" twin dies five times over: no shelf ever carries as little as a half. The honest residues of both claims are the walls themselves: between \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) and \(1\), always, and the page's machines confirm all twenty shelves obey. (c) The doubled-endpoint ruler breaks obligation 1: a partition must place every member on exactly one shelf, but \(2, 4, 8, \ldots\) now sit on two shelves each, so the "shelf sums" overlap and their total exceeds \(H(N)\) by \(\sum_j \tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}}\), a small but real corruption — the census's walls would then be walls around the wrong object. The repair is the half-open convention \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\): every integer on exactly one shelf, nothing double-counted, and the audit trail clean.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · The two logarithms, sentenced by shelves

Two series stand at the boundary: \(\sum_{n \geq 2} \tfrac{1}{n \ln n}\) and \(\sum_{n \geq 2} \tfrac{1}{n \ln^2 n}\). (a) Derive the shelf walls for each: show every shelf of the first sits in \(\left[\tfrac{1}{2(j+1)\ln 2}, \tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\right]\) and every shelf of the second is at most \(\left(\tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\right)^2\). (b) Sentence each series — converge or diverge — naming the story-sum that settles it. (c) The machine receipts: the first sums to \(3.4239\) at \(2^{20}\), the second to \(2.0376\). Explain why neither number, alone, is evidence of its series' verdict — and what, in your shelf argument, is.

Hint

For (a), recall \(\ln 2^j = j \ln 2\), and bound each term of shelf \(j\) by evaluating at the shelf's two ends. For (c), compare with §2's machine friend: what can a partial sum never prove, and what can a wall always prove?

Worked answer

(a) First series: on shelf \(j\), the largest term is \(\tfrac{1}{2^j \cdot j \ln 2}\) (at the left end) and the smallest is essentially \(\tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}(j+1)\ln 2}\) (at the right end), so the \(2^j\)-term shelf carries between \(2^j \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}(j+1)\ln 2} = \tfrac{1}{2(j+1)\ln 2}\) and \(2^j \cdot \tfrac{1}{2^j j \ln 2} = \tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\). Second series: the same left-end estimate gives shelf \(\leq 2^j \cdot \tfrac{1}{(2^j \cdot j\ln 2)^2} = \tfrac{1}{2^j (j \ln 2)^2} \leq \left(\tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\right)^2\). (b) First: the story-sum is bounded below by a constant times \(\sum \tfrac{1}{j+1}\) — harmonic, divergent — so the series diverges. Second: the story-sum is bounded above by \(\sum \left(\tfrac{1}{j \ln 2}\right)^2\), a constant times \(\sum \tfrac{1}{j^2}\) — convergent — so the series converges. The verdicts come from the walls: a lower wall that diverges forces divergence; an upper wall that converges forces convergence. (c) The numbers are the machine friend's report — \(3.4239\) after a million terms is compatible with a series that converges to \(3.5\), and \(2.0376\) is compatible with one that diverges at logarithm-squared speed; no partial sum certifies behavior. The evidence is the shelf structure: a proof that all shelves obey the wall, which is a statement about every shelf at once — the one thing a finite sum can never simulate and the one thing the five-line argument delivers for free.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The census on eight digits of \(\pi\)

Run Example 2's census yourself on the signal \((3, 1, 4, 1, 5, 9, 2, 6)\). (a) Compute the average of the whole room, the two half-averages, the four pair-averages — the tree of Fig P42.2 — and read off the seven split dials, normalized. (b) Verify Parseval: the sum of squared dials must equal \(3^2 + 1^2 + 4^2 + 1^2 + 5^2 + 9^2 + 2^2 + 6^2 = 173\). (c) Build the per-shelf energy ladder — constant, halves, quarters, finest — and say what this signal's story is: smooth, rough, or mixed, and where.

Hint

Work unnormalized first — integer splits, as in the integer census \((36, -16, -4, -4, -1, \ldots)\) of the ramp — then divide by the window sizes: \(\sqrt{8}\) for the whole-room row, \(2\) for quarters, \(\sqrt{2}\) for pairs. For (c), square the normalized dials and group by shelf: the four summands should tell you where the digits of \(\pi\) keep their roughness.

Worked answer

(a) Whole-room average: \(\tfrac{31}{8}\); normalized constant dial: \(\tfrac{31}{\sqrt{8}} \approx 10.9602\). Halves: left sum \(9\), right sum \(22\); halves dial: \(\tfrac{9 - 22}{\sqrt{8}} = -\tfrac{13}{\sqrt{8}} \approx -4.5962\). Quarters: \((3+1) - (4+1) = -1\), dial \(-\tfrac{1}{2} = -0.5\); \((5+9) - (2+6) = 6\), dial \(3\). Pairs: \((3-1)/\sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\); \((4-1)/\sqrt{2} \approx 2.1213\); \((5-9)/\sqrt{2} \approx -2.8284\); \((2-6)/\sqrt{2} \approx -2.8284\). The dial list: \((10.9602, -4.5962, -0.5, 3, 1.4142, 2.1213, -2.8284, -2.8284)\). (b) Squares: \(120.125 + 21.125 + 0.25 + 9 + 2 + 4.5 + 8 + 8 = 173\), exactly the input energy — Parseval holds to the digit, and the round trip (transform, invert) returns \((3,1,4,1,5,9,2,6)\) unchanged. (c) The ladder: \(120.125\) at the constant, \(21.125\) at halves, \(9.25\) at quarters, \(22.5\) at the finest shelf. The story is mixed: the trend lives at the coarse end (\(141.25\) of the \(173\) in the top two shelves), but the finest shelf carries more energy than the quarters — \(22.5\) against \(9.25\) — because \(9\) against \(2\), and \(5\) against \(9\), are jumps, not slopes. That is the diagnostic in action: no smoothness (energy would quarter per octave, \(4\)-ish at the finest), no pure roughness (it would double), but readable structure — a trend with digits rattling on top, which is what a census of \(\pi\)'s digits was always going to be.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The disguise — diagnosed and sentenced

A friend proposes to settle the series \(\sum_{n \geq 1} \tfrac{1}{n^{1 + 1/\ln n}}\) by inspection: "the exponent is \(1 + \tfrac{1}{\ln n}\), which is greater than \(1\), so by the \(p\)-test the series converges." (a) Find the flaw — it is not in the arithmetic. (b) Sentence the series with the shelf machine, showing your work at one full shelf. (c) State the repair to the friend's theorem so that it becomes true.

Worked answer

(a) The flaw is that the \(p\)-test has no moving parts: it sentences \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n^p}\) for a fixed \(p > 1\), and here the exponent is not fixed — \(1 + \tfrac{1}{\ln n}\) is a function of \(n\) that decays toward \(1\), so no single \(p\) governs the whole series. The inspection quoted a theorem about one object to sentence a different one. (b) Simplify the term first: \(n^{1/\ln n} = e^{\ln n / \ln n} = e\), exactly, for every \(n\) — the "variable exponent" is a disguise for a constant. So the series is \(\sum \tfrac{1}{e \, n}\): the harmonic series in a coat. Shelf \(j\) carries \(\tfrac{1}{e} \left(\tfrac{1}{2^{j+1}} + \cdots\right) \in \left(\tfrac{1}{2e}, \tfrac{1}{e}\right]\) — the harmonic walls, rescaled by \(\tfrac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679\), and every wall still above zero, summing to divergence: \(H(N)/e\) passes any bound you name. The series diverges, and the divergence is exactly \(e\)-times slower than the harmonic's. (c) The honest repaired statement: if \(a_n \leq \tfrac{C}{n^p}\) for a fixed \(p > 1\) and a fixed constant \(C\), then \(\sum a_n\) converges — the comparison test with a fixed exponent, which is what the friend's inspection was trying to remember. The moving-exponent trap is worth keeping: exponents that drift toward the boundary are this page's subject matter, and the shelf machine is the instrument that reads them — one shelf, at one scale, with the exponent evaluated where it actually stands.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The wall ladder, and the wall it cannot climb

Take the harmonic sum at \(N = 2^{20}\), true value \(14.4402\), and run the shelf machine with a family of rulers: ratio \(q = 2\), then \(\tfrac{3}{2}\), then \(\tfrac{9}{8}\), then \(\tfrac{17}{16}\) — shelves \([q^j, q^{j+1})\), one bound per shelf, walls summed. (a) Derive the wall pair for general \(q\) and predict the direction the walls move as \(q \to 1\). (b) The machine's ladder reads \([10.00, 20.00]\), \([11.29, 17.30]\), \([12.81, 15.13]\), \([13.13, 14.77]\). Verify the second pair by hand from the integral comparison inside each shelf. (c) The walls close — but the machine says every lower wall stays below \(\ln(2^{20}) = 13.8629\) and the truth sits \(0.5772\) above that, a gap named \(\gamma\). Explain why no ratio, however close to \(1\), closes this last wall — and why that is a feature of the method rather than a bug in the rulers.

Hint

For (a): a shelf of ratio \(q\) holds roughly \((q-1)q^j\) terms, each between \(\tfrac{1}{q^{j+1}}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{q^j}\). For (b): the number of shelves grows like \(\ln N / \ln q\), so watch both the per-shelf width and the shelf count move as \(q\) moves. For (c): compare where the walls are converging with where the truth actually stands — the shelf argument sums an integral in disguise, and \(\gamma\) is exactly what an integral cannot see about a sum.

Worked answer

(a) A \(q\)-shelf \([q^j, q^{j+1})\) carries about \((q-1)q^j\) terms, each at most \(\tfrac{1}{q^j}\) and at least \(\tfrac{1}{q^{j+1}}\), so the shelf carries between \(\tfrac{q-1}{q}\) and \(q - 1\) — walls of relative width \(q\) around the story. Summing over the \(J \approx \ln N / \ln q\) shelves to \(N\): the total walls are \(\left[\tfrac{q-1}{q} \cdot \tfrac{\ln N}{\ln q},\; (q-1) \cdot \tfrac{\ln N}{\ln q}\right]\). As \(q \to 1\), both \(\tfrac{q-1}{\ln q} \to 1\), so both walls converge to \(\ln N\), and the bracket collapses onto \(\ln N\) — from both sides, at speed proportional to \(\ln q\). (b) At \(q = \tfrac{3}{2}\): each shelf carries between \(\tfrac{1}{3}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\); the number of shelves to \(2^{20}\) is about \(\ln 2^{20} / \ln 1.5 \approx 34\); the smooth walls are therefore roughly \(\left[\tfrac{34}{3}, \tfrac{34}{2}\right] = [11.3, 17.0]\), and the machine's honest integer-shelved version \([11.29, 17.30]\) brackets the same story while paying the integer bills (partial shelves, a leftover term) that the smooth derivation rounds away — hand derivation and census in agreement. (c) The \(q \to 1\) limit of the wall-pair is exactly the integral test's bracket \([\ln N, 1 + \ln N]\) — the ladder's last rung is calculus, which is why the widths in Exhibit A fall toward one, not toward zero. The truth \(H(N)\) stands \(\gamma\) above \(\ln N\) forever, so the collapsing walls converge to a point \(\gamma\)-below their target: the shelf machine measures the object's scale structure, which the integral sees, and \(\gamma\) is the fine-grained record of the integers' deviation from their own integral — one part in \(2n\), summed over every shelf, invisible to every wall. A feature: the method's verdicts (divergence, rate, comparison) never needed the constant; the residue tells you precisely what to go get from the machine or the borrowed formula when a value is wanted — and never to dress a wall up as a reading.
