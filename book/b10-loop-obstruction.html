The Patterns Atlas · B10 · bridge page, group C · before P33

Potential differences and the loop obstruction

A surveyor hands you numbers on roads and refuses to say the altitudes. Sometimes a hidden column of town numbers stands behind the table — B09 proved such tables behave perfectly: every route agrees, every closed trip cancels — and sometimes none does, as one triangle of \(+1\)'s already showed. This page answers the question B09 planted and fenced off: which tables of edge numbers come from vertex numbers? The answer is a single criterion, honest and complete — a table qualifies exactly when it passes every loop test, meaning the signed sum around every cycle is \(0\), Kirchhoff's loop law. The proof of the hard direction is a construction the reader already owns: grow a potential along a spanning tree, root-and-accumulate as in B09's tree working, and let every remaining edge sit its own examination. The count of genuinely independent tests is the number B09's exploration promised — \(E - V + 1\), one per edge beyond a spanning tree — and this page proves the count two ways: the tests are independent (each owns a private edge) and they suffice (every cycle is assembled from those few, a fact proved by bookkeeping at the towns, the divergence-style argument B09 said would live here). The page ends where group C always pointed: the obstruction measured, named, and handed to P33.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern — the pages that use this concept come after it, and this page is the floor they stand on. It belongs to group C — graphs, potentials, telescoping — and it is the group's third and last door: B11 taught the ledger on a line, B09 taught the ledger on a graph and proved what potentials buy, and this page settles which tables the potentials can be recovered from. The reader wants three habits from the spine: the small-case census of P02 — every verdict below is checked on tables small enough to audit by hand — the two-way check of P78 — every table is tested forward by loops and rebuilt backward by accumulation — and the naming move of P12: when a number refuses to change no matter what you do, name it, because it is the obstruction. The mechanism underneath is still P69's collapsing sum; nothing on this page is beyond school arithmetic. Take a pencil: before any notation appears, three surveyors' tables and one square will show you the whole story.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Three towns again, one road between each pair, arrows agreed once and for all: \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\). B09 left this triangle in your hands twice: a first surveyor's table \(+50, -75, +25\) came from the altitudes \(40, 90, 15\), and a second surveyor's \(+1, +1, +1\) refused every altitude column — the three demands add to \(0 = 3\), which is not so. Take a third table before any theory: \(+1, +1, -2\). Walk the loop: \((+1) + (+1) + (-2) = 0\). The loop dies. Now try to manufacture altitudes, exactly as B09's tree working taught: declare \(\mathrm{A} = 0\), and let each road dictate the next town:

The last line is the whole event: the third demand, checked against values the first two already fixed, agrees. The altitude column \(0, 1, 2\) stands behind the table — and it would have agreed from any root: \(100, 101, 102\) manufactures the same three numbers, the gauge freedom you already met in B09. On this smallest of graphs, the pencil has watched both verdicts happen: a table whose loop dies has a column behind it; a table whose loop totals \(3\) has none. One test, one loop, and the test decided existence.

Now make the graph one edge richer, and watch a verdict go wrong. Four towns at a square's corners — \(\mathrm{A}\), \(\mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{D}\) in order — roads along the sides plus one diagonal shortcut from \(\mathrm{A}\) to \(\mathrm{C}\). A fourth surveyor hands you five numbers: sides \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\): \(+4\), \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\): \(-9\), \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\): \(+7\), \(\mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\): \(-2\), shortcut \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C}\): \(-6\). Is a hidden column behind them? Test the obvious loop first, the square itself: \(4 - 9 + 7 - 2 = 0\). It dies. Emboldened, manufacture: declare \(\mathrm{A} = 0\) and let each side dictate the next town, walking \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\):

The square closes on itself — the manufactured column \(0, 4, -5, 2\) satisfies all four side roads, and the square test never noticed the shortcut. But the shortcut is sitting right there with its own demand: \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C}\) says \(-6\), and the column says \(\mathrm{C} - \mathrm{A} = -5 - 0 = -5\). The table demands \(-6\); the towns deliver \(-5\). Off by one. So: does some other column work? Try the remaining loops. The low triangle \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\): \(4 - 9 + 6 = +1\) — the shortcut read backward is \(+6\). It refuses. The high triangle \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\): \(-6 + 7 - 2 = -1\). It refuses too, by the other sign. And the two refusals are not independent news: walked together they cover every side once and the shortcut twice in opposite directions, so their failures cancel — \((+1) + (-1) = 0\) — which is exactly why the big square test, the sum of the two small ones, passed and told you nothing. Three facts, one pencil: the big loop's pass was borrowed from two canceling failures; no column exists behind this table; and the number \(+1\) — the low triangle's total, or equally the shortcut's mismatch — measures exactly how far the table is from every altitude column in the world.

What the pencil just showed. On the triangle, one loop test decided existence outright. On the square with a shortcut, the same reasoning broke: passing the big loop did not decide anything, because one loop's arithmetic can be a sum of other loops' arithmetic — failures hiding inside failures. Before this is mathematics, four questions demand answers. What is the test, precisely? — the signed sum around a cycle, with the sign bookkeeping B09 called obligation 1. How many tests are genuinely independent? — the triangle needed one, the square-with-shortcut needed two, and B09's exploration conjectured \(E - V + 1\) in general; that count needs a proof, both halves of it: that many tests suffice, and that fewer never do. Does passing really guarantee a potential? — the triangle and the square suggest yes; the page owes a construction that works on every graph, and a proof that the construction cannot fail when the tests pass. And where does the failure live? — the \(+1\) survived every re-rooting and every gauge shift the pencil tried; if it is genuinely invariant, it deserves the name B09 promised it: an obstruction. The next section names all four answers.

2 · The idea, named

Lecture 0 ran on borrowed machinery: walks, cycles, sign bookkeeping, and the root-and-accumulate construction — all of it B09's, imported whole. What the pencil added is one observation: the tests are not a list to be trusted, they are arithmetic with structure — one loop's total is the sum of other loops' totals when the loops assemble that way — and the structure decides both the criterion and the count. Name the pieces.

Box B10 — the loop test, the criterion, and the obstruction. Fix a connected graph with an edge assignment \(\omega\) (B09's edge ledger: one number per edge, one reference arrow per edge, backward reads negated). For any cycle \(C\) walked in either direction, the loop total \(S(C)\) is the signed sum of \(\omega\) along the walk, each edge read with the sign of the traversal. Then:

The criterion. \(\omega\) is a rise table — some potential \(\varphi\) manufactures it, \(\omega(u \to v) = \varphi(v) - \varphi(u)\) on every edge — if and only if \(S(C) = 0\) for every cycle \(C\). The forward direction is B09's law T1. The backward direction, the one this page proves: pass every test and a potential exists, built by construction. As a circuit law — voltage for altitude, wire for road — the criterion's forward half is Kirchhoff's loop law: potential differences around any loop sum to zero.

The obstruction. If some cycle has \(S(C) \neq 0\), no potential manufactures \(\omega\), and the number \(S(C)\) is the honest measure of the refusal: it is invariant under everything the pencil could try. Re-root the construction elsewhere, shift every potential by a constant, re-choose every reference arrow — the loop total moves only by its own sign when the walking direction flips, never by value. A table fails by a number, and the number is the obstruction.

In plain words: ask of a table of road numbers not "where did it come from?" — you cannot see that — but "what does it do around a loop?". Around a loop, honest differences must cancel; a total that refuses to cancel can never have been differences; and the size of the refusal tells you how far from honest the table is. What the criterion does not do is count the tests — a graph can hold infinitely many cycles, and Lecture 0's square showed that testing big loops naively can waste work on sums of failures already caught. The count is Box B10.A's; the procedure is Box B10.B's.

Box B10.A — spanning trees, fundamental cycles, and the count \(E - V + 1\). A spanning tree of a connected graph on \(V\) vertices is a subgraph that reaches every vertex, connects them, and holds no cycle — a tree wearing all the towns. It has exactly \(V - 1\) edges (the proof is the next section's lemma, and the count is the reason the tree is here). Since the whole graph has \(E\) edges, the tree leaves

\[E \;-\; (V - 1) \;=\; E - V + 1\]

edges off the tree. Each off-tree edge \(e\), added to the tree, creates exactly one new cycle — because the tree already holds exactly one route between \(e\)'s two endpoints, and that route plus \(e\) closes a loop. That cycle is the fundamental cycle \(F_e\), one per off-tree edge, and these are the tests that matter:

Independence. Each \(F_e\) contains its own private off-tree edge \(e\), which no other fundamental cycle touches. So no fundamental test is implied by the others: a table can pass all but one, exactly as Lecture 0's square did.

Sufficiency. Every cycle's total is a signed sum of fundamental totals — assembly arithmetic, proved in section 4 — so a table that passes the \(E - V + 1\) fundamental tests passes every test there is. Fewer tests never suffice, because of independence; more are free, because of sufficiency. The number of genuinely independent loop tests a graph holds is \(E - V + 1\) — B09's exploration conjecture, now a theorem with both halves proved.

Calibration on the four graphs of B09's exploration: the six-town tree, \(E - V + 1 = 0\) — no tests, every table qualifies, B09's Claim 4 recovered as the count's zero row; the triangle, \(1\); the square with a shortcut, \(2\) — Lecture 0's pencil verdict, explained; the complete graph \(K_4\), \(3\).

Box B10.B — deciding a table by hand: three obligations. The criterion becomes a procedure the size of a napkin. Given numbers on roads and no map of altitudes:

1. Choose a spanning tree and root it. Any tree reaching every town, any root \(r\); declare \(\varphi(r) = 0\). The choice is free — the verdict will not depend on it, by sufficiency — but write the choice down, because obligation 1 of B09 still rules: the arithmetic runs on declared walks, not on pictures.

2. Accumulate along the tree. For each town \(v\), add the rises along the one tree route \(r \to v\), reading each road with the sign of the traversal. This manufactures a column of potentials, and B09's Claim 4 guarantees the column's rise table agrees with \(\omega\) on every tree edge — the tree asks no questions. The column is unique up to the gauge constant of B09's Claim 3.

3. Let every off-tree edge examine itself. For each of the \(E - V + 1\) off-tree edges \(u \to v\), compare the table's number with the column's difference: does \(\omega(u \to v) = \varphi(v) - \varphi(u)\)? Agreement on every off-tree edge means the column manufactures the whole table — the table qualifies, and the criterion's backward direction is exactly this check, impossible to fail. The first disagreement, if there is one, is the obstruction caught in the act: the mismatch \(\varphi(v) - \varphi(u) - \omega(u \to v)\) equals the loop total of that edge's fundamental cycle walked tree-route-first (the route from \(u\) to \(v\), then the edge back), and the table is refused — by every potential in the world, the criterion's forward direction seals it.

In plain words: build what the tree lets you build for free, then spend your attention only where the graph can say no. The tree edges cannot refuse; the off-tree edges each hold one test; and the count of tests is the count of off-tree edges. Most errors of this subject are sign errors in step 2's accumulation or step 3's comparison — write the walk, then subtract, the discipline B09 set and this page keeps.

  • New word: loop total. The signed sum of an edge assignment around a cycle, each edge read with the sign of its traversal. The one number a cycle extracts from a table — and the one number a genuine rise table always surrenders as \(0\). Reading the cycle the other way flips the total's sign; the zero totals are zero in both directions, which is why the test's verdict never depends on the walking direction chosen.
  • New word: spanning tree. A subgraph that reaches every vertex, connects them, and holds no cycle — a minimal skeleton of the graph, the shape on which every edge assignment whatsoever can be accumulated (B09's Claim 4). The number of edges a spanning tree holds, \(V - 1\), is the graph's floor; everything beyond it is examination material.
  • New word: fundamental cycle. The one cycle an off-tree edge creates when added to a chosen spanning tree: the tree's unique route between its endpoints, closed by the edge itself. One per off-tree edge; private to that edge; and the complete examination board for the whole graph, however many other cycles it holds.
  • New word: obstruction. A quantity that refuses to change while everything around it is free to move, and whose refusal therefore names an impossibility. Here: a nonzero loop total, invariant under re-rooting, gauge shifts, and re-arrowing, blocking the table from every potential column at once — one number, every altitude in the world refused. The spine's P12 performs this move on problems; this page performs it on a number.

Calibration, honest side first. What the definitions buy:

  • The triangle's three surveyors, one line each: \(+50, -75, +25\) totals \(0\) — qualifies, altitudes \(40, 90, 15\); \(+1, +1, -2\) totals \(0\) — qualifies, altitudes \(0, 1, 2\) by accumulation; \(+1, +1, +1\) totals \(3\) — refused, and the census is total: checking every table with entries from \(-3\) to \(3\), all \(343\) of them, exactly \(37\) pass, and they are precisely the tables whose loop total is \(0\) — a brute-force search over every altitude column in range agrees with the loop test on every one of the \(343\), the criterion machine-checked on the smallest case (P02's habit, and the pattern generalizes: the section 4 proof is the census grown up).
  • Lecture 0's square, explained at last: the big square test is the sum of the two triangle tests — its total \(0\) was borrowed from failures \(+1\) and \(-1\) that cancel. A table can pass any one test and still be refused; passing is only meaningful once the \(E - V + 1 = 2\) fundamental tests are all accounted for.
  • The tree's row in the count: \(E - V + 1 = 0\). No off-tree edges, no examinations, nothing that can refuse — B09's Claim 4, every table qualifies, now read as the zero row of the count rather than a separate miracle.
  • One graph, many spanning trees — and one verdict. The square with a shortcut holds several spanning trees; the tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\}\) examines the off-tree roads \(\mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\) and the shortcut, while the tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}, \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\}\) examines \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\) and the shortcut — different fundamental cycles, same verdicts. The exploration asks whether that is luck.

A short history, none of it load-bearing. The subject of this page is really the second of the two laws Kirchhoff found as a student in 1845 — currents balancing at each junction, and potential differences summing to zero around each loop — and it was Kirchhoff's 1847 paper on the linear distribution of galvanic currents that turned the counting insight loose: a network's independent loops reduce to what remains beyond a spanning tree, exactly the \(E - V + 1\) of Box B10.A, the engineer's version of the fact this page proves as Claim 3. The trees themselves carry the name Cayley gave them in 1857, and B09 already told both stories; this page adds only the observation that Kirchhoff's reduction and B09's exploration conjecture are the same sentence in two costumes. None of these dates carries any claim on this page; the criterion is proved from first principles in section 4.

3 · Worked by hand

Three workings, in order of the count: the triangle (one test), the square with a shortcut (two tests, the pencil's own case re-run as procedure), and \(K_4\) (three tests, the complete graph on four towns). Each ends the way P78 teaches — the table tested forward by loops, then rebuilt backward by accumulation, the two readings forced to agree — and each names its obstruction out loud when one appears. The figures are drawn from the same numbers the text computes.

(a) The triangle — one test, and the census behind it

Run Box B10.B on the second surveyor's table \(+1, +1, +1\): choose the spanning tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\}\), root \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) = 0\), accumulate: \(\varphi(\mathrm{B}) = 0 + 1 = 1\), \(\varphi(\mathrm{C}) = 1 + 1 = 2\). The one off-tree edge is \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\): the column's difference is \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) - \varphi(\mathrm{C}) = 0 - 2 = -2\); the table says \(+1\). The mismatch is \(-2 - 1 = -3\), and the fundamental cycle — the only cycle — totals \(+3\): same refusal, opposite sign, because the mismatch subtracts the table from the column while the loop sums the table against the tree's accumulated \(+2\). The table is refused by every altitude column there is. Now repair it: to pass, the third entry must be \(-2\), and then the column \(0, 1, 2\) — or \(100, 101, 102\), the gauge — manufactures the table exactly. Fig B10.1 draws the refusal.

One more reading, the census habit of P02 made small enough to hold: every triangle table with entries from \(-3\) to \(3\) — \(343\) tables — was tested both ways, by the loop and by brute-force search for an altitude column. The two verdicts agreed on every single table: exactly \(37\) qualify, the ones with loop total \(0\). On this graph the criterion is not just proved, it is censused.

4 · Why it is true

Three claims settle everything the page promised. Claim 1 is the obstruction's certificate — one line, and it is B09's. Claim 2 is the converse, the hard direction, and its proof is the procedure of Box B10.B performed once in the abstract. Claim 3 is the count, and its proof is the divergence-style bookkeeping B09 said would live on this page: sums balancing at the towns, and a leaf with nowhere to hide.

Claim 1 (rise tables pass; failures refuse). If \(\omega\) is the rise table of some potential \(\varphi\), then \(S(C) = 0\) on every cycle \(C\). If some cycle has \(S(C) \neq 0\), no potential manufactures \(\omega\) — and the obstruction survives re-rooting, gauge shifts, and re-arrowing unchanged.

Proof. The first sentence is B09's law T1, proved there as Claim 2 by one telescope: each edge of the walked cycle contributes \(\varphi(\text{head}) - \varphi(\text{tail})\) regardless of the arrow's direction, and around a closed walk the telescope collapses: \[S(C) \;=\; \varphi(\text{last}) - \varphi(\text{first}) \;=\; 0,\] the first and last towns being one and the same. The second sentence is the contrapositive read as refusal. For the invariance: re-rooting and gauge shifts change \(\varphi\) only by adding constants — B09's Claim 3 — and \(S(C)\) never mentions \(\varphi\) at all, only \(\omega\); re-arrowing an edge flips both the reference read of \(\omega\) and the sign the traversal assigns, so every signed contribution is unchanged; walking the cycle the other way negates \(S(C)\), and \(-0 = 0\). A nonzero loop total is therefore a property of the table and the graph alone — one number, every potential in the world refused at once. \(\square\)

Claim 2 (the converse — pass every test and a potential exists). If \(S(C) = 0\) for every cycle \(C\), then \(\omega\) is a rise table: some \(\varphi\) manufactures it. The construction is Box B10.B's.

Proof. Choose a spanning tree \(T\), root \(r\), declare \(\varphi(r) = 0\), and for each vertex \(v\) define \(\varphi(v)\) as the signed sum of \(\omega\) along the one tree route \(r \to v\) — the unique-routes lemma of B09's Claim 4 guarantees the route exists and is single. Two families of edges to check.

Tree edges. B09's Claim 4, invoked verbatim: its two-case argument — the route to the tail either misses the head, in which case appending the edge gives the route to the head, or it visits the head, in which case the tail of the route from the head must be this very edge walked backward — shows \(\varphi(b) - \varphi(a) = \omega(a \to b)\) for every tree edge \(a \to b\). Nothing new is needed; the tree never refuses.

Off-tree edges. Take an off-tree edge \(a \to b\). The tree route \(a \to b\), walked forward, totals \(\varphi(b) - \varphi(a)\) — that is B09's Claim 1 applied to the potential under construction, whose rise table on tree edges is already known to be \(\omega\). Close the route with the edge \(b \to a\), read backward as \(-\omega(a \to b)\): the result is the fundamental cycle \(F_{a \to b}\), a genuine cycle of the graph, so \(S(F) = 0\) by hypothesis. Its total is \[\big(\varphi(b) - \varphi(a)\big) \;-\; \omega(a \to b) \;=\; 0,\] which rearranges to \(\varphi(b) - \varphi(a) = \omega(a \to b)\) — the column agrees with the table on this edge too. Every tree edge and every off-tree edge now agrees, so \(\varphi\)'s rise table is \(\omega\) throughout: the table qualifies. \(\square\)

Read together, Claims 1 and 2 are the criterion of Box B10, both directions proved, and the proof of the hard direction is the procedure — which is why the procedure cannot fail when the tests pass: failure would contradict the construction it embodies. One proof, one algorithm, the P78 habit at book size. What remains is the count, and its two halves have different characters: sufficiency is assembly arithmetic, independence is a private-edge argument — both are short.

Claim 3 (the count — \(E - V + 1\) independent tests, and no fewer). Fix a connected graph on \(V\) vertices with \(E\) edges and a spanning tree \(T\). The \(E - V + 1\) fundamental cycles of \(T\) have totals from which every cycle's total is a signed sum; and no fundamental total is a signed sum of the others. So exactly \(E - V + 1\) loop tests are independent, and they suffice for every test the graph can set.

The proof leans on one bookkeeping device — the one B09 promised this page would carry — and it gets its own subsection.

The divergence bookkeeping — why a tree has nothing to say

Lemma (closed tree trips balance). Any closed trip that travels only tree roads — repetitions allowed, either direction, any length — crosses each road the same number of times in each direction. Its signed total is \(0\) for every edge assignment whatsoever.

Proof. Keep, for each road and each direction, a tally of crossings; a closed trip has the property that at every town the number of arrivals equals the number of departures — what flows in must flow out, the junction balance Kirchhoff wrote for currents and the reason this argument is called divergence-style. Now strip leaves. A tree has a leaf — a town on a single road — and the balance at that town forces the road's tallies in and out to be equal: departures have nowhere else to go. Cancel the paired crossings and delete the leaf town with its road: the remaining tallies are still balanced at every remaining town, on a smaller tree. Repeat — the last tree is one town, holding no roads, and no crossings survived to it. Unwinding, every road's two tallies were forced equal at the moment its end was stripped. A trip that crosses each road equally both ways reads each number as many times positively as negatively: total \(0\), whatever the numbers were. \(\square\)

Proof of Claim 3, sufficiency half. Let \(C\) be any cycle, walked in some direction. For each off-tree edge \(e\) that \(C\) crosses, note the direction; subtract from \(C\)'s crossing tally the fundamental cycle \(F_e\)'s tally, oriented so that its crossing of \(e\) cancels \(C\)'s. The difference of two balanced tallies is balanced, so what remains is a closed trip on tree roads alone — balance is exactly the closed-trip property — and the Lemma totals it at \(0\), for any table. Unwinding the subtractions: \(S(C) = \sum_{e} \pm S(F_e)\), a signed sum of fundamental totals, as promised. Independence half. Suppose some combination \(\sum_e c_e S(F_e)\) of fundamental totals is claimed to be forced by the others — the honest form is: some signed sum of fundamental cycles is identically zero as a crossing tally, \(\sum_e c_e F_e \equiv 0\). Read the tally on the private edge of \(F_{e_0}\): every other fundamental cycle crosses \(e_0\) zero times, so the tally says \(c_{e_0} = 0\). Every coefficient vanishes; no fundamental test is implied by the rest. \(\square\)

The count is now doubly pinned: \(E - V + 1\) tests suffice (every cycle assembles from them), and none can be dropped (each owns a private edge). Lecture 0's pencil verdicts were this theorem in miniature — the square's big test was the sum of two triangle tests, and the triangle needed its one test exactly. One corollary closes the section: the verdict does not depend on the spanning tree chosen — a different tree selects different fundamental cycles, but passing one complete set of independent tests is equivalent, through the assembly arithmetic, to passing any other, and both are equivalent to the criterion. The exploration asks you to watch that happen by hand.

Scope, honestly. What this page settles is binary: a table qualifies or it does not, and the obstruction measures the refusal. What it deliberately does not do: rank repairs (a failing table can be mended by changing edge values — the shortcut's \(-6\) to \(-5\) — and how many edges a repair must touch is a question the count can bound but this page does not pursue); handle the junction law (Kirchhoff's other law — currents balancing at each town — is a different bookkeeping, about a different ledger, and belongs to the story of flows, not potentials); or climb dimensions (on a surface, the obstruction story repeats with faces in the game — data on edges of triangles, consistency on loops no longer enough — and that ladder is P33's to build, on exactly the floor this page lays). The obstruction here is one number per loop; the general theory names the whole shelf of such numbers, and the naming is the far page's work.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the triangle, refused and repaired

A surveyor on the triangle of arrows \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\) hands you \(+3\), \(-1\), \(+5\). (i) Run the loop test and state the obstruction. (ii) Find every way to repair the table by changing exactly one entry. (iii) For one of your repairs, manufacture the altitude column from \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) = 0\) and verify all three entries.

Hint

The loop total decides existence on this graph — one cycle, one test. For (ii), the test is one equation in the entries: ask, for each entry in turn, what value would make the sum \(0\). For (iii), accumulate along any two roads; the third road checks you.

Worked answer

(i) Total: \(3 - 1 + 5 = +7 \neq 0\) — refused, obstruction \(7\); no altitude column exists, and by Claim 1 none ever will. (ii) One equation, three one-entry repairs: change \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\) to \(-4\) (then \(-4 - 1 + 5 = 0\)); change \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\) to \(-8\) (then \(3 - 8 + 5 = 0\)); change \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\) to \(-2\) (then \(3 - 1 - 2 = 0\)). Any single entry can carry the repair — the obstruction fixes the sum, not the culprit. (iii) Take the third repair: \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) = 0\), \(\varphi(\mathrm{B}) = 0 + 3 = 3\), \(\varphi(\mathrm{C}) = 3 - 1 = 2\); check \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{A}\): \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) - \varphi(\mathrm{C}) = 0 - 2 = -2\) ✓. The column \(0, 3, 2\) manufactures the table; so does \(100, 103, 102\), the gauge of B09's Claim 3.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the square, the cancellation, and the forced repair

The table of Lecture 0: sides \(+4\), \(-9\), \(+7\), \(-2\), shortcut \(-6\). (i) Compute all three loop totals and check that the two failures cancel inside the pass. (ii) Verify the high triangle's total two ways: directly, and as the square minus the low triangle. (iii) Repair the table — which entries are candidates, and what does the repair force? — then manufacture the column and verify all five entries.

Hint

For (ii), assemble the high triangle from the other two cycles edge by edge: walked together, which roads cancel? For (iii), the spanning-tree procedure of Box B10.B does the forcing for you — accumulate along any three side roads, then read the shortcut's demand against the column's difference.

Worked answer

(i) Low triangle \(4 - 9 + 6 = +1\); high triangle \(-6 + 7 - 2 = -1\); square \(4 - 9 + 7 - 2 = 0\). And \(+1 + (-1) = 0\): the pass was borrowed from canceling failures — the square test is the assembly sum of the two triangle tests. (ii) Direct: \(-6 + 7 - 2 = -1\). Assembled: the high triangle's road tally equals the square's plus the low triangle walked in reverse (their shortcut crossings cancel, their shared side roads add), so its total is \(0 - (+1) = -1\) ✓ two routes, one number. (iii) Accumulate along \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\) from \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) = 0\): \(\varphi = (0, 4, -5, 2)\); the side roads close (\(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) - \varphi(\mathrm{D}) = -2\) ✓). The shortcut must read \(\varphi(\mathrm{C}) - \varphi(\mathrm{A}) = -5\); it reads \(-6\). Any repair must fix the obstruction's size: changing the shortcut to \(-5\) works and changes nothing else; changing side entries alone cannot — the sides already close consistently, so tilting one side wakes another test (try it: tilt \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\) and the square refuses). With the shortcut at \(-5\): \(\varphi = (0, 4, -5, 2)\) verifies all five — sides \(4, -9, 7, -2\) ✓ and shortcut \(-5\) ✓.

Exploration ◐ — two examiners, one table

The spanning-tree procedure of Box B10.B chose the tree of side roads \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\) — but the choice was ours, and the graph offers a second spanning tree built from the roads \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}\), \(\mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\), \(\mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\). A cynic asks the sharp question: could a table pass one examiner and fail the other? Run both procedures on the failing table of Lecture 0 (\(+4\), \(-9\), \(+7\), \(-2\), shortcut \(-6\)): list each tree's off-tree examinations, manufacture each column, compute each mismatch, and state both verdicts. Then run both on the repaired table (shortcut \(-5\)). Conjecture what a verdict can and cannot depend on, and say what would have to be true — in the language of Claim 2 and assembly — for two examiners ever to disagree. A receipt closes the exploration: over all \(3{,}125\) tables with entries in \([-2, 2]\) on this graph, the two trees' procedures, run entry by entry, agree with each other and with a brute-force column search on every single table; \(75\) tables pass.

Hint

Each tree keeps three roads and examines the other two — the count \(E - V + 1 = 5 - 4 + 1 = 2\) again. The column comes from the kept roads alone; the examinations read the column's demands against the two leftover entries. For the conjecture: a pass means a column exists — and existence never mentions the tree.

Worked answer

First examiner, the side tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\}\): the column is \((0, 4, -5, 2)\) (§3(b)); examinations — the road \(\mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\): demand \(-2\), table \(-2\), pass (the square) — and the shortcut \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C}\): demand \(-5\), table \(-6\), mismatch \(+1\), fail (the low triangle). Second examiner, the tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}, \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\}\): accumulate from \(\varphi(\mathrm{A}) = 0\) — \(\varphi(\mathrm{B}) = 4\), \(\varphi(\mathrm{D}) = 0 - (-2) = 2\), \(\varphi(\mathrm{C}) = 2 + (-7) = -5\) — the same column \((0, 4, -5, 2)\); examinations — the road \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\): the tree route \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{C}\) tallies \(-4 + 2 - 7 = -9\), demand \(-9\), table \(-9\), pass (the square, walked as \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{B}\)) — and the shortcut: the tree route \(\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{C}\) tallies \(2 - 7 = -5\), demand \(-5\), table \(-6\), mismatch \(+1\), fail (the high triangle walked in reverse). Same column, same mismatch, same verdict — but a different catching test: the low triangle for one examiner, the reversed high triangle for the other. On the repaired table both examiners manufacture the same column and pass both examinations — pass, twice. The conjecture the numbers force: the verdict is the table's alone. Here is why the cynic's hope dies. By Claim 2, passing under any tree means that tree's fundamental totals all read \(0\); by the assembly arithmetic, every cycle in the graph is an integer combination of any one tree's fundamental cycles — so if one examiner reads all zeros, every cycle totals \(0\), and the other examiner's tests, being cycles, read \(0\) too. Disagreement would need a walked cycle whose total depends on which tree sent it — but a total is entries read with signs along a walk, and the walk does not know. A third examiner makes the point from the other side: the tree \(\{\mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{B}, \mathrm{A} \to \mathrm{C}, \mathrm{C} \to \mathrm{D}\}\) keeps the shortcut, so the lie sits inside the column itself — \((0, 4, -6, 1)\) — and both its examinations fail by one (demand \(-10\) against table \(-9\) on \(\mathrm{B} \to \mathrm{C}\); demand \(-1\) against table \(-2\) on \(\mathrm{D} \to \mathrm{A}\)). The mismatch moves house; the verdict does not. The receipt: \(3{,}125\) tables, two trees, one brute-force search — three routes through the census, one answer per table, \(75\) passes.
