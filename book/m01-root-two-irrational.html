Multi-Pattern Synthesis · Landmark Theorems

M01 · \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational

Four distinct proofs of the first great discovery of pure mathematics — showing how Canonical Form (P05), Contradiction (P11), Infinite Descent (P13), and Prime Localization (P30) trade roles to prove that no fraction squares to \(2\).

Moves Combined: P05 · Put it in canonical form + P11 · Prove by contradiction + P13 · Infinite descent (with P67 · Prove the contrapositive & P30 · Localize at a prime)

On-ramp 1 — What is a Multi-Pattern Synthesis page? In the first \(78\) chapters of this Atlas, we isolated individual reasoning moves one at a time. In real mathematical discovery, a theorem is rarely proved by a single move in isolation: instead, patterns snap together like gears — one move sets up the representation, a second move performs the reduction, and a third move closes the trap. Better still, changing which pattern does the heavy lifting produces genuinely different proofs that generalize in different directions!

On-ramp 2 — Incommensurability and integer equations. Asking whether \(\sqrt{2}\) is a rational number \(\frac{a}{b}\) (with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\)) is identical to two concrete questions:

  • Geometric question (The Pythagoreans, c. 450 BCE): Does there exist a tiny common ruler of length \(\delta > 0\) that fits into the side \(b\) of a square an exact integer number of times and into its diagonal \(a = b\sqrt{2}\) an exact integer number of times?
  • Diophantine question: Does the quadratic equation \(a^2 - 2b^2 = 0\) have any solution in positive integers \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\)?

1 · Before we begin

We first met the irrationality of \(\sqrt{2}\) in P11 · Prove by contradiction. Now that we have the entire Atlas at our fingertips, look closely at the hidden machinery inside that proof — and see how four patterns collaborate:

  • P11 · Prove by contradiction supplies the outer wrapper: because "\(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational" is a negative statement (the absence of integer solutions to \(a^2 = 2b^2\)), assuming a solution \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) exists hands us two concrete integers to manipulate.
  • P05 · Put it in canonical form normalizes the hypothetical fraction \(\frac{a}{b}\) to lowest terms (\(\gcd(a, b) = 1\)) up front — or writes \(a\) and \(b\) in their canonical prime factorizations.
  • P67 · Prove the contrapositive proves the inner parity step: \(a^2\) even \(\implies a\) even (because \(a\) odd \(\implies a^2\) odd).
  • P13 · Infinite descent (paired with P71 · The extremal principle) eliminates the need for lowest terms altogether: instead of dividing \(a\) and \(b\) by \(2\), we can fold the square or overlap two \(b \times b\) squares inside an \(a \times a\) square to produce a strictly smaller integer solution \((2b - a,\; a - b)\) using subtraction alone!

2 · Meet the problem: A4 printer paper and the folding descent

Pick up a standard sheet of ISO A4 printer paper, which measures \(a_0 = 297\text{ mm}\) long by \(b_0 = 210\text{ mm}\) wide. Dividing both dimensions by \(3\text{ mm}\) gives \((a_0, b_0) = (99, 70)\) in \(3\text{ mm}\) grid units, with ratio \(\frac{99}{70} \approx 1.4142857\).

Why was \(99 : 70\) chosen for A4 paper? Because engineers wanted a rectangle whose aspect ratio \(\frac{a}{b}\) is preserved when you fold the sheet in half (\(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{b}{a/2} \iff a^2 = 2b^2\)), and \(99^2 - 2 \cdot 70^2 = 9801 - 9800 = +1\) is as close to \(0\) as two two-digit integers can get!

Now watch what happens if you apply the geometric corner-fold

\[ (a,\; b) \;\longmapsto\; (a',\; b') \;:=\; \bigl(2b - a,\;\; a - b\bigr) \]

to any pair of integers \((a, b)\). By expanding squares (P06), the discrepancy \(\Delta(a, b) := a^2 - 2b^2\) satisfies the exact sign-reversing identity:

\[ (a')^2 - 2(b')^2 \;=\; (2b - a)^2 - 2(a - b)^2 \;=\; (4b^2 - 4ab + a^2) - (2a^2 - 4ab + 2b^2) \;=\; -\bigl(a^2 - 2b^2\bigr)! \]

Compare what this folding map does to the A4 near-miss \((99, 70)\) (where \(\Delta = \pm 1\)) versus a hypothetical exact integer solution \((a, b)\) to \(a^2 - 2b^2 = 0\):

Fold step \(t\) A4 paper dimensions \((a_t, b_t)\) Decimal ratio \(a_t / b_t\) Pell discrepancy \(a_t^2 - 2b_t^2\) Hypothetical exact solution \(\Delta = 0\)
\(t = 0\) \((99,\; 70)\) \(1.4142857\) \(9801 - 9800 = +1\) \((a_0, b_0) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) with \(a_0^2 - 2b_0^2 = 0\)
\(t = 1\) \((41,\; 29)\) \(1.4137931\) \(1681 - 1682 = -1\) \((2b_0 - a_0,\; a_0 - b_0) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2,\; \Delta = 0\)
\(t = 2\) \((17,\; 12)\) \(1.4166667\) \(289 - 288 = +1\) \((a_2, b_2) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2,\; 0 < b_2 < b_1,\; \Delta = 0\)
\(t = 3\) \((7,\; 5)\) \(1.4000000\) \(49 - 50 = -1\) \((a_3, b_3) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2,\; 0 < b_3 < b_2,\; \Delta = 0\)
\(t = 4\) \((3,\; 2)\) \(1.5000000\) \(9 - 8 = +1\) \(\vdots\)
\(t = 5\) \((1,\; 1)\) \(1.0000000\) \(1 - 2 = -1\) Never hits \(0\) — infinite descent in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\)!

Because \(99^2 - 2\cdot 70^2 = +1 \neq 0\), the A4 paper sequence hits \((1, 1)\) and stops at \(b_6 = 0\). By contrast, if \(a_0^2 - 2b_0^2 = 0\), then \(\frac{a_t}{b_t} = \sqrt{2} \in (1, 2)\) at every step, so \(0 < b_{t+1} = a_t - b_t = (\sqrt{2} - 1)b_t < b_t\) produces an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers \(b_0 > b_1 > b_2 > \cdots > 0\) — which is impossible!

3 · Name the synthesis

Box M01 · The Four-Pattern Synthesis for Irrationality

Every proof that \(\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}\) combines an existence negation (P11) with a canonical invariant or descent mechanism that exposes an irreconcilable clash between \(a^2\) and \(2b^2\):

\[ \text{Assume } a^2 = 2b^2 \text{ in } \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1} \;\;(\text{P11}) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \begin{cases} \textbf{Route 1 (Lowest Terms): } \gcd(a,b)=1 \text{ (P05)} + \text{Parity (P67)} \implies 2 \mid \gcd(a,b), \\[4pt] \textbf{Route 2 (2-Adic Valuation): } 2\nu_2(a) = 2\nu_2(b) + 1 \text{ (P05 + P30 + P09)}, \\[4pt] \textbf{Route 3 (Subtractive Descent): } (a,b) \mapsto (2b-a, a-b) \text{ (P13 + P71 + P76)}. \end{cases} \]

Box M01.A · Role Assignment of Each Pattern in the Synthesis

  1. P11 (Prove by contradiction): Converts the negative target "\(\forall a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1},\; a^2 \neq 2b^2\)" into a concrete hypothetical pair \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) satisfying \(a^2 = 2b^2\).
  2. P05 (Canonical form): Either divides out \(\gcd(a, b)\) so \(\frac{a}{b}\) is in lowest terms (Route 1), or invokes Unique Prime Factorization \(n = \prod p_i^{e_i}\) (Route 2).
  3. P67 (Contrapositive) & P30 (Localize at a prime): Extracts the \(2\)-divisibility obstruction from \(a^2 = 2b^2\).
  4. P13 (Infinite descent) & P71 (Extremal principle): Replaces prime factorization by a purely linear transformation \((a, b) \mapsto (2b - a, a - b)\) that shrinks positive integers below the minimum possible solution.

Box M01.B · Three-Key Synthesis Diagnosis

  • Claim type: Irrationality of an algebraic number (\(\sqrt{2}, \sqrt[k]{N}, \log_2 3\)) or non-existence of integer solutions to a homogeneous Diophantine equation.
  • Problem shape: An equation \(a^k = N b^k\) (or \(2^a = 3^b\)) where the two sides have incompatible prime valuations or admit a self-similar scaling reduction.
  • Tools at hand: Greatest common divisor normalization (P05), \(p\)-adic valuations \(\nu_p\) (P30), Inclusion–Exclusion area balance (P76), and Well-Ordering / Infinite Descent (P71 + P13).

4 · Worked synthesis exemplars

Exemplar 1 (Route 2: P11 + P05 + P30 + P09) · The \(2\)-adic valuation proof and the complete \(\sqrt[k]{N}\) theorem

Problem. Prove that \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational without reducing fractions to lowest terms, and generalize the argument to prove that for any integers \(N \ge 2\) and \(k \ge 2\), \(\sqrt[k]{N}\) is rational if and only if \(N = m^k\) is the \(k\)-th power of an integer.

Solution.

Step 1 (The 1-line parity clash for \(\sqrt{2}\)). Suppose for contradiction (P11) that \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}\) for some positive integers \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) (not necessarily coprime!), so

\[ a^2 \;=\; 2b^2. \]

By Unique Prime Factorization (P05), localize at the prime \(p = 2\) (P30) by counting the exact exponent \(\nu_2(n)\) of the prime \(2\) dividing each side:

  • Whenever an integer \(a\) is squared, every prime exponent in its factorization doubles: \(\nu_2(a^2) = 2\,\nu_2(a)\), which is always even.
  • Multiplying \(b^2\) by \(2\) adds \(1\) to the exponent of \(2\): \(\nu_2(2b^2) = 1 + 2\,\nu_2(b)\), which is always odd.

Equating the exponents of \(2\) on both sides of \(a^2 = 2b^2\) forces \(2\,\nu_2(a) = 2\,\nu_2(b) + 1\) — an even integer equal to an odd integer (P09)! Contradiction.

Step 2 (Generalization to \(\sqrt[k]{N}\)). If \(\sqrt[k]{N} = \frac{a}{b}\), then \(a^k = N b^k\). For every prime \(p\), applying \(\nu_p\) gives

\[ k\,\nu_p(a) \;=\; \nu_p(N) + k\,\nu_p(b) \quad\implies\quad \nu_p(N) \;=\; k\bigl(\nu_p(a) - \nu_p(b)\bigr). \]

Since \(N\) is an integer, \(\nu_p(N) \ge 0\) is divisible by \(k\) for every prime \(p\), so \(e_p := \nu_p(N) / k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}\) is a nonnegative integer for every prime \(p\). Setting \(m = \prod_p p^{e_p} \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) gives \(N = m^k\)!

Exemplar 2 (Route 3: P11 + P71 + P76 + P13) · Tennenbaum's overlapping carpets and Estermann's multiplier descent

Problem.

  1. Give Stanley Tennenbaum's geometric proof that \(a^2 = 2b^2\) has no positive integer solutions by overlapping two \(b \times b\) squares inside a minimal \(a \times a\) square.
  2. Generalize this descent to prove that for any positive integer \(d\) that is not a perfect square, \(\sqrt{d}\) is irrational.

Solution.

Part 1 (Tennenbaum's overlapping squares). Suppose \(a^2 = 2b^2\) has solutions in positive integers (P11). By the Well-Ordering Principle (P71), choose the solution \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) with the smallest possible side \(a > 0\). Place two \(b \times b\) squares in the top-left and bottom-right corners of the \(a \times a\) square (Fig M01.2, left). Because \(b < a < 2b\), the two \(b \times b\) squares overlap in a central square of side \(2b - a > 0\) and leave two uncovered corner squares of side \(a - b > 0\). By Inclusion–Exclusion (P76), the total area of the \(a \times a\) square is

\[ a^2 \;=\; \underbrace{2b^2}_{\text{two } b\times b \text{ carpets}} \,-\, \underbrace{(2b - a)^2}_{\text{double-covered overlap}} \,+\, \underbrace{2(a - b)^2}_{\text{two uncovered corners}}. \]

Subtracting \(a^2 = 2b^2\) from both sides yields \((2b - a)^2 = 2(a - b)^2\)! Since \(b < a < 2b\), the integers \(a' = 2b - a\) and \(b' = a - b\) satisfy \(0 < a' < a\) and \((a')^2 = 2(b')^2\) — a strictly smaller positive integer solution (P13), contradicting the minimality of \(a\)!

Part 2 (Estermann–Beukers fractional-part descent for \(\sqrt{d}\)). Let \(d \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 2}\) not be a perfect square, and let \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{d} \rfloor\) be the unique integer such that \(m < \sqrt{d} < m + 1\), so \(0 < \sqrt{d} - m < 1\). If \(\sqrt{d} \in \mathbb{Q}\), let \(b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) be the smallest positive integer such that \(a := b\sqrt{d}\) is an integer (P71). Define

\[ b' \;:=\; b\bigl(\sqrt{d} - m\bigr) \;=\; a - mb \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}. \]

Because \(0 < \sqrt{d} - m < 1\), we have \(0 < b' < b\); and multiplying \(b'\) by \(\sqrt{d}\) gives

\[ b'\sqrt{d} \;=\; b d - m(b\sqrt{d}) \;=\; d b - m a \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}, \]

contradicting that \(b\) was the smallest positive integer multiplier sending \(\sqrt{d}\) into \(\mathbb{Z}\)!

Exemplar 3 (Route 4: P05 + P30 + P67) · The Rational Root Theorem for monic polynomials

Problem. Prove that if a monic polynomial with integer coefficients

\[ P(x) \;=\; x^n + c_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_1 x + c_0 \;\in\; \mathbb{Z}[x] \]

has a rational root \(x = \frac{a}{b} \in \mathbb{Q}\), then \(x \in \mathbb{Z}\) must be an integer! Use this to prove in two lines that \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is irrational.

Solution.

Step 1 (Clear denominators in lowest terms). Write \(x = \frac{a}{b}\) in canonical lowest terms with \(b \ge 1\) and \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\) (P05). Substituting \(x = \frac{a}{b}\) into \(P(x) = 0\), multiplying by \(b^n\), and isolating the leading term \(a^n\) gives

\[ a^n \;=\; -b\bigl(c_{n-1} a^{n-1} + c_{n-2} a^{n-2} b + \cdots + c_0 b^{n-1}\bigr). \]

Suppose for contradiction that \(b > 1\), and let \(p\) be any prime factor of \(b\) (P30). Because \(p \mid b\), the right-hand side is a multiple of \(p\), so \(p \mid a^n\). By the contrapositive of Euclid's lemma (P67: if \(p \nmid a\), then \(p \nmid a^n\)), we have \(p \mid a\). Thus \(p\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\), contradicting \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\)! Hence \(b = 1\) and \(x = a \in \mathbb{Z}\).

Step 2 (Application to \(\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\)). Squaring \(\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) gives \(\alpha^2 = 5 + 2\sqrt{6} \implies (\alpha^2 - 5)^2 = 24 \implies \alpha^4 - 10\alpha^2 + 1 = 0\). By Step 1, any rational root of \(x^4 - 10x^2 + 1 = 0\) must be an integer dividing the constant term \(1\), namely \(x = \pm 1\) (where \((\pm 1)^4 - 10(\pm 1)^2 + 1 = -8 \neq 0\), or simply note \(3 = 1 + 2 < \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} < 2 + 2 = 4\), so \(\alpha\) lies strictly between the consecutive integers \(3\) and \(4\)). Therefore \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}\) is irrational!

5 · Where each proof breaks when stress-tested (P03)

Exhibit A — Stress-testing all three routes on \(\sqrt{4} = 2\): where does each proof stop? Applying P03 · Stress-test with special cases, replace \(2\) by the perfect square \(4\) (\(a^2 = 4b^2\)) and watch the exact line where each of our three proofs halts:

  • Route 1 (Euclid's Parity): \(a^2 = 4b^2 \implies a = 2k \implies 4k^2 = 4b^2 \implies k^2 = b^2\). The factor of \(4\) cancels completely, leaving \(1 \cdot b^2\) with no factor of \(2\) to force \(b\) to be even!
  • Route 2 (\(2\)-Adic Valuation): \(2\nu_2(a) = \nu_2(4) + 2\nu_2(b) = 2 + 2\nu_2(b)\), which is even = even (satisfied by \(\nu_2(a) = \nu_2(b) + 1\)).
  • Route 3 (Tennenbaum / Estermann Descent): Since \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{4} \rfloor = 2\), the new denominator is \(b' = a - 2b = 2b - 2b = 0\), which drops out of \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) because \(\sqrt{4} - 2 = 0\) is not strictly positive!

Exhibit B — Extending Route 1 to composite \(N\) without prime localization: why "\(12 \mid a^2 \implies 12 \mid a\)" is false! Suppose a student tries to prove \(\sqrt{12} \notin \mathbb{Q}\) by writing: "\(a^2 = 12b^2 \implies 12 \mid a^2 \implies 12 \mid a \implies a = 12k\)." That implication is false: for \(a = 6\), \(a^2 = 36\) is divisible by \(12\), yet \(12 \nmid 6\)! The step \(N \mid a^2 \implies N \mid a\) holds if and only if \(N\) is squarefree. By contrast, Route 2 (localizing at a single prime \(p = 3\) where \(\nu_3(12) = 1\) is odd, P30) or Route 3 (Estermann's descent with \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{12} \rfloor = 3\)) works effortlessly for every non-square \(N\).

Exhibit C — Why polynomial descent needs a new ingredient for transcendental numbers (\(e\) and \(\pi\)). All four routes in this chapter exploit an algebraic equation \(P(\alpha) = 0\) with integer coefficients. When \(\alpha = e = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!}\), there is no polynomial \(P(x)\) to start from; instead, Fourier (1815) combined P11 + P73 (multiplying the series for \(e = a/b\) by \(b!\)) + P15 (squeezing the tail \(R_b = b! \sum_{n=b+1}^\infty \frac{1}{n!} \in (0, \frac{1}{b})\)) to trap an integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\)!

Honest scope of this synthesis. Comparing multiple proofs of the same theorem is one of the highest-leverage habits in mathematics: Euclid's lowest-terms parity proof generalizes to prime ideals in Dedekind domains (P32); the \(2\)-adic valuation proof generalizes to Ostrowski's valuation theory and Newton polygons (P40); and the geometric folding / continued-fraction descent generalizes to Pell's equation \(a^2 - db^2 = \pm 1\) and Diophantine approximation.

7 · Lineage interlude

From Hippasus's pentagon and Aristotle's parity proof to Dedekind, Estermann, and Tennenbaum (c. 450 BCE – 1955).

  • Hippasus of Metapontum (c. 450 BCE) & Theodorus of Cyrene (c. 400 BCE): The early Pythagoreans built their cosmology on the belief that all lengths are commensurable (ratios of whole numbers). Discovering that the diagonal and side of a regular pentagon or square have an unending mutual subtraction process (anthyphairesis — our geometric descent in Section 2 and Exercise M01.3!) shattered that dogma and launched Greek deductive mathematics. In Plato's Theaetetus, Theodorus proved the irrationality of \(\sqrt{3}, \sqrt{5}, \dots, \sqrt{17}\).
  • Aristotle (c. 350 BCE) & Euclid (c. 300 BCE): In the Prior Analytics (I.23), Aristotle cited the parity proof ("if the diagonal is commensurable, odd numbers would equal even numbers") as the canonical example of reductio ad impossibile (P11), preserved in Book X of Euclid's Elements.
  • Carl Friedrich Gauss (1801) & Richard Dedekind (1858): In the Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (Article 42), Gauss proved that any monic polynomial with integer coefficients cannot have a non-integer rational root (Exemplar 3). Dedekind was inspired by the gap at \(\sqrt{2}\) in \(\mathbb{Q}\) to construct the real numbers \(\mathbb{R}\) via Dedekind cuts.
  • Theodor Estermann (1975) & Stanley Tennenbaum (1950s): Estermann popularized the three-line multiplier descent \(b' = b(\sqrt{d} - \lfloor\sqrt{d}\rfloor)\), while logician Stanley Tennenbaum discovered the overlapping-carpets visual proof (Fig M01.2, left), later shared with the world by John Horton Conway in The Power of Mathematics.

8 · Exercises

Exercise M01.1 (●○○ — Irrationality of \(\log_2 3\) and \(\log_{10} 2\) via P11 + P05 + P09).

  1. Prove that \(\log_2 3\) is irrational by assuming \(\log_2 3 = \frac{a}{b}\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) and clearing both the logarithm and the fraction.
  2. Prove that \(\log_{10} 2\) is irrational by comparing the \(5\)-adic valuation \(\nu_5\) on both sides of \(2^b = 10^a\).
Hint

Since \(\log_2 3 > 0\), if \(\log_2 3 = a/b\) we can take \(a, b \ge 1\). Exponentiating base \(2\) gives \(2^{a/b} = 3 \iff 2^a = 3^b\).

Worked answer

1. Irrationality of \(\log_2 3\). Since \(3 > 1\), \(\log_2 3 > 0\). Suppose for contradiction (P11) that \(\log_2 3 = \frac{a}{b}\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\). Exponentiating base \(2\) and raising to the \(b\)-th power gives

\[ 2^a \;=\; 3^b. \]

Because \(a \ge 1\), the left-hand side \(2^a\) is an even integer, while the right-hand side \(3^b\) is a product of odd numbers and is therefore odd (P09) — contradiction!

2. Irrationality of \(\log_{10} 2\). If \(\log_{10} 2 = \frac{a}{b}\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\), then \(10^a = 2^b \implies 2^a \cdot 5^a = 2^b\). Localizing at the prime \(p = 5\) (P30), the left-hand side has \(\nu_5(10^a) = a \ge 1\), while the right-hand side has \(\nu_5(2^b) = 0\) — contradicting Unique Prime Factorization (P05)!

Exercise M01.2 (●●○ — Explicit linear descent for \(\sqrt{3}\) and \(\sqrt{5}\)).

  1. For \(d = 3\), we have \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{3} \rfloor = 1\). If \(a^2 = 3b^2\) in positive integers, Estermann's multiplier descent sets \(b' = a - b\) and \(a' = 3b - a\). Verify algebraically that \((a')^2 - 3(b')^2 = 2(3b^2 - a^2) = 0\) and that \(0 < b' < b\).
  2. For \(d = 5\), we have \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{5} \rfloor = 2\). Write down the corresponding linear map \((a, b) \mapsto (a', b')\) and verify that \((a')^2 - 5(b')^2 = -(a^2 - 5b^2)\) and \(0 < b' < b\).
Hint

For \(d = 5\), set \(b' = b(\sqrt{5} - 2) = a - 2b\) and \(a' = b'\sqrt{5} = 5b - 2a\), then expand \((5b - 2a)^2 - 5(a - 2b)^2\).

Worked answer

1. Descent for \(\sqrt{3}\). Expanding \((3b - a)^2 - 3(a - b)^2\) gives

\[ (9b^2 - 6ab + a^2) - 3(a^2 - 2ab + b^2) \;=\; 6b^2 - 2a^2 \;=\; -2\bigl(a^2 - 3b^2\bigr) \;=\; 0. \]

Since \(1 < \sqrt{3} < 2\), we have \(b < a < 2b\), so \(0 < b' = a - b < b\) and \(a' = 3b - a > b > 0\), giving a strictly smaller positive integer solution to \((a')^2 = 3(b')^2\).

2. Descent for \(\sqrt{5}\). Since \(m = \lfloor \sqrt{5} \rfloor = 2\), set \(b' = a - 2b\) and \(a' = 5b - 2a\). Expanding \((a')^2 - 5(b')^2\) gives

\[ (5b - 2a)^2 - 5(a - 2b)^2 \;=\; (25b^2 - 20ab + 4a^2) - (5a^2 - 20ab + 20b^2) \;=\; -\bigl(a^2 - 5b^2\bigr) \;=\; 0. \]

Since \(2 < \sqrt{5} < 3\), we have \(2b < a < 3b\), so \(0 < b' = a - 2b < b\), completing the infinite descent.

Exercise M01.3 (●●● — Hippasus's regular pentagon and the golden-ratio descent). In a regular pentagon with side length \(b\) and diagonal length \(a\), the ratio \(\varphi = \frac{a}{b}\) satisfies \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{b}{a - b} \iff a^2 - ab - b^2 = 0\), and the five diagonals enclose a smaller regular pentagon in the center with side \(b' = a - b\) and diagonal \(a' = b - b' = 2b - a\).

  1. Verify the exact algebraic identity \((2b - a)^2 - (2b - a)(a - b) - (a - b)^2 = -\bigl(a^2 - ab - b^2\bigr)\).
  2. Apply the map \((a, b) \mapsto (2b - a,\; a - b)\) repeatedly to the Fibonacci near-miss \((a_0, b_0) = (13, 8)\), and explain why no pair of positive integers \((a, b)\) can ever satisfy \(a^2 - ab - b^2 = 0\)!
Hint

Expand \((4b^2 - 4ab + a^2) - (3ab - a^2 - 2b^2) - (a^2 - 2ab + b^2)\). On \((13, 8)\), note that \(2(8) - 13 = 3\)? Wait: for \(a' = b = 8\) and \(b' = a - b = 5\)? Check whether the inner pentagon has diagonal \(b' = a - b\) and side \(2b - a\)!

Worked answer

1. Pentagon vs. Fibonacci descent identity. Note the convention: in a regular pentagon, the shorter segment cut by diagonals is \(s' = 2b - a\) and the longer segment (the new diagonal) is \(d' = a - b\), or simply taking the standard Euclidean step \(a_{\text{new}} = b\) and \(b_{\text{new}} = a - b\)! Under \((a, b) \mapsto (b,\; a - b)\):

\[ b^2 - b(a - b) - (a - b)^2 \;=\; b^2 - ab + b^2 - (a^2 - 2ab + b^2) \;=\; -\bigl(a^2 - ab - b^2\bigr)! \]

(And taking two steps at once gives \((a, b) \mapsto (a - b,\; 2b - a)\).) Thus if \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^2\) satisfies \(a^2 - ab - b^2 = 0\), then \((b,\; a - b)\) also satisfies the exact same equation with \(0 < a - b < b\) (since \(1 < \varphi < 2\)).

2. Fibonacci trace from \((13, 8)\) and irrationality of \(\varphi\). Under \((a, b) \mapsto (b,\; a - b)\), the Fibonacci pair \((13, 8)\) (where \(13^2 - 13(8) - 8^2 = 169 - 104 - 64 = +1\)) steps down the Fibonacci sequence:

\[ (13, 8) \mapsto (8, 5) \mapsto (5, 3) \mapsto (3, 2) \mapsto (2, 1) \mapsto (1, 1) \mapsto (1, 0), \]

alternating \(\Delta = +1, -1, +1, -1, +1, -1, +1\) until hitting \(0\). A hypothetical exact solution with \(\Delta = 0\) can never reach \((1, 0)\) (where \(\Delta = +1 \neq 0\)), so it would descend in \(\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) forever — proving \(\varphi = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}\).

Exercise M01.4 (◆ — Pattern-unknown synthesis). Prove that the number

\[ \alpha \;=\; \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5} \]

is irrational.

Worked answer

1. Pattern synthesis (P11 + P78 isolating radicals + P30 valuation). Suppose for contradiction (P11) that \(\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5} \in \mathbb{Q}\) is rational. To eliminate two radicals at once when squaring, isolate \(\sqrt{5}\) on the right-hand side:

\[ \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \;=\; \alpha - \sqrt{5}. \]

Squaring both sides yields

\[ 2 + 2\sqrt{6} + 3 \;=\; \alpha^2 - 2\alpha\sqrt{5} + 5 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\sqrt{6} + 2\alpha\sqrt{5} \;=\; \alpha^2. \]

Squaring a second time eliminates both \(\sqrt{6}\) and \(\sqrt{5}\) except in their single cross-product \(\sqrt{6}\sqrt{5} = \sqrt{30}\):

\[ 24 + 8\alpha\sqrt{30} + 20\alpha^2 \;=\; \alpha^4 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 8\alpha\sqrt{30} \;=\; \alpha^4 - 20\alpha^2 - 24. \]

2. Valuation clash on \(\sqrt{30}\). Since \(\alpha = \sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5} > 0\) is strictly positive, \(8\alpha \neq 0\) (P78). Dividing by the non-zero rational number \(8\alpha\) would express

\[ \sqrt{30} \;=\; \frac{\alpha^4 - 20\alpha^2 - 24}{8\alpha} \;\in\; \mathbb{Q} \]

as a rational number! Yet \(\nu_2(30) = \nu_2(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5) = 1\) is odd, so by Exemplar 1 (\(2\)-adic valuation, P30), \(\sqrt{30}\) is irrational — contradiction! Therefore \(\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} + \sqrt{5} \notin \mathbb{Q}\).

Exercise M01.5 (◐ — Exploration: Fourier's 1815 synthesis proof that \(e\) is irrational). How do we prove that Euler's base \(e = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!} = 1 + \frac{1}{1!} + \frac{1}{2!} + \frac{1}{3!} + \cdots\) is irrational when \(e\) is not the root of a simple quadratic? Joseph Fourier (1815) combined four patterns from our Atlas: P11 (Contradiction: assume \(e = \frac{a}{b}\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\)), P73 (Factorial clearing: multiply by \(b!\)), P69 (Geometric series domination), and P15 (Squeeze an integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\)).

Carry out Fourier's proof by defining \(N := b!\Bigl(e - \sum_{n=0}^b \frac{1}{n!}\Bigr)\):

  1. Explain why \(N\) must be an integer if \(e = \frac{a}{b}\).
  2. Bound the infinite tail \(N = \frac{1}{b+1} + \frac{1}{(b+1)(b+2)} + \frac{1}{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)} + \cdots\) above by a geometric series with ratio \(\frac{1}{b+1}\) to prove \(0 < N < \frac{1}{b} \le 1\), and conclude that \(e \notin \mathbb{Q}\)!
Hint

For \(0 \le n \le b\), \(\frac{b!}{n!} = (n+1)(n+2)\cdots b\) is an integer, and \(b! \cdot \frac{a}{b} = (b-1)! a\) is an integer. In the tail, replace each factor \((b+2), (b+3), \dots\) with the smaller number \((b+1)\)!

Worked answer

1. Integrality of \(N\) under \(e = \frac{a}{b}\). Suppose \(e = \frac{a}{b}\) with \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}\) (we may take \(b \ge 2\) since \(2 < e < 3\) is not an integer). Then \(b!\,e = (b-1)!\,a \in \mathbb{Z}\), and for every \(0 \le n \le b\), \(\frac{b!}{n!} \in \mathbb{Z}\). Therefore their difference

\[ N \;:=\; b!\,e \,-\, \sum_{n=0}^b \frac{b!}{n!} \;\in\; \mathbb{Z} \]

must be an exact integer!

2. Squeezing \(0 < N < 1\) (P15). Writing out \(N\) from the Taylor series \(e = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!}\) gives

\[ 0 \;<\; N \;=\; \frac{1}{b+1} + \frac{1}{(b+1)(b+2)} + \frac{1}{(b+1)(b+2)(b+3)} + \cdots \;<\; \frac{1}{b+1} + \frac{1}{(b+1)^2} + \frac{1}{(b+1)^3} + \cdots \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{b+1}}{1 - \frac{1}{b+1}} \;=\; \frac{1}{b} \;\le\; \frac{1}{2} \;<\; 1. \]

Thus \(N\) is an integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) — impossible! Hence \(e\) is irrational.
