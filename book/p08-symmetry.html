The Patterns Atlas · P08 · the eighth move of the spine

Divide by symmetry

When the problem treats twins alike, do the work once and let the clones carry their share. One honest case, chosen to stand for its whole family, answers for all of them.

New word: symmetry. In daily speech, a symmetry is a mirror line or a pretty flower. Mathematically, a symmetry of a problem is a move that leaves the problem unchanged: rotate the table, swap the two variables, flip the picture, relabel the boxes. The move may change everything you see, and still change nothing the question asks about. New word: orbit. Take one object and apply every symmetry to it; the family of clones you reach is its orbit. New word: representative. The single member of the family you choose to work on, standing in for its clones — the way a sample seat stands for every seat in a rotation-tied family.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, and P07 built the objects nobody handed us. P07's closing pointer promised this page: when your build creates a symmetry, something new becomes possible — and this page is that something, turned around. P07's move was about objects the problem forgot to give you. This one is about structure the problem already has: the moves that leave it unchanged.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Some problems are massively redundant. Ask for a count, and every answer arrives with its clones attached; ask for a solution, and its mirror twin solves along with it. Brute force does not notice — it pays eight times for what it should pay for once, then reports the inflated bill as the answer. The good news: when a move genuinely leaves the problem unchanged, the clones are guaranteed identical, so one of them can stand for all. The bad news, stated honestly up front: the division has to be booked. How many clones a family has is a number you must know and prove — not assume — and the wrong ledger is the most seductive wrong answer this move produces (block 5 collects the wreckage). What this page teaches is the discipline: find the move, prove it is a symmetry of the question and not just of the picture, count the clones honestly, and then — literally — divide.

2 · Meet the problem

Diwali dinner. Eight relatives, one round table, eight identical chairs. How many different seatings are there?

Start where brute force starts. If the chairs were in a row — chair 1, chair 2, …, chair 8, all different — the count is a permutation count: \(8! = 40{,}320\) ways. So is that the answer? Try it small first (P02's instinct), because something about "round" is itching. Seat three people, \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), around a small table, and list the row-orders:

\[ABC, \quad BCA, \quad CAB, \qquad ACB, \quad CBA, \quad BAC.\]

Six row-orders — but only two seatings. Walk around the table and check: \(ABC\) and \(BCA\) and \(CAB\) are the same seating, everyone with the same left and right neighbours, merely rotated. The other three are the second seating. Each seating was counted three times, once per rotation, and the count repairs itself by division:

\[\frac{3!}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2 \quad \text{seatings.}\]

Four people, same check: \(4! = 24\) row-orders, and each seating is described by exactly four of them (its four rotations), so \(24/4 = 6\) seatings. The pattern is not a coincidence — it is a proof shape. Around a table, the only thing that defines a seating is who sits next to whom; a rotation moves every person but changes no neighbour, so rotated orders are descriptions of one seating. And with all eight relatives distinct, no rotation other than the full turn can return everyone to their own chair — each seating therefore has exactly eight row-descriptions, its eight rotations, and no more. So the dinner count is

\[\frac{8!}{8} = \frac{40{,}320}{8} = 5{,}040 \quad \text{seatings}.\]

Notice what actually happened, because it is the whole move in miniature. The question — who neighbours whom — was unchanged by a rotation; the row-count was not. A count of all descriptions (\(40{,}320\)), divided by the number of descriptions per seating (\(8\), proved, not guessed), produced the count of things actually asked about. Nothing was built; nothing was transformed. The problem arrived with its symmetry installed — the table's roundness did that — and the move just charged the clones for their share. The move has a name, and it is the eighth pattern of the spine.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P08 — Divide by symmetry. When the problem's objects come in symmetry-tied families and the question treats every member of a family identically: (1) name the moves that leave the question unchanged — those are the symmetries; (2) pick one representative per family and do the work on it, once; (3) cash out by the ledger — multiply the representative's answer by the family size, or divide the brute-force total by it. The family of clones one object generates under the symmetries is its orbit.

Or, in words: twins do twin work. When a rotation, a swap, or a flip hands you identical copies of the same case, do the case once and bill the copies — but keep honest books. The ledger — exactly how many copies a family holds — is the mathematical heart of the move, and it is a fact to prove, not a habit to assume. A division by a number you did not prove is a guess wearing a fraction bar.

Box P08.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The move must leave the question unchanged — all of it. A rotation leaves "who neighbours whom" alone, so it is a symmetry of the seating question; but it moves "who sits nearest the door", so it is not a symmetry of that question. Test the move against every clause, not just the picture. What breaks: the phantom symmetry — block 5's second exhibit shows a swap that looks legal and quietly changes the constraint.
  2. Every orbit's size must be known — and proved. Eight distinct people: every orbit has exactly eight members, proved by the no-rotation-fixes-it argument. But introduce repeated beads, or a solution like \((4,4)\) that is its own twin, and orbits shrink: sizes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\) occur, and the ledger must record them all. What breaks: the uneven-orbit division — a total divided by a clone-count that only some families have; block 5's first and third exhibits are both this failure, in different clothes.
  3. The representative must carry only what its clones carry. Work on a representative, and every property you use must be shared by the whole orbit — otherwise you proved something about your choice, not about the family. "Assume \(a \le b\)" is fine when the question ignores the order; it is not fine when the question asks about \(a - b\). What breaks: the over-privileged representative — an accident of your choice enters the proof as if it were a property of the family.

Three-key diagnosis. The claim type: a count, a computation, or a proof where brute force works but visibly pays for the same thing several times. The problem shape: the statement is unchanged under a move you can point at — rotating the table, swapping the names, flipping the figure — while the brute-force answer is not. The tools at hand: P02's small cases, which expose orbit sizes before you commit to any ledger (the \(n = 3\) table caught the division instantly), plus P01's pinning, which tells you exactly what the question does and does not ignore.

When does P08 apply? Not every problem is symmetric, and dividing where no symmetry exists is worse than counting twice. The honest trigger: you can name a concrete move, you have checked the question against it clause by clause, and the small cases show the clones arriving in steady families. When all three hold, stop paying retail. One more habit belongs to the trigger: before trusting any division, run it on a case small enough to count by hand — a division that yields a non-integer or contradicts a hand count is the move itself telling you the ledger is wrong (block 5 makes this a worked exhibit).

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. Each names every pattern used — and each ends with the ledger closed.

Example 1 (counting) — two dice, one honest ledger

Two fair dice are thrown. What is the probability the sum is \(8\)? The sample space is the \(36\) ordered pairs \((a, b)\), each with probability \(1/36\) — pinned that way (P01), because the dice are two physical objects, so \((2,6)\) and \((6,2)\) are different outcomes of the experiment even though they feed the same sum.

A scan is cheap here, but look at the symmetry first: the question asks about \(a+b\), which does not change under the swap \((a,b) \leftrightarrow (b,a)\). The ordered pairs fall into swap-tied families: \(\{(2,6),(6,2)\}\), \(\{(3,5),(5,3)\}\), \(\{(4,4)\}\). Three representatives, three families — and notice the third one is its own twin: no swap produces anything new. The ledger, honestly kept:

\[\underbrace{2 + 2}_{\text{two families of size } 2} + \underbrace{1}_{\text{one family of size } 1} = 5 \quad \text{favourable ordered pairs,}\]

so the probability is \(5/36\). The division instinct — "count unordered, then double" — is correct only after the fixed family is booked separately: \(2 \times 2 + 1\), not \(3 \times 2\). Run the check both ways: the direct scan over all \(36\) ordered pairs finds exactly \(5\) with \(a+b=8\); the family ledger predicts the same \(5\); and the twin count for sum \(7\) has no fixed family at all (\(7\) is odd, so \(a = b\) is impossible), giving \(3\) families of \(2\): probability \(6/36 = 1/6\). Two questions, two ledgers, both closed by the same census discipline. Moves used: P08 (the swap families and their honest census), P01 (the sample space pinned as ordered pairs — the ledger's unit), P02 (the 36-point scan that keeps the ledger honest).

Example 2 (algebra) — the system that solves itself twice

Solve the system

\[x + y = 7, \qquad xy = 12,\]

and then compute \(x^2 + y^2\) and \(x^3 + y^3\) — all without ever knowing which of \(x, y\) is which.

The system is unchanged under the swap \(x \leftrightarrow y\): both equations read the same after it. That is not decoration; it is the whole solution. Because the equations are swap-symmetric, whatever they say about \((x, y)\) they say equally about \((y, x)\) — solutions arrive in twin pairs. Now do the work once, on the representative. Both unknowns satisfy the same quadratic — by Vieta's formulas the two unknowns are exactly the roots of \(t^2 - 7t + 12 = 0\) — so the canonical move (P05) reduces the system to one equation: \(t^2 - 7t + 12 = (t-3)(t-4) = 0\). So \(\{x, y\} = \{3, 4\}\) — and the swap certifies the second solution for free: since \((3,4)\) satisfies both equations, so does \((4,3)\); there is nothing left to check.

Now the computations, which never pick a representative at all. The expressions \(x^2+y^2\) and \(x^3+y^3\) are swap-invariant, so they have values computable from the symmetric data \(s = x+y = 7\) and \(p = xy = 12\) alone:

\[x^2 + y^2 = (x+y)^2 - 2xy = 7^2 - 2 \cdot 12 = 49 - 24 = 25,\]

\[x^3 + y^3 = (x+y)^3 - 3xy(x+y) = 7^3 - 3 \cdot 12 \cdot 7 = 343 - 252 = 91.\]

Check both by direct substitution, twice each, on both twins: \(3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25\) and \(4^2 + 3^2 = 25\); \(3^3 + 4^3 = 27 + 64 = 91\) and \(4^3 + 3^3 = 91\). The invariants could not tell the twins apart — that is exactly what invariance means, and exactly why the computations succeeded without a representative. Moves used: P08 (the swap symmetry: twin solutions, invariant expressions), P05 (one canonical quadratic for both unknowns), P04 (the factoring that solved it), P02 (the twin substitutions, run as checks).

Example 3 (geometry) — the rectangle that beats its rotations

Among all rectangles with perimeter \(40\), which one has the greatest area? Write the sides as \(a\) and \(20 - a\) (their sum is \(20\)), so the area is \(A(a) = a(20-a)\), and the question is: which \(a\) wins?

First the symmetry, because it will do half the work. The rectangle with sides \((a, 20-a)\) and the one with sides \((20-a, a)\) are the same rectangle, turned through a quarter-turn — the side-swap is a rotation of the picture, so it is a symmetry of the question. Now the representative discipline, in reverse: if the winning rectangle is unique, it must be its own twin — a rectangle literally identical to its quarter-turned self. Self-twin means the two sides agree: \(a = 20-a\), so \(a = 10\): if a unique winner exists, it is the \(10 \times 10\) square, area \(100\).

Read that "if" again, because it is the honest half of the move. Symmetry has located the candidate; it has not proved the candidate wins. A claim like "greatest area" is a comparison against every competitor, and the comparison needs its proof — here, one line of algebra:

\[100 - a(20-a) = 100 - 20a + a^2 = (a-10)^2 \ \ge 0 \quad \text{for every } a,\]

so \(a(20-a) \le 100\) for every \(a\), with equality exactly at \(a = 10\). The square wins, and the identity — not the symmetry — is the certificate. Run the grid as a detector: for every integer \(a\) from \(1\) to \(19\), the area \(a(20-a)\) tops out at \(100\) at \(a = 10\), and the twin pairs bookend symmetrically: \(a = 5\) and \(a = 15\) both give \(75\), \(a = 1\) and \(a = 19\) both give \(19\). One honest note for later: the grid and the identity both work because a largest area genuinely exists to be found — for sides ranging over all real numbers in \([0,20]\), the continuous area function must attain its maximum; P21 makes that principle official when the ladder reaches it. Moves used: P08 (the side-swap symmetry locating the self-twin candidate), P05 (the canonical form \(a(20-a)\)), P03 (the hostile question — why not a long thin rectangle? — answered by the grid and the identity), P02 (the 19-point detector grid).

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

Every failure below is a ledger failure. The symmetry itself is usually real; what breaks is the accounting around it.

  • The uneven-orbit division (the divide-by-\(n\) trap). Thread six beads on a necklace, three red and three blue: \(6!/(3!\,3!) = 20\) linear arrangements. Divide by 6 rotations? \(20/6 \approx 3.33\) — not even an integer, and the move itself is confessing: the orbits are not all size 6. The arrangement \(RBRBRB\) returns to itself after just two rotations, so its family holds only 2 members, not 6. The honest census: one family of size 2, three families of size 6 — so \(2 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 3 = 20\), and the necklace count is \(4\), not \(3.33\) and not \(3\). Mitigation: census the orbit sizes on small cases before dividing (P02's habit — the \(n = 3\) table would have caught this instantly). Price: the census is a scan, and for a one-off small problem it can cost more than the division saves. Default: all objects distinct and the symmetry move has no fixed cases — divide freely; repeats or self-twins present — census first, divide never.
  • The phantom symmetry (the move is not a symmetry of the question). Solve \(x + y = 10\) in positive integers with \(x < y\), and the tempting half-division — "solutions come in swap pairs, so count half" — quietly destroys the problem: the constraint \(x < y\) changes under the swap, so \((3,7)\) is a solution and \((7,3)\) is not, and the twins you divided by do not exist. Half of nothing is still nothing; the halved count reports solutions the problem forbids. Mitigation: before trusting any symmetry, apply the move to every clause of the question and watch what breaks. Price: the clause-by-clause check is strict bookkeeping, and it occasionally kills a symmetry you were fond of. Default: the move must fix the full statement — data, constraints, and goal — not just the picture you drew of it.
  • The question breaks the symmetry (the marked chair). Same eight relatives, same round table — but now one chair is the head seat, nearest the door, and the question asks who sits where. The door is a mark on the frame: rotations move people relative to the door, so the rotation twinning is gone, and every seating is its own family. The count is the full \(8! = 40{,}320\) — the division you were owed a minute ago has evaporated. Marks like the door, an origin, a labelled axis, or the word "first" pin the frame and kill frame symmetries. Mitigation: find the largest symmetry the question actually keeps, and divide only by that. Price: sometimes the honest answer is that nothing survives — full price. Default: hunt the pinning mark first; the division you may keep is the one that moves the mark onto itself.
  • The hidden symmetry (waste, not wrongness). Missing a symmetry does not produce wrong answers — it produces honest ones at retail price: computing \(x^2 + y^2\) separately for \((3,4)\) and \((4,3)\), or scanning all \(36\) dice pairs by hand when \(3\) representatives and a census close the ledger. Mitigation: run the three-key diagnosis (block 3) before brute force, and let a small scan show the twins arriving in families. Price: none — this is the rare failure whose repair is a discount. Default: one small scan, every time, before committing to the long count.
  • Symmetry locates, verification proves. Example 3's square was located by symmetry but proved by the identity \((a-10)^2 \ge 0\). Stop at the location and you have a candidate, not a theorem: a problem can be symmetric and still have no winner at all, or several tied winners sitting off the mirror line. Mitigation: treat every symmetry conclusion as a fork that still needs its certificate — usually one line of algebra. Price: the certificate line. Default: symmetry prunes, the proof confirms; ship both.
Scope, honestly. This page runs the school cases: distinct-object rotations, swap twins, fixed points, self-twins. When orbits get truly uneven — many sizes at once, colors in complicated patterns — the honest census has a machinery (averaging over the symmetry moves themselves, the result often mis-attributed to Burnside), and that machinery is P36's, far up the ladder. The proof-side phrase for "one representative, argued honestly for its whole orbit" — without loss of generality — is important enough to be its own pattern, and it is P68's. The general language for all of it — groups, actions, orbits — exists, is beautiful, and is beyond this page; nothing here secretly used it.

6 · What to try next

  • The orbits came out uneven, and you still need the count. The census habit works, but the machinery for averaging over symmetries directly is a pattern of its own: P36 · Count with characters.
  • You want what the move never changes, not what it ties. A symmetry is a move that preserves something; the something it preserves is the twin concept — P09 · Find the invariant is the next page of the spine, and the two moves are most powerful together.
  • Your families are too big and two objects must collide inside one. Big families force repeats; repeats are pigeonholes — P10 · Force a collision.
  • You need one representative to stand for its whole orbit inside a proof. The proof-side uniform of this move — without loss of generality, used honestly — is P68 · Without loss of generality.
  • The symmetry ties two problems, not two cases. When the same symmetry relates two different questions so that each solves the other, that is P16 · Same problem, different clothes — Example 3's twin problems (max area at fixed perimeter, min perimeter at fixed area) are a first taste.
  • You want both spine moves at once on one problem. Six people at a party, and a question about mutual strangers and mutual acquaintances — M04 (\(R(3,3) = 6\)) — is a problem whose solution runs on symmetry and collision together.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. Counting round-table seatings is ancient school combinatorics; the general habit of dividing by symmetries crystallized slowly. The orbit-averaging theorem that repairs the divide-by-\(n\) trap circulated among Cauchy, Frobenius and Burnside — appearing in Burnside's 1911 book, attributed there to Frobenius, and now bearing Burnside's name: Stigler's law of eponymy ("no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer") doing exactly what it says. The theorem's full counting power arrived with Pólya in 1937, counting chemical arrangements — the same census discipline as Exercise 5, scaled up. Gauss's pairing of \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 100\) with its own reversed copy is the swap move at its most famous — and that story is folklore with a name attached, Stigler's law again. None of this history is load-bearing: block 2's division, block 4's ledgers, and Exercise 5's census all stand on their own counts.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the small round table

Five people, \(A\)–\(E\), sit around a round table with identical chairs. (a) How many seatings are there? (b) Verify the division honestly: show that the \(5! = 120\) row-orders fall into orbits of exactly five descriptions each.

Hint

For (b): take the row-order \(ABCDE\) and list all its rotations; then argue that any rotation other than the full turn changes at least one person's chair — all five people are distinct.

Worked answer

(a) The rotations of \(ABCDE\) are \(BCDEA, CDEAB, DEABC, EABCD\) — five descriptions of one seating, and there are no others: a rotation that returns every distinct person to their own chair must be the full turn. So each seating has exactly five row-descriptions and the count is \(5!/5 = 120/5 = 24\). (b) Walking through all \(120\) row-orders and grouping them by rotation confirms \(24\) orbits of exactly \(5\) members each — no smaller orbit can occur, because a smaller orbit would mean a non-trivial rotation fixing a seating of five distinct people, which (a)'s argument rules out. The division is honest because every family has the same, proved size: five.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — three sums, three ledgers

Two fair dice are thrown. Use the swap symmetry — honestly — to compute the probability of: (a) sum \(8\); (b) sum \(7\); (c) sum \(9\). In each case write the family ledger (how many swap-pairs, how many fixed pairs) before multiplying.

Hint

Fixed pairs are pairs with \(a = b\); they exist only when the sum is even. Count representatives with \(a \le b\), then convert to ordered pairs with the ledger: pairs with \(a < b\) contribute two ordered pairs each; pairs with \(a = b\) contribute one.

Worked answer

(a) Sum \(8\), representatives \(a \le b\): \((2,6), (3,5), (4,4)\). Ledger: two pairs of size 2, one of size 1 — \(2 + 2 + 1 = 5\) ordered pairs, probability \(5/36\). (b) Sum \(7\): \((1,6), (2,5), (3,4)\) — all with \(a < b\), since \(7\) is odd and no \(a = b\) pair can sum to it. Ledger: three pairs of size 2 — \(6\) ordered pairs, probability \(6/36 = 1/6\). (c) Sum \(9\): \((3,6), (4,5)\) — again no fixed pair. Ledger: two pairs of size 2 — \(4\) ordered pairs, probability \(4/36 = 1/9\). Notice how the parity of the sum decides the ledger's shape before any counting: odd sums have no fixed families, even sums must book theirs separately.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the square wins twice

(a) Among all rectangles with perimeter \(40\), prove that the square has the greatest area. (b) Among all rectangles with area \(100\), prove that the square has the least perimeter. (c) What do the two problems have in common — and what does "self-twin" mean in each?

Hint

For (a): sides \(a, 20-a\); for (b): sides \(a, 100/a\). In both, locate the candidate by the side-swap symmetry first, then prove the comparison with an identity. For (b), the quantity to dominate is \(2a + 200/a\), and the target value to find first is the perimeter of the square.

Worked answer

(a) Sides \(a\) and \(20-a\), area \(a(20-a)\). The side-swap is a quarter-turn of the same rectangle, so a unique winner must be self-twin: \(a = 20-a\), so \(a = 10\), candidate area \(100\). The certificate: \(100 - a(20-a) = (a-10)^2 \ge 0\) for every \(a\), so the square's area \(100\) is the maximum, with equality exactly at \(a = 10\). (b) Sides \(a\) and \(100/a\), perimeter \(2(a + 100/a)\). The side-swap is again a quarter-turn; a unique winner must satisfy \(a = 100/a\), so \(a = 10\), candidate perimeter \(2 \cdot 20 = 40\). The certificate: \(2a + \frac{200}{a} - 40 = \frac{2(a-10)^2}{a} \ge 0\) for every \(a > 0\), so the square's perimeter \(40\) is the minimum. A grid of divisor-sided rectangles confirms both: area \(100\) with sides \(2, 50\) has perimeter \(104\); sides \(4, 25\) gives \(58\); sides \(5, 20\) gives \(50\); the square gives \(40\) — and on the perimeter-\(40\) side, the integer-width areas climb \(19, 36, 51, 64, 75, 84, 91, 96, 99, 100\) and descend the same ladder, peaking only at the square. (c) The two problems are twins of each other: each is the other with the roles of the fixed quantity and the optimized quantity swapped. "Self-twin" means \(a = 20-a\) in (a) and \(a = 100/a\) in (b) — the side-swap symmetry pins the candidate in both, and in both the proof, not the symmetry, is what certifies it. Two problems solved by one structure is a first taste of P16.

Exercise 4 ◆ — diagnose the division

Three students propose three divisions. Diagnose each: honest, or broken — and if broken, which demand of Box P08.A failed, and what is the honest ledger?

  1. "Ten distinct people around a round table: \(10!/10 = 362{,}880\) seatings."
  2. "Positive integers with \(xy = 16\): there are \(5\) ordered solutions, and by swap symmetry \(5/2 \approx 3\) — call it \(3\), close enough."
  3. "A necklace of six beads, three red and three blue: \(20\) linear arrangements, divide by \(6\) rotations — \(20/6 \approx 3.33\), so about \(3\) necklaces."
Worked answer

(1) Honest. All ten people are distinct, so no rotation but the full turn fixes a seating — every orbit has exactly ten members, the ledger is uniform, and \(10!/10 = 362{,}880\) is right. The same argument checked exhaustively on the small cases: five distinct people give \(120\) row-orders in orbits of exactly \(5\); six give \(720\) in orbits of exactly \(6\); seven give \(5{,}040\) in orbits of exactly \(7\). (2) Broken ledger — demand 2 (orbit sizes must be proved), in the form of a fixed point. The five ordered solutions are \((1,16), (2,8), (4,4), (8,2), (16,1)\); the pair \((4,4)\) is its own twin — a family of size 1 — so the division by 2 is illegal. The honest count: \((5-1)/2 + 1 = 3\). The verdict is scarier than a wrong answer: the broken ledger here happens to round to the honest value, so it looks right — only the honest ledger knows why. (3) Broken ledger — demand 2 again, now as uneven orbits. The \(20\) linear arrangements split into one orbit of size 2 (the alternating \(RBRBRB\) and its half-turn partner \(BRBRBR\)) and three orbits of size 6 — \(2 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 3 = 20\) checks out — so the necklace count is \(4\), not \(3.33\) and not \(3\). The non-integer was the move confessing: a division that produces a non-integer count of whole objects is never a rounding inconvenience; it is a proof that the ledger is wrong. All three rows share one moral: the division step is where the mathematics lives, and "about" has no place in a count.

Exercise 5 ◐ — the necklace census, explored

Take all \(64\) binary strings of length \(6\) (think: six-bead necklaces, two colours). Explore: group the strings into rotation families and census the orbit sizes. How many necklaces are there? Then find the pattern in the census, and test it on length \(5\).

Worked answer

Grouping all \(64\) strings under the six rotations: the orbits number \(14\), and the census is \(\{1, 1, 2, 3, 3, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6\}\) — two orbits of size 1 (the constant strings \(000000\) and \(111111\)), one of size 2 (the alternating pair \(010101, 101010\)), two of size 3 (the strings repeating a block of three), and nine of size 6 (everything else). Check the total: \(2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 3 + 9 \cdot 6 = 64\). So there are \(14\) binary necklaces of length \(6\). The pattern in the census: orbit sizes divide the length, and each string's family size is exactly the first time it comes home — the smallest number of single-step rotations after which the string first reappears (\(000000\) comes home after \(1\); \(010101\) after \(2\); \(001001\) after \(3\); a generic string only after \(6\)). Length \(5\), same exploration: \(2^5 = 32\) strings; the census is two orbits of size 1 (the constant strings) and six of size 5 — \(2 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 5 = 32\) — so \(8\) necklaces. One glimpse further, honestly labelled: the necklace count \(14\) is also the average \(\frac{1}{6}(64 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 4 + 2) = 84/6 = 14\), where the six numbers are the counts of strings fixed by each rotation (a rotation by \(k\) positions fixes \(2^{\gcd(6,k)}\) strings); for length \(5\), \(\frac{1}{5}(32 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2) = 40/5 = 8\). Averaging over the symmetry moves like this is a theorem — and it is P36's, not this page's; here the two censuses make it a detector, and a striking one.
