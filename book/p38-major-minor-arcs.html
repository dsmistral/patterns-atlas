The Patterns Atlas · P38 · the fourth move of tier 4

Major and minor arcs

When an integral around the frequency circle counts additive solutions, carve out the narrow rational spikes that carry the structured signal and bound the wide irrational sea as cancelling noise.

Exponential sums \(S(\alpha)\), orthogonality on \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\), and the major/minor split \(\mathfrak{M} \sqcup \mathfrak{m}\). Using the shorthand \(e(\theta) = e^{2\pi i\theta}\), the unit circle \(\alpha \in [0, 1)\) satisfies the wave-orthogonality filter \(\int_0^1 e(k\alpha)\,d\alpha = 1\) if \(k = 0\) and \(0\) if \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\). Given a finite set of integers \(A \subset [0, X]\), its exponential sum \(S(\alpha) = \sum_{a \in A} e(a\alpha)\) turns the additive equation \(a_1 + \cdots + a_s = N\) into the circle integral \(\int_0^1 S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\). The major arcs \(\mathfrak{M}\) are narrow neighborhoods around fractions \(a/q\) with small denominator \(q \le Q\) where the phases \(e(a\alpha)\) align in arithmetic progressions; the minor arcs \(\mathfrak{m} = [0,1) \setminus \mathfrak{M}\) are the remaining frequencies where phases scatter and cancel.

1 · Before we begin

P24 taught us how to build a complicated signal out of simple harmonics and extract individual dial settings by orthogonality (as on the electric-organ square wave \(\sin x + \frac{1}{3}\sin 3x + \frac{1}{5}\sin 5x + \cdots\)). P27 applied contour and Fourier integrals to turn discrete counting problems into continuous wave integrals, and P15 showed us how to trap an intractable quantity between computable upper and lower walls.

Here is the obstacle that stops naive Fourier inversion cold on nonlinear number theory problems like Waring's problem (\(N = m_1^k + \cdots + m_s^k\)) or Goldbach's ternary prime problem (\(N = p_1 + p_2 + p_3\)). Writing the solution count as \(\int_0^1 S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\) is a one-line identity — evaluating that integral is another matter entirely! Unlike a smooth function whose Fourier modes decay at high frequency, the exponential sum \(S(\alpha) = \sum_{m \le M} e(m^2\alpha)\) of squares or primes erupts into violent, needle-sharp spikes near every rational fraction \(\alpha \approx a/q\) with small denominator \(q\), because \(m^2 \bmod q\) repeats periodically! You cannot expand \(S(\alpha)^s\) around \(\alpha = 0\) alone, and you cannot bound \(|S(\alpha)|\) uniformly everywhere. How do you evaluate an integral whose integrand is a forest of arithmetic needles rising out of a choppy sea?

2 · Meet the problem: counting \(30 = m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2 + m_4^2\) from six rational needles

Take the first six squares \(\{0^2, 1^2, 2^2, 3^2, 4^2, 5^2\} = \{0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25\}\) and form their six-wave sum on the frequency circle \(\alpha \in [0, 1)\):

\[f(\alpha) = \sum_{m=0}^5 e^{2\pi i m^2 \alpha} = 1 + e^{2\pi i\alpha} + e^{8\pi i\alpha} + e^{18\pi i\alpha} + e^{32\pi i\alpha} + e^{50\pi i\alpha}.\]

When we raise \(f(\alpha)\) to the fourth power, every product of four terms adds their exponents: \(e^{2\pi i(m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2 + m_4^2)\alpha}\). Multiplying by \(e^{-2\pi i(30)\alpha}\) and integrating around \(\alpha \in [0,1)\) kills every quadruple except those summing to \(30\):

\[R_4(30) = \int_0^1 f(\alpha)^4\,e^{-2\pi i(30)\alpha}\,d\alpha = 48,\]

corresponding to the \(4! = 24\) permutations of \(25 + 4 + 1 + 0 = 30\) plus the \(4! = 24\) permutations of \(16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 30\). Now inspect the fourth-power intensity \(|f(\alpha)|^4\) at specific frequencies around the circle:

If we carve out narrow major arcs \(\mathfrak{M}\) of half-width \(\delta = 0.02\) around the fractions with denominator \(q \le 4\) — covering just \(24\%\) of the circle — the integral over \(\mathfrak{M}\) already yields \(\mathbf{45.72}\) out of \(\mathbf{48.00}\) (\(95.2\%\) of the exact answer!), while the remaining \(76\%\) of the circle (the minor arcs \(\mathfrak{m}\)) contributes only \(+2.28\). As the cutoff \(M \to \infty\), the major arcs shrink to \(0\%\) of the circle's length while carrying \(100\%\) of the asymptotic main term!

3 · Name the move

G. H. Hardy, J. E. Littlewood, and I. M. Vinogradov turned this split into a universal machine for additive number theory. Whenever we integrate an exponential sum \(S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\) over \(\alpha \in [0, 1)\), we choose a denominator cutoff \(Q\) and a width \(\delta = Q / (N \text{ or } M^k)\) so that the intervals \(\mathfrak{M}(a, q) = \left[\frac{a}{q} - \delta,\, \frac{a}{q} + \delta\right]\) around coprime fractions \(1 \le a \le q \le Q\), \(\gcd(a,q) = 1\), are pairwise disjoint.

Local Gauss sums \(S(a,q)\), the singular series \(\mathfrak{S}(N)\), and the singular integral \(\mathfrak{J}(N)\). Near a rational point \(\alpha = \frac{a}{q} + \beta\) (with \(|\beta| \le \delta\) tiny), sorting the summation index \(m = qk + r\) by its remainder \(r \bmod q\) (B01) factors the exponential sum \(S\bigl(\frac{a}{q} + \beta\bigr) \approx \frac{S(a,q)}{q} V(\beta)\) into a local arithmetic factor \(S(a,q) = \sum_{r=1}^q e(a r^k / q)\) (measuring congruence obstructions modulo \(q\)) times a smooth archimedean integral \(V(\beta) = \int_0^M e(\beta x^k)\,dx\) (measuring real density). Summing the \(s\)-th powers of the local factors over all \(q \ge 1\) builds the singular series \(\mathfrak{S}(N) = \sum_{q=1}^\infty \sum_{(a,q)=1} \left(\frac{S(a,q)}{q}\right)^s e(-Na/q)\), while integrating \(V(\beta)^s e(-N\beta)\,d\beta\) builds the singular integral \(\mathfrak{J}(N)\).

Pattern P38 — Major and minor arcs. To evaluate or bound the number of solutions \(R(N) = \int_0^1 S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\) to an additive problem, partition the unit circle \([0,1) = \mathfrak{M} \sqcup \mathfrak{m}\) into major arcs \(\mathfrak{M} = \bigsqcup_{q \le Q} \bigsqcup_{(a,q)=1} \mathfrak{M}(a,q)\) (narrow neighborhoods of low-denominator rationals) and minor arcs \(\mathfrak{m} = [0,1) \setminus \mathfrak{M}\):

\[R(N) = \underbrace{\int_{\mathfrak{M}} S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha}_{\text{Structured main term } \mathfrak{S}(N)\,\mathfrak{J}(N)} \;+\; \underbrace{\int_{\mathfrak{m}} S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha}_{\text{Pseudorandom error bounded by } \bigl(\sup_{\mathfrak{m}}|S|\bigr)^{s-2}\int_0^1 |S|^2}.\]

Or, in words: Do not treat all frequencies on the circle equally. Isolate the rational resonances \(\alpha \approx a/q\) where modular arithmetic creates constructive spikes and integrate them to get the main term; bound the rest of the circle by combining a supremum bound with an \(L^2\) energy identity.

Box P38.A — The three obligations of a circle-method proof.

  1. Disjoint Farey / Dirichlet Calibration (P10): Choose denominator threshold \(Q\) and arc half-width \(\delta\) so that distinct major arcs \(\mathfrak{M}(a,q)\) and \(\mathfrak{M}(a',q')\) do not overlap (\(\left|\frac{a}{q} - \frac{a'}{q'}\right| \ge \frac{1}{qq'} > 2\delta\), requiring \(2Q^2 \delta < 1\)), while Dirichlet's pigeonhole approximation guarantees every \(\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}\) lies near a fraction with denominator \(q > Q\).
  2. Major-Arc Local-to-Global Factorization: On each major arc \(\alpha = \frac{a}{q} + \beta\), factor \(S(\alpha) = \frac{S(a,q)}{q}V(\beta) + O(q + q|\beta|X)\) into its mod-\(q\) Gauss sum and real integral, and check that the singular series \(\mathfrak{S}(N) = \prod_p \sigma_p(N)\) is strictly positive (no local mod-\(p\) congruence obstruction).
  3. Minor-Arc Hybrid Squeeze (Supremum \(\times\) Parseval \(L^2\), P15 + P24): Never bound \(\int_{\mathfrak{m}} |S(\alpha)|^s\,d\alpha\) by \(\sup_{\mathfrak{m}} |S(\alpha)|^s\) alone! Pull out \(s - 2\) (or \(s - 2r\)) powers via the supremum on \(\mathfrak{m}\) and spend the remaining \(2\) (or \(2r\)) powers on a full-circle Parseval energy integral:

    \[\left|\int_{\mathfrak{m}} S(\alpha)^s e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\right| \;\le\; \left(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |S(\alpha)|\right)^{s-2} \int_0^1 |S(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha.\]

Key Signature
Claim type An asymptotic count \(R(N) \sim \mathfrak{S}(N)\mathfrak{J}(N) + o(\text{main})\) for representations of a large integer \(N\) as a sum of \(s\) elements from a structured set \(A\) (squares, \(k\)-th powers, primes), or \(k\)-AP counts in dense sets.
Problem shape An additive condition \(a_1 + \cdots + a_s = N\) whose multiplicative constraints (primality or \(k\)-th powers) become spiky exponential sums in Fourier space.
Tools at hand Dirichlet's pigeonhole theorem (P10), Weyl differencing / van der Corput bounds on \(\sup_{\mathfrak{m}}|S|\), and Parseval/Hua mean-value theorems \(\int_0^1 |S|^{2r}\,d\alpha\) (P24).

Three-key diagnosis. Reach for P38 whenever Fourier inversion turns an additive counting problem into a circle integral with sharp spikes at rational frequencies.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 · Weyl differencing: squaring a quadratic sum to linearize its phase

How do we prove that the quadratic sum \(S(\alpha) = \sum_{m=1}^M e(\alpha m^2)\) is small on the minor arcs \(\mathfrak{m}\)? Because the exponent \(\alpha m^2\) is quadratic in \(m\), \(S(\alpha)\) is not a geometric series. Hermann Weyl discovered in 1916 that multiplying \(S(\alpha)\) by its complex conjugate \(\overline{S(\alpha)}\) and changing variables to the step difference \(h = m_1 - m_2\) (B11) lowers the degree of the phase from \(2\) to \(1\):

\[|S(\alpha)|^2 = \sum_{m_1=1}^M \sum_{m_2=1}^M e\bigl(\alpha(m_1^2 - m_2^2)\bigr) = \sum_{h=-(M-1)}^{M-1} e(\alpha h^2) \sum_{m \in I_h} e(2\alpha h\,m),\]

where \(I_h = [1, M] \cap [1-h, M-h]\) is an interval of length at most \(M\). Look at the inner sum over \(m\): the \(m^2\) term has canceled completely, leaving a pure geometric progression with common ratio \(e(2\alpha h)\)! Summing the geometric series (P69) and squeezing (P15) by \(|\sin(\pi\theta)| \ge 2\|\theta\|_{\mathbb{Z}}\) gives

\[|S(\alpha)|^2 \;\le\; M + \sum_{h=1}^{M-1} 2\min\!\left(M,\; \frac{1}{2\|2\alpha h\|_{\mathbb{Z}}}\right).\]

By Dirichlet's pigeonhole theorem (P10), any \(\alpha \in [0,1)\) lies within \(\frac{1}{qQ}\) of a reduced fraction \(\frac{a}{q}\) with \(q \le Q\); on the minor arcs \(\mathfrak{m}\), we know \(q > Q\) (or \(|\alpha - a/q| > \delta\)), so as \(h\) runs through blocks of length \(q\), the fractional parts \(\{2\alpha h\}\) spread evenly across the \(q\) pigeonholes of width \(1/q\), yielding Weyl's bound

\[|S(\alpha)| \;\ll\; M^{1+\varepsilon}\!\left(\frac{1}{q} + \frac{1}{M} + \frac{q}{M^2}\right)^{1/2}.\]

Choosing \(Q = M\) guarantees \(M \le q \le M\) on the boundary of \(\mathfrak{m}\), saving a power \(M^{-1/2 + \varepsilon}\) across the entire minor-arc sea!

Exemplar 2 · Quadratic Gauss sums (\(|S(a,q)| = \sqrt{q}\)) and the singular series \(\mathfrak{S}(N)\)

Now step onto a major arc \(\alpha = \frac{a}{q} + \beta\) around a fraction with odd denominator \(q\) and \(\gcd(a,q) = 1\). By Fig P38.2, the size of the rational spike is controlled by the normalized Gauss sum \(\frac{S(a,q)}{q}\), where \(S(a,q) = \sum_{r=1}^q e(a r^2 / q)\). Apply the exact same squaring trick as in Exemplar 1, this time modulo \(q\) (B02), setting \(r_1 \equiv r_2 + h \pmod q\):

\[|S(a,q)|^2 = \sum_{r_1=1}^q \sum_{r_2=1}^q e\!\left(\frac{a(r_1^2 - r_2^2)}{q}\right) = \sum_{h=1}^q e\!\left(\frac{ah^2}{q}\right) \sum_{r_2=1}^q e\!\left(\frac{2ah\,r_2}{q}\right).\]

Because \(q\) is odd and \(\gcd(a,q) = 1\), we have \(\gcd(2a, q) = 1\). Therefore, by character orthogonality on \(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}\) (P36), the inner sum \(\sum_{r_2=1}^q e(2ah r_2 / q)\) vanishes for every \(h \not\equiv 0 \pmod q\), and equals \(q\) when \(h \equiv 0 \pmod q\)! Consequently:

\[|S(a,q)|^2 = 1 \cdot q = q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bigl|S(a,q)\bigr| = \sqrt{q}, \qquad \left|\frac{S(a,q)}{q}\right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}.\]

\(q = 3\) (\(\equiv 3 \bmod 4\)) \(0, 1, 1\) \(1 + 2e(1/3) = +i\sqrt{3}\) \(\sqrt{3} \approx 1.732051\) \(1 / 9 \approx 0.1111\)
\(q = 5\) (\(\equiv 1 \bmod 4\)) \(0, 1, 4, 4, 1\) \(1 + 2e(1/5) + 2e(4/5) = +\sqrt{5}\) \(\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236068\) \(1 / 25 = 0.0400\)
\(q = 7\) (\(\equiv 3 \bmod 4\)) \(0, 1, 4, 2, 2, 4, 1\) \(+i\sqrt{7}\) \(\sqrt{7} \approx 2.645751\) \(1 / 49 \approx 0.0204\)
\(q = 9\) (\(\equiv 1 \bmod 4\)) \(0, 1, 4, 0, 7, 7, 0, 4, 1\) \(+3\) \(\sqrt{9} = 3.000000\) \(1 / 81 \approx 0.0123\)
\(q = 11\) (\(\equiv 3 \bmod 4\)) \(0, 1, 4, 9, 5, 3, 3, 5, 9, 4, 1\) \(+i\sqrt{11}\) \(\sqrt{11} \approx 3.316625\) \(1 / 121 \approx 0.0083\)

Summing the \(s\)-th powers \(\left(\frac{S(a,q)}{q}\right)^s e(-Na/q)\) over all \(q \ge 1\) and \(\gcd(a,q) = 1\), the \(q\)-th term is bounded by \(\phi(q)\,q^{-s/2} \le q^{1 - s/2}\), which converges absolutely whenever \(s \ge 5\). Even better, by the Chinese Remainder Theorem (B04, P30), \(\mathfrak{S}(N) = \prod_p \sigma_p(N)\) factors into an Euler product of local \(p\)-adic solution densities! If \(N\) violates a congruence condition modulo \(p^r\) (such as \(N \equiv 7 \pmod 8\) for \(s = 3\) squares), the local factor \(\sigma_2(N) = 0\) kills the main term automatically.

Exemplar 3 · Vinogradov's three-primes theorem (\(N = p_1 + p_2 + p_3\)) and the \(L^\infty \times L^2\) minor-arc squeeze

To count representations of a large odd integer \(N\) as a sum of three primes \(N = p_1 + p_2 + p_3\), I. M. Vinogradov formed the prime exponential sum weighted by \(\ln p\):

\[S_P(\alpha) = \sum_{p \le N} (\ln p)\,e(p\alpha), \qquad R_3(N) = \int_0^1 S_P(\alpha)^3 e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha.\]

On the major arcs \(\mathfrak{M}\) (around \(a/q\) with \(q \le (\ln N)^A\)), primes distribute evenly among the \(\phi(q)\) coprime residue classes, so \(S_P(\frac{a}{q}+\beta) \approx \frac{\mu(q)}{\phi(q)}\int_2^N e(\beta x)\,dx\), producing the major-arc main term

\[\int_{\mathfrak{M}} S_P(\alpha)^3 e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha = \frac{1}{2}\mathfrak{S}_3(N)\,N^2 + o(N^2), \qquad \mathfrak{S}_3(N) = \prod_{p \mid N}\left(1 - \frac{1}{(p-1)^2}\right)\prod_{p \nmid N}\left(1 + \frac{1}{(p-1)^3}\right) > 1.\]

How do we prove that the minor-arc integral \(\int_{\mathfrak{m}} S_P(\alpha)^3 e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\) is \(o(N^2)\)? Apply Obligation 3 of Box P38.A: pull out one power of \(|S_P(\alpha)|\) via the minor-arc supremum, and evaluate the remaining two powers over the whole circle by Parseval's identity (P24):

\[\left|\int_{\mathfrak{m}} S_P(\alpha)^3 e(-N\alpha)\,d\alpha\right| \;\le\; \left(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |S_P(\alpha)|\right) \int_0^1 |S_P(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha = \left(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |S_P(\alpha)|\right) \sum_{p \le N} (\ln p)^2.\]

Look at the exponent miracle: by the Prime Number Theorem, \(\sum_{p \le N} (\ln p)^2 \sim N\ln N\) has size only \(O(N\ln N)\), not \(N^2\)! Therefore, to beat the main term \(\sim c N^2\), we only need \(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |S_P(\alpha)| \ll N / (\ln N)^2\) — a tiny logarithmic saving over the trivial bound \(N\)! Vinogradov achieved \(\sup_{\mathfrak{m}} |S_P(\alpha)| \ll N(\ln N)^{-A}\) for every \(A > 0\), proving that every sufficiently large odd number is the sum of three primes.

5 · When the trick breaks

The circle method is exact as an integral identity, but three famous barriers govern where major-and-minor arc estimates break down:

Exhibit A — The binary square-root barrier (\(s = 2\) Goldbach and twin primes: why \(L^2\) energy is too big!). Why did Exemplar 3 succeed for \(s = 3\) primes (\(N = p_1 + p_2 + p_3\)) but fail for \(s = 2\) primes (\(N = p_1 + p_2\), binary Goldbach)? For \(s = 2\), the expected main term is of size \(\sim \mathfrak{S}_2(N)\frac{N}{(\ln N)^2} \ll N\). Yet on the minor arcs, the best possible cancellation for a sum of \(\sim N/\ln N\) primes is square-root cancellation \(|S_P(\alpha)| \sim \sqrt{N\ln N}\), and by Parseval's identity (P24) the \(L^2\) integral over the circle is

\[\int_0^1 |S_P(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha = \sum_{p \le N} (\ln p)^2 \sim N\ln N \;\gg\; N\,!\]

When \(s = 2\), you have zero spare powers of \(S_P(\alpha)\) to pull out in front of the \(L^2\) integral, and the triangle inequality \(\int_{\mathfrak{m}} |S_P(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha \sim N\ln N\) is strictly larger than the main term \(N\)!

Mitigation: Switch from the circle method to sieve methods (P39 · The optimized sieve, proving Chen's theorem \(N = p + P_2\)) or average over a family of targets \(N\) (showing almost all even numbers are sums of two primes).

Price: Replacing exact two-prime equality with almost-primes or statistical density.

Default: Always check the Parseval \(L^2\) energy \(\int_0^1 |S|^2\) against the expected main term before launching P38; you need \(s \ge 3\) (or \(s > 2k\) for \(k\)-th powers) for \(L^\infty \times L^2\) to win.

Exhibit B — Overlapping major arcs when the cutoff \(Q\) is chosen too large (\(2Q^2 \delta \ge 1\)). To make the minor-arc supremum \(\sup_{\mathfrak{m}} |S(\alpha)|\) as small as possible, it is tempting to push the major-arc denominator cutoff \(Q\) as high as \(M\). But if \(Q^2 \delta \ge 1\), the intervals \(\mathfrak{M}(a, q)\) and \(\mathfrak{M}(a', q')\) around distinct fractions overlap (\(\left|\frac{a}{q} - \frac{a'}{q'}\right| = \frac{1}{qq'} \le 2\delta\)), double-counting the spikes!

Mitigation: Enforce the Farey disjointness lock \(2Q^2\delta < 1\) (typically \(Q = M^\theta\) with \(\theta < 1\)), or use the Kloosterman refinement (exact Farey dissection where each arc ends precisely at the mediant \(\frac{a+a'}{q+q'}\), harnessing cancellation in the sum over \(a \bmod q\)).

Price: Tracking incomplete Kloosterman sums along variable-width Farey intervals.

Default: Always check \(2Q^2\delta < 1\) in Obligation 1 before summing over \((a,q) = 1\).

Exhibit C — Vanishing singular series \(\mathfrak{S}(N) = 0\) due to hidden local congruence obstructions. Even when the minor-arc error is tiny (\(o(M^{s-k})\)) and the real integral \(\mathfrak{J}(N) > 0\) is positive, the coefficient \(\mathfrak{S}(N) = \prod_p \sigma_p(N)\) can vanish identically if a single prime power \(p^r\) blocks the equation modulo \(p^r\) (for instance, sums of \(4\)th powers modulo \(16\), where \(x^4 \equiv 0 \text{ or } 1 \pmod{16}\), requiring \(s \ge 16\) fourth powers locally at \(p = 2\) even though \(s = 5\) suffices globally at all other primes!).

Mitigation: Localize at every prime \(p\) (P30) and verify Hensel's lemma lifting \(\sigma_p(N) > 0\) for all \(p\), paying special attention to \(p = 2\) and primes dividing the exponent \(k\).

Price: Checking finite congruence tables modulo small prime powers.

Default: Never declare \(R(N) \to \infty\) until you have proved \(\sigma_p(N) > 0\) for every prime \(p\).

Honest scope of this chapter. In Waring's problem for \(k\)-th powers, elementary Weyl differencing + Hua's lemma proves the asymptotic formula for \(s \ge 2^k + 1\) variables (\(s \ge 5\) for squares, \(s \ge 9\) for cubes). Reaching \(s = 4\) squares requires the Kloosterman circle refinement (or Jacobi's modular theta identity), and reaching \(G(k) \sim k\ln k\) for large \(k\) requires Vinogradov's mean-value theorem (proved in full generality via Bourgain–Demeter–Guth \(\ell^2\) decoupling, P42).

7 · Lineage interlude

From Ramanujan and Hardy's 1918 partition asymptotic to Littlewood's Waring machine and Vinogradov's trigonometric sums. In 1918 G. H. Hardy and Srinivasa Ramanujan (Proceedings of the London Mathematical Society 17, 75–115) invented the circle method to compute the number of integer partitions \(p(n)\) by integrating its generating function along a circle \(|z| = r < 1\) and isolating the Farey-arc singularities at roots of unity \(e^{2\pi i a/q}\). Between 1920 and 1925, Hardy and J. E. Littlewood (Acta Mathematica 44, "Some problems of 'Partitio Numerorum'") reshaped the method for Waring's problem and conditional prime-sum asymptotics, naming the major arcs (German große Bögen) and minor arcs (kleine Bögen). In 1928–1937 Ivan Matveyevich Vinogradov replaced infinite power series inside the unit disk with finite trigonometric sums \(\sum_{n \le X} e(f(n)\alpha)\) on \(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\) and proved his unconditional minor-arc estimates for primes, settling the ternary Goldbach conjecture for all sufficiently large odd integers (completed for all odd \(N \ge 7\) by Harald Helfgott in 2013).

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Parseval's \(L^2\) energy identity on the six-square sum \(f(\alpha)\)

Return to the six-square exponential sum \(f(\alpha) = \sum_{m=0}^5 e(m^2\alpha) = 1 + e(\alpha) + e(4\alpha) + e(9\alpha) + e(16\alpha) + e(25\alpha)\) from Section 2.
(a) Compute the full-circle \(L^2\) energy integral \(\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha\) and the \(L^4\) energy integral \(\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^4\,d\alpha\) exactly by counting solutions to \(m_1^2 = m_2^2\) and \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 = m_3^2 + m_4^2\) with \(m_j \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}\).
(b) Using your exact value of \(\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha\) and the minor-arc supremum \(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |f(\alpha)|^2 = 5.0\) on a minor-arc set \(\mathfrak{m}\), state the upper bound on \(\int_{\mathfrak{m}} |f(\alpha)|^4\,d\alpha\) given by Obligation 3 of Box P38.A.

Hint

In (a), expanding \(|f(\alpha)|^2 = f(\alpha)\overline{f(\alpha)}\) and integrating picks out pairs \(m_1^2 = m_2^2\) (\(6\) diagonal pairs). Expanding \(|f(\alpha)|^4 = |f(\alpha)^2|^2\) counts ordered quadruples \((m_1, m_2, m_3, m_4) \in \{0..5\}^4\) with \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 = m_3^2 + m_4^2\); remember the Pythgorean triple \(3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2 + 0^2 = 25\)!

Worked answer

(a) Exact \(L^2\) and \(L^4\) integrals by orthogonality (P24): Since the six squares \(0, 1, 4, 9, 16, 25\) are distinct, \(m_1^2 = m_2^2\) holds only for the \(6\) diagonal pairs \(m_1 = m_2\), so

\[\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha = 6.\]

For \(\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^4\,d\alpha\), we count quadruples with \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 = m_3^2 + m_4^2\):

  • The \(6\) diagonal sums \(2m^2\) (\(0, 2, 8, 18, 32, 50\)) each have \(1^2 = 1\) representation (contributing \(6 \times 1 = 6\)).
  • The \(\binom{6}{2} - 2 = 13\) off-diagonal sums \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 \ne 25\) (\(m_1 \ne m_2\)) each have \(2\) ordered pairs \((m_1, m_2)\) and \((m_2, m_1)\), so \(2^2 = 4\) quadruples (contributing \(13 \times 4 = 52\)).
  • The special sum \(25 = 5^2 + 0^2 = 0^2 + 5^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 4^2 + 3^2\) has \(4\) ordered pairs, contributing \(4^2 = 16\) quadruples!

Summing gives \(\int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^4\,d\alpha = 6 + 52 + 16 = 74\).

(b) Hybrid \(L^\infty \times L^2\) bound: By Obligation 3 of Box P38.A,

\[\int_{\mathfrak{m}} |f(\alpha)|^4\,d\alpha \;\le\; \left(\sup_{\alpha \in \mathfrak{m}} |f(\alpha)|^2\right) \int_0^1 |f(\alpha)|^2\,d\alpha = 5.0 \times 6 = 30.\]

Exercise 2 ●●○ — Evaluating the Even-Modulus Gauss Sum \(S(a, 2^r)\) and the \(\bmod\;8\) Obstruction for Three Squares

Let \(S(a, q) = \sum_{r=0}^{q-1} e(ar^2/q)\) be the quadratic Gauss sum.
(a) Compute \(S(1, 2)\), \(S(1, 4)\), and \(S(a, 8)\) for all odd \(a \in \{1, 3, 5, 7\}\). Why does \(S(1, 2) = 0\), while \(|S(1, 4)| = 2 = \sqrt{2\cdot 4}\) and \(|S(a, 8)| = 2\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2\cdot 8}\)?
(b) For \(s = 3\) squares, evaluate the \(q = 8\) local contribution \(\sum_{a \in \{1,3,5,7\}} \left(\frac{S(a,8)}{8}\right)^3 e(-Na/8)\) when \(N \equiv 7 \pmod 8\), and verify that combining \(q = 1, 2, 4, 8\) gives zero \(2\)-adic density \(\sigma_2(N) = 0\) whenever \(N \equiv 7 \pmod 8\).

Hint

Modulo \(8\), the squares of \(r = 0, 1, \dots, 7\) are \(0, 1, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 1\) (two \(0\)'s, two \(4\)'s, and four \(1\)'s!). Since \(e(a\cdot 0/8) + e(a\cdot 4/8) = 1 + e(a/2) = 1 - 1 = 0\) for odd \(a\), only the four odd residues \(r^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\) survive in \(S(a, 8)\)!

Worked answer

(a) Gauss sums at \(q = 2, 4, 8\):

  • For \(q = 2\): \(S(1, 2) = e(0/2) + e(1/2) = 1 + (-1) = 0\) (because \((r+1)^2 \equiv r^2 + 1 \pmod 2\) shifts every term by \(-1\)).
  • For \(q = 4\): \(r^2 \bmod 4 = 0, 1, 0, 1\), so \(S(1, 4) = 2(1 + i)\), with \(|S(1, 4)| = 2\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2q}\).
  • For \(q = 8\) and odd \(a\): \(r^2 \bmod 8 = 0, 1, 4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 1\). The two \(0\)'s give \(2e(0) = 2\), and the two \(4\)'s give \(2e(4a/8) = 2(-1) = -2\), which cancel! Only the four odd squares \(r^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\) remain:

    \[S(a, 8) = 4\,e(a/8), \qquad |S(a, 8)| = 4 = \sqrt{2\cdot 8}.\]

(b) Vanishing modulo \(8\) for \(N \equiv 7 \pmod 8\): Since \(S(a, 8)/8 = \frac{1}{2}e(a/8)\), its cube is \(\left(\frac{S(a,8)}{8}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{8}e(3a/8)\). When \(N \equiv 7 \equiv -1 \pmod 8\), we have \(e((3 - N)a/8) = e(4a/8) = e(a/2) = -1\) for every odd \(a \in \{1, 3, 5, 7\}\)! Summing over the \(4\) odd residues gives

\[\sum_{a \in \{1,3,5,7\}} \left(\frac{S(a,8)}{8}\right)^3 e(-7a/8) = \frac{1}{8}\bigl((-1) + (-1) + (-1) + (-1)\bigr) = -\frac{1}{2}.\]

Meanwhile, at \(q = 1\) the term is \(+1\), at \(q = 2\) it is \(0\) (since \(S(1,2)=0\)), and at \(q = 4\) (\(\pm 1 \bmod 4\)) we have \(\frac{1}{8}\bigl((1+i)^3(-i) + (1-i)^3(i)\bigr) = \frac{1}{8}\bigl(2(-1+i)(-i) + 2(-1-i)(i)\bigr) = -\frac{1}{2}\). Summing \(q = 1, 2, 4, 8\) gives

\[\sigma_2(7) = 1 + 0 - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = 0,\]

reflecting the elementary congruence obstruction that \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2 \in \{0,1,4\} + \{0,1,4\} + \{0,1,4\} \not\equiv 7 \pmod 8\).

Exercise 3 ●●● — Computing the Singular Integral \(\mathfrak{J}_s(N)\) via Euler's Beta and Gamma Functions

On the major arc around \(\alpha = 0\) for \(k\)-th powers up to \(M = N^{1/k}\), the smooth archimedean approximation is \(V(\beta) = \int_0^{N^{1/k}} e(\beta x^k)\,dx = \frac{1}{k}\int_0^N u^{\frac{1}{k}-1} e(\beta u)\,du\) (under \(u = x^k\)).
(a) By Fourier inversion (P24), show that the singular integral \(\mathfrak{J}_s(N) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(\beta)^s e(-N\beta)\,d\beta\) equals the \((s-1)\)-fold convolution volume

\[\mathfrak{J}_s(N) = \frac{1}{k^s}\idotsint_{\substack{u_1, \dots, u_{s-1} > 0 \\ u_1 + \cdots + u_{s-1} < N}} (u_1 u_2 \cdots u_{s-1})^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\bigl(N - u_1 - \cdots - u_{s-1}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{k}-1} du_1\cdots du_{s-1}.\]

(b) Rescale \(u_j = N v_j\) (P56) and use Euler's Dirichlet simplex integral \(\idotsint_{v_j > 0, \sum v_j < 1} v_1^{a-1}\cdots v_{s-1}^{a-1}(1-\sum v_j)^{a-1}\,dv = \frac{\Gamma(a)^s}{\Gamma(sa)}\) (with \(a = \frac{1}{k}\)) to prove Hardy and Littlewood's singular integral formula:

\[\mathfrak{J}_s(N) = \frac{\Gamma(1 + 1/k)^s}{\Gamma(s/k)}\,N^{\frac{s}{k} - 1}.\]

Hint

In (b), substituting \(u_j = N v_j\) pulls out \(N^{(1/k - 1)s}\) from the integrand and \(N^{s-1}\) from the differentials \(du_1 \cdots du_{s-1}\), giving total power \(N^{s/k - 1}\). Then use \(\frac{1}{k}\Gamma(1/k) = \Gamma(1 + 1/k)\).

Worked answer

(a) Convolution representation: Since \(V(\beta) = \int_0^\infty \frac{1}{k}u^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\mathbf{1}_{(0,N]}(u) e(\beta u)\,du\) is the Fourier transform of the density \(\rho(u) = \frac{1}{k}u^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\) on \((0, N]\), the inverse Fourier transform \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(\beta)^s e(-N\beta)\,d\beta\) evaluates the \(s\)-fold convolution \((\rho * \cdots * \rho)(N)\), which is the exact integral over the simplex \(u_1 + \cdots + u_s = N\), \(u_j > 0\).

(b) Rescaling and Gamma evaluation: Setting \(u_j = N v_j\) for \(j = 1, \dots, s-1\) factors out \(N^{s(1/k - 1) + (s - 1)} = N^{\frac{s}{k} - 1}\), leaving

\[\mathfrak{J}_s(N) = \frac{N^{\frac{s}{k}-1}}{k^s}\idotsint_{\substack{v_j > 0 \\ \sum v_j < 1}} (v_1\cdots v_{s-1})^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\Bigl(1 - \sum_{j=1}^{s-1} v_j\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{k}-1} dv_1\cdots dv_{s-1} = \frac{N^{\frac{s}{k}-1}}{k^s}\frac{\Gamma(1/k)^s}{\Gamma(s/k)}.\]

Applying the functional equation \(\frac{1}{k}\Gamma(1/k) = \Gamma(1 + 1/k)\) across all \(s\) factors in the numerator yields \(\mathfrak{J}_s(N) = \frac{\Gamma(1 + 1/k)^s}{\Gamma(s/k)}\,N^{\frac{s}{k}-1}\).

Exercise 4 ◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis: circle method, sieve, or elementary modular obstruction?

Diagnose the right tool for each additive problem below:

  1. You want an asymptotic formula as \(N \to \infty\) for the number of ways to write \(N = m_1^3 + m_2^3 + \cdots + m_9^3\) as a sum of \(s = 9\) non-negative cubes.
  2. You want to prove that \(N = 8{,}000{,}007\) cannot be written as a sum of three integer squares \(m_1^2 + m_2^2 + m_3^2\).
  3. You want to prove that there are infinitely many primes \(p\) such that \(p + 2\) has at most two prime factors (\(P_2\)).
Worked answer
  1. Genuine P38 (Major and minor arcs for Waring's problem): Here \(k = 3\) and \(s = 9 = 2^3 + 1 > 2k\); Weyl differencing (twice, lowering degree \(3 \to 2 \to 1\)) combined with Hua's \(L^8\) mean-value bound crushes the minor arcs, while the singular series and singular integral (Exercise 3) give the exact asymptotic main term \(\frac{\Gamma(4/3)^9}{\Gamma(3)}\mathfrak{S}(N)N^2\).
  2. Elementary local obstruction suffices (B02 / P12 / P30): Since \(8{,}000{,}007 = 8 \times 1{,}000{,}000 + 7 \equiv 7 \pmod 8\), and squares modulo \(8\) belong to \(\{0, 1, 4\}\), no three squares can sum to \(7 \pmod 8\). No circle integral is needed to rule out a locally obstructed target.
  3. Fails the binary \(L^2\) barrier (Exhibit A) — use P39 · The optimized sieve instead: Twin primes (\(p' - p = 2\)) is a binary (\(s = 2\)) prime problem where the Parseval \(L^2\) energy \(\sim N\ln N\) exceeds the \(O(N/(\ln N)^2)\) main term by three powers of \(\ln N\). Chen's theorem (\(p + 2 = P_2\)) is proved via optimized linear/quadratic sieves (P39).

Exercise 5 ◐ — Exploration: Roth's theorem on 3-term arithmetic progressions via Fourier major and minor modes

Show how Klaus Roth (1953) transferred P38 from infinite number theory to finite combinatorics on \(\mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}\) (compare P58, Exemplar 3). Let \(A \subset \{1, \dots, M\}\) (for odd \(M\)) have density \(\delta = |A|/M > 0\), and define its discrete Fourier transform \(\widehat{A}(r) = \sum_{a \in A} e(ar/M)\) for \(r \in \mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}\).
(a) Verify by orthogonality that the number of \(3\)-term arithmetic progressions \((a, a+d, a+2d) \in A^3\) (meaning \(a_1 + a_3 = 2a_2\)) equals

\[T_3(A) = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1} \widehat{A}(r)^2\,\widehat{A}(-2r) = \underbrace{\frac{|A|^3}{M}}_{\text{Major mode } r=0} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=1}^{M-1} \widehat{A}(r)^2\,\widehat{A}(-2r)}_{\text{Minor modes } r \ne 0}.\]

(b) Apply the hybrid \(L^\infty \times L^2\) bound (Obligation 3 of Box P38.A) to the minor modes \(r \ne 0\) using Parseval's identity \(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1} |\widehat{A}(r)|^2 = |A| = \delta M\). Show that if \(\sup_{r \ne 0} |\widehat{A}(r)| \le \frac{1}{2}\delta^2 M\), then \(T_3(A) \ge \frac{1}{2}\delta^3 M^2\) (forcing non-trivial 3-APs when \(\delta^3 M^2 > 2\delta M\)); and explain what happens in the opposite case when a single minor mode \(r_0 \ne 0\) is large (\(|\widehat{A}(r_0)| > \frac{1}{2}\delta^2 M\)).

Hint

In (b), bound \(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r \ne 0} |\widehat{A}(r)|^2 |\widehat{A}(-2r)| \le \bigl(\sup_{r \ne 0}|\widehat{A}(-2r)|\bigr)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r} |\widehat{A}(r)|^2\) (since \(M\) is odd, \(-2r \ne 0 \bmod M\) for \(r \ne 0\)).

Worked answer

(a) Major mode \(r = 0\) vs. Minor modes \(r \ne 0\): Since \(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1} e(r(a_1 + a_3 - 2a_2)/M) = \mathbf{1}_{a_1 + a_3 \equiv 2a_2}\), expanding \(\widehat{A}(r)^2\widehat{A}(-2r)\) counts solutions to \(a_1 + a_3 \equiv 2a_2 \pmod M\). At the major mode \(r = 0\), \(\widehat{A}(0) = |A| = \delta M\), giving the random prediction \(\frac{|A|^3}{M} = \delta^3 M^2\).

(b) The dichotomy between pseudorandomness and density increment: Because \(M\) is odd, \(r \ne 0 \implies -2r \not\equiv 0 \pmod M\). Applying Obligation 3 of Box P38.A gives

\[\left|\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=1}^{M-1} \widehat{A}(r)^2\,\widehat{A}(-2r)\right| \;\le\; \left(\max_{s \ne 0} |\widehat{A}(s)|\right)\frac{1}{M}\sum_{r=0}^{M-1} |\widehat{A}(r)|^2 = \left(\max_{s \ne 0} |\widehat{A}(s)|\right)\delta M.\]

If \(\max_{s \ne 0} |\widehat{A}(s)| \le \frac{1}{2}\delta^2 M\) (minor modes are small / \(A\) is pseudorandom), then the minor sum is at most \(\frac{1}{2}\delta^3 M^2\), so \(T_3(A) \ge \delta^3 M^2 - \frac{1}{2}\delta^3 M^2 = \frac{1}{2}\delta^3 M^2\). Conversely, if some mode \(r_0 \ne 0\) has \(|\widehat{A}(r_0)| > \frac{1}{2}\delta^2 M\) (a major-arc resonance!), then \(A\) correlates with the wave \(e(r_0 x / M)\); partitioning \(\mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}\) into sub-progressions where \(e(r_0 x / M)\) is nearly constant increases the density of \(A\) from \(\delta\) to \(\delta + c\delta^2\), which can happen at most \(O(1/\delta)\) times before \(\delta\) exceeds \(1\)!
