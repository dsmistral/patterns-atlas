The Patterns Atlas · P38 · the twelfth move of tier 4

Major and minor arcs

How many ways can three dice make ten? The census is a table anyone can build; the explanation is a circle. Put the count on a circle — one full turn, the pieces spinning as they go — and the count becomes an average, the average becomes a landscape with one tall spike, and the question of where a count's mass lives becomes a question of where the circle is loud. Split the loud from the quiet, integrate the first honestly, bound the second crudely, and counting becomes analysis with walls. The circle, it turns out, is where additive questions agree to be read.

New word: the counting sum. Fix a set of pieces — say the die faces \(1\) through \(6\) — and wrap them into one function of the angle \(\theta\): \[ F(\theta) \;=\; e^{i\theta} + e^{2i\theta} + \cdots + e^{6i\theta}. \] Each piece is a wave of one full turn per unit of its face value (P24 drew these waves; here they stand on a circle). The machine reads back counts by averaging: the number of ordered \(s\)-tuples of pieces summing to \(n\) is exactly the average of \(F(\theta)^{s}\,e^{-in\theta}\) over one full turn — the target total \(n\) is tuned to cancel every spin except the one that lands on it. This is the finite cousin of the shelf series P27 read at its breaks: same object, wrapped around a circle instead of unrolled along a line.
New word: major arc — where the sum is loud. The counting sum has a height at every angle, and the heights are wildly uneven: near the angle \(0\) (and the full turn \(2\pi\)) every piece points the same way and \(F\) stands at its maximum \(6\); a third of a turn away it is exactly zero. A major arc is a stretched neighborhood of a loud angle — an angle where the sum piles up. In general the loud angles are the rational fractions of a turn \(\frac{a}{q}\) with small denominator \(q\): the pieces fall back into step there, and the smaller the denominator, the louder and wider the pileup. The denominators are where the arithmetic hides — this page's central example needs only \(q=1\), and the general case is the same story told once per denominator.
New word: minor arc — where the sum is quiet. Everything else: the rest of the circle, where the pieces point every which way and the sum stays small. The move's discipline, inherited from P15, is that the minor arcs are never integrated — they are bounded. One wall per quiet stretch: the largest height there, times the stretch's length. A wall you can evaluate is a wall you can spend; the counting read lives or dies by how fast those walls shrink.

1 · Before we begin

Three pages built this one. P15 built the walls: two computable bounds that trap an unknowable quantity, and the license to call a thing known when the walls close. P27 taught the local read: a counting question, carried off the real line, is answered at the break — the nearest singularity sets the radius, and the break's shape sets the growth. P28 bent the plane so the breaks sit where they are cheapest to read. This page is the one those three were aiming at, and the published pages say so in their own voices. P28's footer hands over the commission: up the tier — major and minor arcs (P38, planned): where the circle this page kept bending becomes the place counts live, and the arcs divide where a count's mass concentrates. This page's readers already know how to rearrange the arcs; the counting is the new gear. And P27's door out named this page among its next gear: reading many breaks at once, arcs of them, major and minor (P38, planned). P15 pointed here too, from its own door list: The frontier where bounds stop being scaffolding and become load-bearing: split the problem into main roads that carry the weight exactly and side roads that need only bounds … the honest home of every wall that never met its partner.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P27's read was local: one break, one radius, one growth law — because a shelf series has a single nearest break. Counting questions are not so polite. The object on the circle is a trigonometric polynomial, and its loud angles come in crowds — one crowd per small denominator — with the answer's mass smeared across all of them at once. No single break owns the count. The move is to stop looking for one and to split the circle instead: arcs where the sum is loud get the full local treatment, one break-read each; everything between gets a wall. The count comes out as a main term — arithmetic, from the loud angles — plus an error, from the walls. The whole game is making the error honest.

2 · The encounter

Three ordinary dice. How many ordered triples make a total of \(n\)? Brute bookkeeping answers it: run the \(6^{3} = 216\) triples and tally. The tally, from \(n=3\) to \(n=18\):

\(1,\; 3,\; 6,\; 10,\; 15,\; 21,\; 25,\; 27,\; 27,\; 25,\; 21,\; 15,\; 10,\; 6,\; 3,\; 1\) — sixteen values, summing to \(216\), peaking at \(27\) for the totals \(10\) and \(11\). Any child can produce this table by patience. The question this page cares about is different: why is the peak where it is, why do the tails fall the way they fall, and what would you do if the table had a trillion entries and brute patience was not on the menu?

The counting sum of the on-ramp answers by averaging. With \(F(\theta) = e^{i\theta} + e^{2i\theta} + \cdots + e^{6i\theta}\), the claim is:

\[ N(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi} F(\theta)^{3}\, e^{-in\theta}\, d\theta. \]

Check it the honest way first — on a grid, where the average is a plain sum of \(216\) sample values and the integral claim becomes arithmetic. Machine-run: the \(216\)-point average reproduces all sixteen census values to the integer, from \(1\) at \(n=3\) through the double peak \(27, 27\) and back down to \(1\) at \(n=18\). Nothing was approximated; the average is the count. (Why a grid can be exact is Exhibit C — and the exactness threshold turns out to be \(16\), not \(216\); hold that thought.)

Read the landscape. The spike at \(\theta = 0\) is where all six waves agree; its neighborhood — the shaded band, one twelfth of a turn on each side — is where the sum still exceeds \(1/\sin(\pi/12) = 3.8637\), the largest height anywhere in the quiet remainder (attained exactly at the band's edge, and the bound \(1/\sin(\delta/2)\) at distance \(\delta\) from the spike is the wall this page spends). Between loud angles the sum dies completely — exactly zero at every third of a turn — and its leftover bumps never exceed \(2\). When this landscape is raised to the third power (three dice), the spike's neighborhood towers over everything else: \(6^{3} = 216\) at the spike against at most \(2^{3} = 8\)... but the count at \(n=10\) is only \(27\), so the loud stretch cannot simply be everything — the cancellation \(e^{-in\theta}\) is doing delicate work inside it. Integrate the loud stretch honestly and the spike's contribution to the peak comes to \(25.043\) against the true \(27\): the loud stretch carries \(93\%\) of the answer, the quiet stretch the rest. Fig P38.2 lays the two ledges side by side.

The encounter's lesson, before any naming: the count is an average on a circle; the average's mass is concentrated near the loud angle; and "near" is a measurable stretch of the circle, not a metaphor. Everything this page adds is the discipline for doing this when the table has a trillion entries and the loud angles come in crowds.

3 · The pattern

Pattern P38 — Major and minor arcs. When a counting question is carried to the circle as a coefficient — \(N(n)\), the number of \(s\)-tuples of pieces summing to \(n\), read off as the average of \(F(\theta)^{s} e^{-in\theta}\) — split the circle at the loud angles: the rational fractions \(\frac{a}{q}\) of a turn with small denominator \(q\), where the pieces fall back into step. Around each loud angle take a major arc, wide enough to hold the pileup; integrate there with the full local machinery — each major arc is P27's break-read run once, and the collection of local reads assembles into a main term whose arithmetic is the denominators'. On the remaining minor arcs, integrate nothing: bound the sum by its largest height times the arc's length — P15's wall, one per quiet stretch. The count emerges as \[ N(n) \;=\; \text{main term (from the major arcs)} \;+\; \text{error (from the minor arcs)}, \] and the method's whole content is the bookkeeping that makes the error smaller than the resolution demanded. What the major arcs see: growth, shape, the peak's location. What only the minor arcs see: the corrections — and, sometimes, that the true count is zero where the main term is not.

Or, in words: put the count on a circle; find where the circle is loud; read those stretches exactly and wall off the rest. The loud stretches are arithmetic — denominators, remainders, local obstructions; the quiet stretches are analysis — heights and lengths, walls and squeezes. The count is the sum of the two ledgers.

Three obligations, and the pattern fails without any one of them:

Box P38.A — the three obligations. First, locate the loud angles. The rationals \(\frac{a}{q}\) with small \(q\); for the dice, just \(q=1\) — the angle \(0\). Miss one and its contribution lands in the walls, where it is invisible. Second, integrate the major arcs honestly. Each major arc gets a local model of \(F\) near its loud angle — for the dice, \(F(\theta) \approx 6\, e^{3.5 i\theta}\) with the cubic giving a smooth peak — and the local reads must be assembled, not just the biggest one taken. Third, bound the minor arcs. A wall per stretch: \(\bigl|F(\theta)\bigr| \le 1/\sin(\delta/2)\) at distance \(\delta\) from the nearest loud angle, times the stretch's length. The wall must actually be small enough — and knowing whether it is, is knowing whether the method applies at all.

Diagnosis. Claim type: a count, usually asymptotic — the growth law with an honest error term, or an exact small-case census used as a detector. Problem shape: additive — a total built from independent pieces, where each piece contributes a wave. Tools at hand: P27's local read at a break, P15's trapping walls, P10's bookkeeping for the small cases that keep everyone honest.

How you could have found this move: start from the exactness you already trust. The grid average reproduces the census because the sample points are just far enough apart that no two totals alias — so an exact read demands a grid finer than the totals' spread, and a trillion-entry table cannot afford one. The escape is to stop demanding exactness where the sum is quiet: the heights \(\bigl|F(\theta)\bigr|\) concentrate wherever the pieces fall back into step, and that concentration is visible in the two-dice spike landscape long before any notation arrives. Split there, integrate the loud stretches with the local machinery you already own, and wall the quiet stretches with P15's bound — the method is the only honest division of labor between what you can compute and what you can only bound.

4 · Three exemplars

Exemplar 1 · The peak, priced two ways

Three dice, the total \(n=10\), the count \(27\) — machine-census, \(216\) triples. The loud stretch (\(\bigl|\theta\bigr| \le \pi/6\), one twelfth of a turn on each side of the spike), integrated with the trapezoid rule at \(200{,}000\) steps, contributes \(25.043\). The quiet stretch therefore contributes \(27 - 25.043 = 1.957\) — about \(7.25\%\) of the answer. The Gaussian read (each die has variance \(35/12 \approx 2.9167\); three dice, variance \(8.75\), standard deviation \(2.958\)) puts the peak at \(216/\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 8.75} \approx 29.13\): right shape, \(8\%\) high at the top — the Gaussian is the loud stretch's local model pushed past its license. All three numbers — \(27\), \(25.043\), \(29.13\) — are one machine run; the census is exact by enumeration, the loud-stretch integral by the trapezoid rule whose error at this resolution is below the printed digit, and the Gaussian by its formula.

Now the tails, where the ledger turns honest. At \(n=3\) the true count is \(1\) — the single triple \((1,1,1)\) — but the loud stretch alone integrates to \(1.164\): it overshoots. The quiet stretch's contribution is \(-0.164\), not a positive bonus. The split into major and minor is a ledger of signed corrections, not a partition into parts; the quiet stretch exists to cancel the loud stretch's spillover, and at the edges of the range that is its whole job. A reader who expects the minor arcs to always "add the missing bit" has the bookkeeping backwards — Exercise 4 referees exactly this error.

Exemplar 2 · Two variables, the quiet stretch stops being quiet

Two dice. The census, totals \(2\) through \(12\): \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1\) — the triangle law \(N(n) = 6 - \bigl|n - 7\bigr|\), exact by enumeration and exact again by the \(36\)-point grid average. Now price it the P38 way: integrate only the loud stretch. The result undershoots everywhere: at the peak it reads \(4.67\) against the true \(6\) — a \(22\%\) shortfall — and at the extremes \(1.57\) against \(1\), \(2.45\) against \(2\). With only two factors, \(F(\theta)^{2}\), the quiet stretch carries a fifth of the answer, and no wall around it is small: the largest quiet height \(3.8637\) squared is \(14.9\), against a main term of order \(6\). The method's engine — walls shrinking below the main term — has not started yet. This is the two-variable disease in miniature, and it is why the method's great theorems all live at large \(s\): the quiet stretch submits only when there are enough factors to squash it. Exhibit B shows the same disease at full scale, where it becomes a famous open problem.

Exemplar 3 · Four variables, the wall begins to submit

Four dice. The census, totals \(4\) through \(24\): \(1, 4, 10, 20, 35, 56, 80, 104, 125, 140, 146, 140, 125, 104, 80, 56, 35, 20, 10, 4, 1\) — \(1296\) triples... quadruples, peaking at \(146\) for the total \(14\). The loud stretch alone integrates to \(138.079\): the quiet share is \(7.921\), about \(5.43\%\) — down from \(7.25\%\) at three dice. One more factor and the quiet stretch lost a quarter of its relative weight. The Gaussian read puts the peak at \(1296/\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 35/3} \approx 151.37\), again high by the familiar overshoot. The trend is the method's promise: each added factor multiplies the main term by \(\sim M\) but the quiet wall by only \(\sim 1\) — the walls shrink relative to the signal, and at large \(s\) they vanish below any demanded resolution. The count's mass concentrates where the circle is loud, and the concentration is measurable, monotone, and the entire engine of everything that follows.

5 · Three exhibits

Exhibit A · The wall the loud angles cannot see

Which whole numbers are a sum of three squares? Census to \(40\), by exhaustive search over \(0 \le a \le b \le c\): every \(n\) works except \(7,\; 15,\; 23,\; 28,\; 31,\; 39\). The circle method's plain read — one loud angle at \(q=1\), walls elsewhere — is blind to every one of these failures: its main term is positive at each. The obstruction is local. Squares are only \(0, 1, 4\) modulo \(8\), and three of them sum to \(0,1,2,3,4,5,6\) — never \(7\). So no \(n \equiv 7 \pmod 8\) works: that is \(7, 15, 23, 31, 39\). And when all three squares are even, divide by \(4\) and descend: \(28 = 4 \cdot 7\) inherits \(7\)'s failure. The full legend — \(n\) is a sum of three squares exactly when \(n\) is not of the form \(4^{a}(8b+7)\) — is the product of these local facts, one per small denominator, and the census to \(100\) confirms it: the failures are exactly \(7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71, 79, 87, 92, 95\), every one of the form \(4^{a}(8b+7)\), machine-checked.

This is the singular series in miniature: the main term from the major arcs must be multiplied through by local factors — one per small denominator, each factor the density of solutions modulo \(q\) — before it can be believed. The major arcs see the size of the count; the denominators see its possibility. A count that is zero for local reasons has positive main term and zero truth, and only the local factors know.

The wall, spoken in the singular series' own accent. The denominator-\(q\) local factor is a Gauss sum: \(S(a,q) = \sum_{r=0}^{q-1} e^{2\pi i a r^2/q}\), one term per remainder. Machine-run at the small denominators: \(S(1,2) = 0\) exactly — its two terms cancel; \(S(1,4) = 2(1+i)\); and for odd \(a\), \(S(a,8) = 4\,e^{2\pi i a/8}\), because the squares mod \(8\) are \(0,1,4,1,0,1,4,1\) and the two 0s and two 4s wipe each other out, leaving the four odd residues. Assemble the \(q = 1, 2, 4, 8\) contributions at \(N = 7\) — each \(\bigl(S(a,q)/q\bigr)^{3}\) weighted by \(e^{-2\pi i \cdot 7a/q}\): \(+1\) from \(q=1\), nothing from \(q=2\), then \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) from \(q=4\) and \(-\tfrac{1}{2}\) from \(q=8\). The two-adic local factor is exactly zero: the mod-8 wall of this exhibit, heard the way the major arcs hear it. The census, the wall, and the singular series say the same thing in three dialects, and the machine run confirms all three.

The main term has a second ledger, silent while the pieces are dice. The local factors price possibility; the singular integral prices size — the same split integrated near one loud angle with the arithmetic stripped out. For \(k\)-th powers it too has a closed form, due to Hardy and Littlewood: \[ \mathfrak{J}_s(N) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma\bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{k}\bigr)^{s}}{\Gamma\bigl(\tfrac{s}{k}\bigr)}\, N^{\frac{s}{k}-1}, \] machine-checked by quadrature to within one percent at \((k,s) = (2,3), (2,4), (3,4)\). On this page's dice the role is played by the Gaussian read of Exemplar 1 — the smooth peak whose height the quiet stretch corrects. The full main term is the product of the two ledgers: the singular series says whether, the singular integral says how many.

Exhibit B · Where the method stops: two primes

Is every even number beyond \(2\) a sum of two primes? This is binary Goldbach, and it is open — not because nobody has tried the circle method, but because the method's engine demonstrably does not start at two variables. Exemplar 2 showed the disease in dice: with \(s=2\), the quiet stretch carries a fifth of the answer and no wall around it is small. For two primes the same wall stands, and three centuries of effort have not pushed it. What the method does prove, by exactly the machinery this page built — enough variables that the walls submit, local factors assembled denominator by denominator — is Vinogradov's theorem (1937): every sufficiently large odd number is a sum of three primes. The three-variable version submits; the two-variable version does not. The boundary is not mathematical cosmetics: it is the wall between \(F(\theta)^{3}\) and \(F(\theta)^{2}\), the same wall the dice already showed.

Scope note. This page's machinery is the finite, dice-sized core of the method: exact grid averages, one loud angle, walls measured by \(1/\sin(\delta/2)\). The full method for genuine counting problems — primes, Waring's problem, partitions — runs the same skeleton but needs: major arcs at every small denominator \(q\) (not just \(q=1\)), local models with the denominator's arithmetic inside, and minor-arc walls that beat the main term, which at the frontier takes estimates this page does not build. What transfers honestly: the splitting discipline, the three obligations, and the reason the number of variables is load-bearing. What does not: any claim that the walls here are already small enough — at \(s=2\) they are not, and the page says so where it matters.

Exhibit C · Why the grid can be exact, and how small it may be

The \(216\)-point average reproduced every census value to the integer — and the threshold is sharper than it looks. The counts' support runs from \(3\) to \(18\): sixteen values. A \(K\)-point grid average collides two support values exactly when they differ by a multiple of \(K\), so the average is exact for all sixteen totals precisely when \(K \ge 16\). Machine-run: \(K = 16, 17, \ldots\) all exact; \(K = 15\) fails at \(n = 18\), reading \(2\) — the true \(1\) at \(18\) plus the true \(1\) at \(3\), wrapped around the circle onto the same sample; \(K = 14\) fails at \(n=18\) reading \(4\) — \(1\) plus the \(3\) at \(n=4\). The failure mode is not numerical noise; it is two honest counts landing on the same address. Small-case tests are detectors, not proofs — the exactness here is a theorem about trigonometric polynomials (the sampled average of \(F^{3}e^{-in\theta}\) is a finite sum of shifted coefficients), and the shift-and-collide mechanism is the whole content. The same mechanism at scale is why the circle method's integrals can be traded for finite sums when the pieces are finite — the computer-checked backbone under the analysis.

6 · The doors this opens

Mitigation. When the quiet stretch will not submit, add variables. Each factor multiplies the main term by the pieces' scale while the walls grow by a single height — the quiet share falls, monotone, as Exemplar 3 measured (\(7.25\%\) at three dice, \(5.43\%\) at four). Vinogradov's three-primes theorem is this mitigation executed at the frontier: not a cleverer wall, just enough factors that the old wall is finally small enough. Price. The major arcs' local reads must be assembled honestly — one per small denominator — and their product, the singular series, is genuine arithmetic: Exhibit A's mod-\(8\) wall is a local factor equal to zero, and a main term multiplied by zero is zero. The method prices possibility locally and size globally; skip the local census and the main term lies. Default. When the variables are too few (the \(s=2\) disease) or a local factor vanishes, fall back to what never lies: exact enumeration of small cases (P02), the census as detector, and the honest label "open" where the walls have not fallen — the label Exhibit B earns and keeps.

One floor deeper: the cleverer wall. The wall this page builds — height \(1/\sin(\delta/2)\) times the stretch's length — is the method's crudest minor-arc instrument, and the frontier replaces it with differencing. Weyl (1916) noticed that squaring the sum and reindexing by the step difference \(h = m_1 - m_2\) — B11's discrete derivative, applied to the phase — cancels the leading term: \[ \bigl|S(\alpha)\bigr|^{2} \;=\; \sum_{h} e^{2\pi i \alpha h^{2}} \sum_{m} e^{4\pi i \alpha h m}, \] a geometric progression in \(m\) — P69's ledger — inside a sum whose phase is only linear in \(h\). Machine-run: the identity holds to \(10^{-12}\) at \(M = 6\) and \(M = 12\), and the derived wall \(\bigl|S(\alpha)\bigr|^{2} \le M + \sum_{h=1}^{M-1} 2\min\!\bigl(M, \tfrac{1}{2\|2\alpha h\|}\bigr)\) holds at every sample angle tested. Iterating the trick and pigeonholing the angles (P10) yields Weyl's bound \(\bigl|S(\alpha)\bigr| \ll M^{1+\varepsilon}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{q} + \tfrac{1}{M} + \tfrac{q}{M^{2}}\bigr)^{1/2}\) on the minor arcs — the quiet sea beaten down by a power of \(M\) (our sweep to \(M = 24\) never saw the implied constant exceed \(1.3\); a detector, not a proof). The dice never need it; Vinogradov's three primes cannot live without it. The door is named, its machinery owed.

The doors themselves. The optimized sieve (P39) is the sibling splitter: it too divides a count by where its mass can live — sifted residues versus the rest — and its "small sieve versus large sieve" wall-discipline is this page's quiet-stretch bookkeeping wearing number-theory clothes; readers of both will recognize the same ledger. And down P27's original door list, the next gear after this one is tropicalize (P40, planned): trading the smooth for the piecewise-linear, where the breaks are not obstacles to read around but the whole structure — the circle method's loud/quiet split taken to the limit where only the breaks remain. Nothing on that page is assumed here; the door is named, its machinery owed.

7 · Lineage

Five dates, one line each, none load-bearing. Gauss (1801, in the Disquisitiones) proved the three-squares legend of Exhibit A — the local obstruction read off before the circle existed to read it on. Hardy and Ramanujan (1918) built the circle method to count partitions, the subject's birth. Hardy and Littlewood (1920s) generalized it into a system — the major/minor vocabulary is theirs — and staked Waring's problem on it. Vinogradov (1937) tamed the minor arcs deeply enough for three primes, the method's first frontier theorem. Vaughan's The Hardy–Littlewood Method (2nd edition, 1997) is the modern treatment this page's scope note gestures toward. The pattern — split where the mass is, read exactly there, wall off the rest — is older than all of it: every estimating accountant has lived it.

Exercise 1 · The bridge, by hand ●○○
(a) Two dice, the counting sum \(F(\theta) = e^{i\theta} + \cdots + e^{6i\theta}\): evaluate \(\bigl|F(\theta)\bigr|\) at \(\theta = 0\), at a third of a turn, and at a quarter of a turn, and check the three answers against Fig P38.1's landscape. (b) Write the \(36\)-point grid average that claims to produce the two-dice census, and verify all eleven values \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1\) by direct enumeration of the \(36\) ordered pairs. (c) The triangle law says \(N(n) = 6 - \bigl|n-7\bigr|\): confirm it on all eleven totals. (d) The energy ledger: show \(\int_0^1 \bigl|F(\theta)\bigr|^{2}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi} = 6\) and \(\int_0^1 \bigl|F(\theta)\bigr|^{4}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi} = \sum_n N(n)^2 = 146\) — orthogonality for the first, ordered pairs of totals for the second — and use the quiet stretch's largest height (\(\bigl|F\bigr|^2 \le 14.93\) there) to bound the quiet stretch's fourth-power share.

Hint

(a) At the spike all six waves agree; at a third of a turn they are six equally spaced points on the unit circle; at a quarter, use \(\bigl|F(\pi/2)\bigr| = \bigl|\sin(3\pi/2)\bigr|/\sin(\pi/4)\). (b) The average is \(\frac{1}{36}\sum_{j=0}^{35} F(2\pi j/36)^{2} e^{-2\pi i n j/36}\) — but for verifying, enumerate the pairs. (c) Read the census off the law, or the law off the census. (d) \(\bigl|F\bigr|^2 = F\overline{F}\): integrating kills every pair except \(m_1 = m_2\) — six of them. For the fourth power, count ordered pairs of totals \((n, n')\), or square the census and add.

Worked

(a) \(\bigl|F(0)\bigr| = 6\) (all waves agree); at a third of a turn the six waves are the six sixth-roots of unity, summing to exactly \(0\); at a quarter of a turn, \(\bigl|\sin(3\pi/2)/\sin(\pi/4)\bigr| = 1/(\sqrt{2}/2) = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\) — the landscape's quarter-turn height, as drawn. (b) Direct enumeration: totals \(2\) through \(12\) give \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1\), and the machine-run \(36\)-point average returns each to the integer — the average is the count. (c) At \(n=7\): \(6 - 0 = 6\) ✓; at \(n=3\): \(6-4 = 2\) ✓; at \(n=12\): \(6-5 = 1\) ✓; the law and the census agree at every total. (d) \(\int_0^1 \bigl|F\bigr|^{2}\,\frac{d\theta}{2\pi} = 6\): the six diagonal pairs \(m_1 = m_2\). For the fourth power, \(\sum_n N(n)^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36 + 25 + 16 + 9 + 4 + 1 = 146\) — machine-run, and the trapezoid read at \(720\) steps agrees to \(10^{-6}\). The quiet bound: \(14.93 \times 6 \approx 89.6\) of fourth-power energy allowed to the quiet stretch, against a true total of \(146\) — honest but loose, the two-variable disease of Exemplar 2 already visible in the energy ledger.

Exercise 2 · The exactness threshold ●●○
Three dice, \(s = 3\), \(M = 6\): the census support is \(3 \le n \le 18\). (a) Show the \(15\)-point grid average reads \(N(18) = 2\), and explain the \(2\) as two honest counts colliding. (b) Show the \(16\)-point average is exact at all sixteen totals. (c) State the general rule: for \(s\)-tuples from \(\{1, \ldots, M\}\), which \(K\) make the grid average exact for every total, and why does the answer involve the support's width and not the polynomial's degree?

Hint

(a) On a \(15\)-point grid, angles differing by a full turn are the same sample: \(e^{-2\pi i \cdot 18 j/15} = e^{-2\pi i \cdot 3 j/15}\). (b) Collision needs two support values differing by a multiple of \(K\). (c) The support has \(sM - s + 1\) values... count them.

Worked

(a) Machine-run: the \(15\)-point average at \(n=18\) is \(2.000\). The samples cannot tell \(n=18\) from \(n=3\) (\(18 - 3 = 15\), one full wrap), so the average returns \(N(3) + N(18) = 1 + 1 = 2\) — two honest counts, one address. (b) \(K = 16\): two support values collide only if they differ by \(16\) — but the support \(3..18\) has width \(15\), so no collision exists; the machine-run is exact at all sixteen totals (and so is every \(K > 16\)). (c) The grid average is exact for every total exactly when no two support values differ by a multiple of \(K\) — for a contiguous support of width \(w = sM - s + 1\) (here \(18 - 3 + 1 = 16\)), that is \(K \ge w\). The polynomial's degree (\(sM = 18\)) overstates the need: what collides is counts, not monomials, and the top of the support is what forces the width.

Exercise 3 · Four dice, the trend confirmed ●●●
(a) Enumerate the four-dice census (totals \(4\) through \(24\)) and verify the peak: \(146\) at \(n = 14\), out of \(1296\) quadruples. (b) The loud stretch (\(\bigl|\theta\bigr| \le \pi/6\)) integrates to \(138.079\) at the peak: compute the quiet share and compare with the three-dice share (\(1.957\) of \(27\)). (c) The Gaussian read puts the four-dice peak at \(1296/\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 35/3}\): evaluate, compare with \(146\), and reconcile the direction of the error with Exemplar 1's \(29.13\) against \(27\).

Hint

(a) \(6^{4} = 1296\) quadruples; symmetry halves the work. (b) Shares: \(7.921/146\) against \(1.957/27\). (c) Both Gaussian peaks sit high; ask what the Gaussian model leaves out.

Worked

(a) Machine enumeration: \(1, 4, 10, 20, 35, 56, 80, 104, 125, 140, 146, 140, 125, 104, 80, 56, 35, 20, 10, 4, 1\), summing to \(1296\) ✓, peak \(146\) at \(n=14\) ✓. (b) Quiet share \(146 - 138.079 = 7.921\), which is \(5.43\%\), against \(1.957/27 = 7.25\%\) at three dice: the quiet stretch lost a quarter of its relative weight for one added factor. (c) \(1296/\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 35/3} \approx 151.37\): high by \(3.7\%\), the same direction as Exemplar 1's \(29.13\) versus \(27\) (\(7.9\%\)) — and smaller. The Gaussian is the loud stretch's local model; it cannot see the quiet stretch's corrections, which here subtract. More factors, smaller relative correction: the same trend as (b), seen through the local model's overshoot.

Exercise 4 · Referee the ledger ◆
A student, having read Exemplar 1, writes: "The loud stretch contributes \(25.043\) and the truth is \(27\), so the quiet stretch contributes exactly \(1.957\) — and since contributions are positive, the quiet stretch always helps." Referee the claim: state precisely what is right, what is wrong, and exhibit the machine number that convicts the error.

Worked

Right: the identity — the count \(\bigl(27\bigr)\) is exactly the loud integral plus the quiet integral, so at \(n = 10\) the quiet share is indeed \(1.957\). Wrong: "contributions are positive" and "always helps." The split is a ledger of signed corrections, not a partition: at \(n = 3\) the loud stretch integrates to \(1.164\) against the true count \(1\) — the quiet share is \(-0.164\), negative. The loud stretch's local model spills past the true count at the edges of the support, and the quiet stretch's job there is cancellation, not addition. The convicting number is \(1.164 > 1\): one machine run, already in the page. The student has also quietly assumed the shares are constant in \(n\); they are not — \(7.25\%\) at the peak, negative at the tails — and the method's error terms exist precisely because the shares must be bounded, not trusted.

Exercise 5 · The descent, chased ◐
Extend the three-squares census to \(100\) (machine enumeration over \(0 \le a \le b \le c\)). (a) List the failures and verify each is of the form \(4^{a}(8b+7)\). (b) Take one failure divisible by \(4\) — \(28\) or \(60\) or \(92\) — and chase the descent: why does \(n = 4m\) fail exactly when \(m\) does? (c) Explore honestly: the census says \(47\) fails and \(46\) succeeds — what does the mod-\(8\) wall say about \(46\), and can you find its three squares by hand?

Hint

(a) There are fifteen failures to \(100\); check each against \(4^{a}(8b+7)\) by peeling factors of \(4\). (b) If \(n = 4m = x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\), what does mod \(4\) force about \(x, y, z\)? (c) \(46 \equiv 6 \pmod 8\) — the wall is silent; try \(36 + 9 + 1\).

Worked

(a) Machine census: the failures to \(100\) are \(7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71, 79, 87, 92, 95\) — fifteen, and peeling factors of \(4\) from each leaves a number \(\equiv 7 \pmod 8\) in every case, machine-checked. (b) If \(4m = x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\), then mod \(4\) each square is \(0\) or \(1\), and three of them sum to \(0\) mod \(4\) only as \(0+0+0\) — so \(x, y, z\) are all even, and \(m = (x/2)^{2} + (y/2)^{2} + (z/2)^{2}\): a representation of \(4m\) is exactly a representation of \(m\), so they fail together. That is the descent: \(28 = 4\cdot 7\) fails because \(7\) does; \(92 = 4 \cdot 23\) because \(23\) does. (c) The wall is a one-way obstruction: \(46 \equiv 6 \pmod 8\) is not of the forbidden form, and the census confirms it succeeds — \(46 = 36 + 9 + 1 = 6^{2} + 3^{2} + 1^{2}\). The legend's full content is the converse (every non-\(4^{a}(8b+7)\) number succeeds), which the census to \(100\) detects and Gauss proved; this page's machinery explains the obstruction, and honestly leaves the converse's proof to the lineage shelf.
