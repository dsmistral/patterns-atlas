Tier 4 · Specialized Moves

P40 · Tropicalize

Pass a polynomial or exponential system through a high-base logarithm so multiplication becomes addition, addition becomes maximum, and curved algebraic varieties skeletonize into balanced piecewise-linear polyhedra.

Prerequisites: P16 · Same problem, different clothes, P17 · Flip to the dual, P30 · Localize at a prime

On-ramp 1 — The tropical semiring (max-plus and min-plus arithmetic). In ordinary arithmetic on positive numbers, the logarithm \(\log_t(x \cdot y) = \log_t x + \log_t y\) turns multiplication into addition. What does \(\log_t(x + y)\) do when the base \(t\) grows huge (\(t \to \infty\))? Write \(x = t^a\) and \(y = t^b\) with \(a \ge b\): then

\[ \log_t\bigl(t^a + t^b\bigr) \;=\; \log_t\Bigl(t^a \bigl(1 + t^{b-a}\bigr)\Bigr) \;=\; a + \frac{\ln(1 + t^{b-a})}{\ln t} \;\xrightarrow{t \to \infty}\; a \;=\; \max(a, b). \]

The max-plus tropical semiring \((\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \oplus, \odot)\) replaces classical addition and multiplication with

\[ a \oplus b \;:=\; \max(a, b), \qquad a \odot b \;:=\; a + b, \]

where \(-\infty\) is the additive identity (\(\max(a, -\infty) = a\)) and \(0\) is the multiplicative identity (\(a + 0 = a\)). Replacing \(\max\) by \(\min\) (via \(x = t^{-a}\) as \(t \to \infty\) or \(p\)-adic valuations) gives the isomorphic min-plus tropical semiring \((\mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}, \min, +)\).

On-ramp 2 — Tropical polynomials and the corner locus. A classical polynomial \(f(x) = \sum_{i=0}^d c_i x^i\) becomes, under \(x = t^X\) and \(c_i \sim t^{a_i}\), a tropical polynomial

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(f)(X) \;:=\; \bigoplus_{i=0}^d \bigl(a_i \odot X^{\odot i}\bigr) \;=\; \max_{0 \le i \le d} \bigl(a_i + i X\bigr), \]

which is the upper envelope of finitely many straight lines \(Y = a_i + i X\) — a convex, piecewise-linear function! In tropical arithmetic there is no subtraction, so what is a "root" of \(\operatorname{Trop}(f)\)? In the classical polynomial \(f(t^X) = \sum_{i=0}^d \pm t^{a_i + i X} = 0\), if one term \(t^{a_k + k X}\) has a strictly larger exponent than every other term, it dominates as \(t \to \infty\) and cannot be cancelled to zero. Cancellation to zero can happen only where at least two linear pieces tie for the maximum! The set of points \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) where \(\max_{\mathbf{i}} (a_{\mathbf{i}} + \mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{X})\) is achieved by at least two monomials is the corner locus (or tropical hypersurface) \(\mathcal{T}(f)\).

On-ramp 3 — Newton polytopes and Legendre-dual subdivisions. For a multivariate polynomial \(f(x, y) = \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{A}} c_{i,j} x^i y^j\), the Newton polygon \(\operatorname{Newt}(f) = \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{A}) \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) is the convex hull of the exponent pairs \((i,j)\) that appear in \(f\). Lifting each lattice point \((i,j)\) to height \(a_{i,j}\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and projecting the upper facets of the 3D convex hull back to \(\mathbb{R}^2\) partitions \(\operatorname{Newt}(f)\) into smaller polygons called its regular subdivision. Each vertex of the tropical curve \(\mathcal{T}(f)\) corresponds to a 2D cell (triangle/polygon) of this subdivision, and each ray or edge of \(\mathcal{T}(f)\) is perpendicular to an edge of the subdivision!

1 · Before we begin

Three earlier patterns in the Atlas converge inside tropicalization:

  • In P16 · Same problem, different clothes, we saw how an isomorphism translates a hard structure into an easier one — whether turning Number Scrabble into Tic-Tac-Toe via the \(3 \times 3\) magic square, or using \(\ln : (0, \infty) \to \mathbb{R}\) to turn multiplication \(x \cdot y\) into addition \(\ln x + \ln y\). Classical logarithms, however, stall on addition \(\ln(x + y)\). Tropicalization completes the dictionary by sending \(\log_t \to \infty\), turning addition into \(\max\).
  • In P17 · Flip to the dual, we saw Legendre–Fenchel duality exchange a convex curve with the family of its supporting tangent lines \(X \mapsto \max_i (a_i + i X)\). In tropical geometry, the corner locus of \(\max_i (a_i + i X)\) is literally the Legendre dual of the lifted Newton polygon!
  • In P30 · Localize at a prime, we used the \(p\)-adic valuation \(\nu_p(n)\), which satisfies \(\nu_p(a \cdot b) = \nu_p(a) + \nu_p(b)\) and the ultrametric inequality \(\nu_p(a + b) \ge \min(\nu_p(a), \nu_p(b))\) — with strict equality whenever \(\nu_p(a) \neq \nu_p(b)\). Negating the valuation (\(X = -\nu_p(x)\)) produces the exact max-plus tropical laws without even taking a limit.

2 · Meet the problem: the log-paper limit of a quadratic

Consider the one-parameter family of classical quadratic equations indexed by a large positive parameter \(t > 1\):

\[ P_t(x) \;=\; t^0 x^2 \,-\, t^3 x \,+\, t^4 \;=\; x^2 - t^3 x + t^4 \;=\; 0. \]

By the quadratic formula, the two exact classical roots are

\[ x_{\pm}(t) \;=\; \frac{t^3 \pm \sqrt{t^6 - 4 t^4}}{2} \;=\; \frac{t^3 \bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4 t^{-2}}\bigr)}{2}. \]

Let us evaluate the base-\(t\) logarithms \(X_-(t) = \log_t x_-(t)\) and \(X_+(t) = \log_t x_+(t)\) as \(t\) grows from \(10\) to \(1000\):

Base parameter \(t\) Smaller root \(x_-(t)\) Valuation \(\log_t x_-(t)\) Larger root \(x_+(t)\) Valuation \(\log_t x_+(t)\)
\(t = 10\) \(10.102051\) \(1.00440958\) \(989.897949\) \(2.99559042\)
\(t = 100\) \(100.010002\) \(1.00002172\) \(999899.989998\) \(2.99997828\)
\(t = 1000\) \(1000.001000\) \(1.00000014\) \(999998999.999\) \(2.99999986\)
Limit \(t \to \infty\) \(x_-(t) \sim t^1\) \(X_- = 1\) \(x_+(t) \sim t^3\) \(X_+ = 3\)

Why do the logarithmic roots freeze onto the exact integers \(X = 1\) and \(X = 3\)? Substitute \(x = t^X\) into the three monomials of \(P_t(x)\):

\[ \text{Term } i=2:\; t^{2X + 0}, \qquad \text{Term } i=1:\; -t^{X + 3}, \qquad \text{Term } i=0:\; t^{0\cdot X + 4}. \]

On a base-\(t\) logarithmic scale as \(t \to \infty\), the dominant exponent of \(P_t(t^X)\) is governed by the tropical polynomial

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(P)(X) \;=\; (0 \odot X^{\odot 2}) \oplus (3 \odot X) \oplus 4 \;=\; \max\bigl(2X,\; X + 3,\; 4\bigr). \]

Follow the three linear pieces as \(X\) sweeps from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\):

  • For \(X < 1\): \(4 > X + 3 > 2X\), so the constant term \(t^4\) is strictly larger than the other two terms by a factor of \(t^{1-X} \to \infty\). No root can exist here!
  • At \(X = 1\) (First Corner Root): The constant exponent \(4\) and the linear exponent \(X + 3 = 4\) tie for the maximum (\(4 = 4 > 2\)), allowing \(-t^{X+3} + t^4 = 0\) to cancel at \(x \sim t^1\).
  • For \(1 < X < 3\): \(X + 3\) strictly dominates both \(4\) and \(2X\). Again, no root can exist.
  • At \(X = 3\) (Second Corner Root): The linear exponent \(X + 3 = 6\) and the quadratic exponent \(2X = 6\) tie for the maximum (\(6 = 6 > 4\)), allowing \(x^2 - t^3 x = 0\) to cancel at \(x \sim t^3\).

Without ever computing a square root, piecewise-linear tie-breaking found the exact asymptotic exponents \(X = 1\) and \(X = 3\)!

3 · Name the move

Box P40 · The Tropicalize Move

When faced with a polynomial system, algebraic curve, intersection count, or exponential dynamic program whose asymptotic exponents, valuations, or combinatorial degree invariants are obscured by nonlinear algebra, pass to the tropical limit (\(X_k = \log_t |x_k|\) as \(t \to \infty\), or \(X_k = -\nu(x_k)\) for a non-Archimedean valuation \(\nu\)):

\[ \sum_{\mathbf{i} \in \mathcal{A}} c_{\mathbf{i}}(t)\, \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}} \;=\; 0 \quad\xrightarrow{\;\text{Tropicalize}\;}\quad \operatorname{Trop}(f)(\mathbf{X}) \;:=\; \max_{\mathbf{i} \in \mathcal{A}} \bigl(a_{\mathbf{i}} + \mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{X}\bigr) \;\text{ achieves its maximum at least twice,} \]

where \(a_{\mathbf{i}} = \lim_{t \to \infty} \log_t |c_{\mathbf{i}}(t)|\). Smooth algebraic varieties collapse onto balanced, piecewise-linear polyhedral complexes \(\mathcal{T}(f) \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) whose vertices, edge lengths, and intersection multiplicities are read directly from the Legendre-dual subdivision of the Newton polytope \(\operatorname{Newt}(f) = \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{A})\).

Box P40.A · The Four Verification Obligations of Tropicalization

  1. Valuation & Semiring Convention: State explicitly whether you are working in the max-plus semiring \((\mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}, \max, +)\) via \(X = \log_t |x|\) (\(t \to \infty\)) or \(X = -\nu(x)\), or in the min-plus semiring \((\mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}, \min, +)\) via \(X = +\nu(x)\). Record the tropical coefficient \(a_{\mathbf{i}}\) of each monomial \(\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}}\).
  2. Newton Polytope & Dual Regular Subdivision: Form the exponent support \(\mathcal{A} = \{\mathbf{i} : c_{\mathbf{i}} \neq 0\} \subset \mathbb{Z}^n\) and its convex hull \(\operatorname{Newt}(f) = \operatorname{conv}(\mathcal{A})\). Project the upper facets of \(\operatorname{conv}\{(\mathbf{i}, a_{\mathbf{i}})\}\) (for \(\max\)) or lower facets (for \(\min\)) to obtain the regular subdivision \(\Delta_a\) of \(\operatorname{Newt}(f)\).
  3. Corner Locus & Integer Lattice Multiplicities: Each edge/facet \(e\) of the tropical hypersurface \(\mathcal{T}(f)\) is dual (orthogonal) to an edge \(e^* = \overline{\mathbf{i}\,\mathbf{j}}\) of \(\Delta_a\), and carries the integer lattice multiplicity \(w_e = \gcd(i_1 - j_1, \dots, i_n - j_n) = |\mathbb{Z}^n \cap e^*| - 1\).
  4. Zero-Tension Balancing & Transversality Check: At every vertex \(V\) of \(\mathcal{T}(f)\), verify the zero-tension balancing condition \(\sum_{e \ni V} w_e \mathbf{v}_e = \mathbf{0}\), where \(\mathbf{v}_e \in \mathbb{Z}^n\) is the primitive integer direction vector of edge \(e\) pointing away from \(V\). Before lifting tropical intersections back to classical solutions (Kapranov's or Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem), confirm that no cancellation of leading coefficients occurs and that intersections are transverse.

Box P40.B · Three-Key Recognition Diagnosis

  • Claim type: You need the asymptotic exponents (Puiseux valuations) of solutions to a polynomial system as a parameter \(t \to \infty\) or \(t \to 0\), an intersection count / genus / curve count in enumerative algebraic geometry, or the exact optimum of an exponential-sum / dynamic-programming recursion.
  • Problem shape: Expressions built from positive (or non-Archimedean valued) variables using sums and products \(\sum c_{\mathbf{i}} \mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{i}}\), where taking a logarithm turns every monomial into an affine linear functional \(a_{\mathbf{i}} + \mathbf{i} \cdot \mathbf{X}\).
  • Tools at hand: Piecewise-linear convex analysis (P17), Newton polygons and mixed volumes, determinant multiplicities \(|\det(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})|\), and tropical matrix multiplication \((\min, +)\) or \((\max, +)\).

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 (Algebraic Geometry & Balancing) · The smooth tropical conic, its Newton subdivision, and why zero-tension balancing holds

Problem. Consider the bivariate tropical quadratic polynomial in the max-plus semiring:

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(C)(X, Y) \;=\; \max\bigl(2X,\; X + Y + 3,\; 2Y,\; X + 4,\; Y + 4,\; 4\bigr). \]
  1. Find all vertices of the tropical curve \(\mathcal{T}(C) \subset \mathbb{R}^2\) (points where at least three linear pieces tie for the maximum) and match each vertex to its dual triangle in the regular subdivision of the Newton triangle \(\operatorname{Newt}(C) = \operatorname{conv}\{(0,0), (2,0), (0,2)\}\).
  2. Find the direction vector and weight of every bounded edge and infinite ray of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\), and verify the zero-tension balancing condition \(\sum_{e \ni V} w_e \mathbf{v}_e = \mathbf{0}\) at every vertex.

Solution.

Step 1 (Exponent heights and the four dual triangles). The six monomials \((i,j)\) with \(i + j \le 2\) have tropical coefficients (heights)

\[ a_{0,0} = 4, \quad a_{1,0} = 4, \quad a_{0,1} = 4, \quad a_{1,1} = 3, \quad a_{2,0} = 0, \quad a_{0,2} = 0. \]

Because the three interior/midpoint heights \(a_{1,0} = 4\), \(a_{0,1} = 4\), \(a_{1,1} = 3\) sit well above the average of the corners \(a_{2,0} = 0\) and \(a_{0,2} = 0\), the upper convex hull of \(\{(i, j, a_{i,j})\}\) subdivides the \(2 \times 2\) Newton triangle into four unit lattice triangles \(V_1^*, V_2^*, V_3^*, V_4^*\) (shown in Fig P40.2, right). Setting the three linear pieces of each triangle equal gives the four vertices of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\):

  • Vertex \(V_1\) (dual to \(V_1^* = \{(0,0), (1,0), (0,1)\}\)): Setting \(4 = X + 4 = Y + 4\) gives \((X, Y) = (0, 0)\) with common value \(4\). Checking the other three terms at \((0,0)\) gives \(X+Y+3 = 3 < 4\) and \(2X = 2Y = 0 < 4\), so the tie is strictly maximal!
  • Vertex \(V_2\) (dual to \(V_2^* = \{(1,0), (0,1), (1,1)\}\)): Setting \(X + 4 = Y + 4 = X + Y + 3\) gives \((X, Y) = (1, 1)\) with value \(5\) (exceeding \(a_{0,0} = 4\) and \(2X = 2Y = 2\)).
  • Vertex \(V_3\) (dual to \(V_3^* = \{(1,0), (1,1), (2,0)\}\)): Setting \(X + 4 = X + Y + 3 = 2X\) gives \(X = 4\) and \(Y = 1\) with value \(8\) (exceeding \(4, 5, 2\)).
  • Vertex \(V_4\) (dual to \(V_4^* = \{(0,1), (1,1), (0,2)\}\)): By symmetry, setting \(Y + 4 = X + Y + 3 = 2Y\) gives \((X, Y) = (1, 4)\) with value \(8\).

Step 2 (Edges, rays, and zero-tension balancing). Why does every vertex of a tropical curve satisfy \(\sum_{e \ni V} w_e \mathbf{v}_e = \mathbf{0}\)? Along an edge \(e\) leaving vertex \(V\), two monomials \((i_1, j_1)\) and \((i_2, j_2)\) of the active Newton triangle remain tied while the third drops below them:

\[ a_{i_1, j_1} + i_1 X + j_1 Y \;=\; a_{i_2, j_2} + i_2 X + j_2 Y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (i_2 - i_1)\,\Delta X + (j_2 - j_1)\,\Delta Y \;=\; 0. \]

Thus the weighted outgoing direction vector \(w_e \mathbf{v}_e\) is obtained by rotating the Newton polygon edge vector \(\mathbf{e}^* = (i_2 - i_1, j_2 - j_1)\) by \(90^\circ\): specifically, \(w_e \mathbf{v}_e = \bigl(-(j_2 - j_1),\, i_2 - i_1\bigr)\). Because the three edges of any closed triangle \(V^*\) sum to zero around its boundary (\(\sum_{k=1}^3 \mathbf{e}_k^* = \mathbf{0}\)), rotating by \(90^\circ\) proves that the outgoing rays at \(V\) automatically sum to \(\mathbf{0}\)! Checking each vertex of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) explicitly (all edges have lattice length \(w_e = 1\)):

  • At \(V_1 = (0,0)\): two infinite rays \((-1, 0)\) and \((0, -1)\) plus the bounded edge to \(V_2\) along \((1, 1)\) sum to \((-1,0) + (0,-1) + (1,1) = (0,0)\).
  • At \(V_2 = (1,1)\): three bounded edges to \(V_1, V_3, V_4\) in directions \((-1,-1)\), \((1,0)\), and \((0,1)\) sum to \((-1,-1) + (1,0) + (0,1) = (0,0)\).
  • At \(V_3 = (4,1)\): bounded edge \((-1,0)\) to \(V_2\) plus infinite rays \((0,-1)\) and \((1,1)\) sum to \((-1,0) + (0,-1) + (1,1) = (0,0)\).
  • At \(V_4 = (1,4)\): bounded edge \((0,-1)\) to \(V_2\) plus infinite rays \((-1,0)\) and \((1,1)\) sum to \((0,-1) + (-1,0) + (1,1) = (0,0)\).

Exemplar 2 (Enumerative Geometry) · Tropical Bézout's theorem and determinant intersection multiplicities

Problem. Intersect the smooth tropical conic \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) of Exemplar 1 (degree \(2\)) with the tropical line

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(L)(X, Y) \;=\; \max\bigl(X + 1,\; Y + 2.5,\; 3\bigr) \qquad (\text{degree } 1). \]

Find all intersection points \(P \in \mathcal{T}(C) \cap \mathcal{T}(L)\), compute the local tropical intersection multiplicity \(\operatorname{mult}_P(\mathcal{T}(C) \cap \mathcal{T}(L)) = w_e w_{e'} |\det(\mathbf{u}_e, \mathbf{v}_{e'})|\) at each point, and verify that the total intersection count equals \(\deg(C) \cdot \deg(L) = 2\).

Solution.

Step 1 (Skeleton of the tropical line \(\mathcal{T}(L)\)). Setting the three linear pieces of \(\operatorname{Trop}(L)\) equal gives \(X + 1 = Y + 2.5 = 3\), so \(\mathcal{T}(L)\) has its unique vertex at \(W = (2, 0.5)\) and three weight-\(1\) rays leaving \(W\):

  • West ray \(R_W\): direction \(\mathbf{v}_W = (-1, 0)\), equation \(Y = 0.5\) for \(X \le 2\).
  • South ray \(R_S\): direction \(\mathbf{v}_S = (0, -1)\), equation \(X = 2\) for \(Y \le 0.5\).
  • Diagonal ray \(R_D\): direction \(\mathbf{v}_D = (1, 1)\), equation \(Y - 0.5 = X - 2 \iff Y = X - 1.5\) for \(X \ge 2\).

Step 2 (Locating the transverse intersections and determinant multiplicities). Inspecting the segments of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) from Exemplar 1 against the three rays of \(\mathcal{T}(L)\) (shown in Fig P40.3, left):

  • Intersection \(P_1 = (0.5, 0.5)\): The West ray \(R_W\) (\(Y = 0.5, X \le 2\), direction \(\mathbf{v}_W = (-1, 0)\)) crosses the bounded diagonal segment \(\overline{V_1 V_2}\) of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) (\(Y = X\) for \(0 \le X \le 1\), direction \(\mathbf{u}_{12} = (1, 1)\)) at \(P_1 = (0.5, 0.5)\). Its tropical intersection multiplicity is the lattice parallelogram area \[ \operatorname{mult}_{P_1}\bigl(\mathcal{T}(C) \cap \mathcal{T}(L)\bigr) \;=\; 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \left|\det\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\right| \;=\; |0 - (-1)| \;=\; 1. \]
  • Intersection \(P_2 = (2.5, 1.0)\): The Diagonal ray \(R_D\) (\(Y = X - 1.5, X \ge 2\), direction \(\mathbf{v}_D = (1, 1)\)) crosses the horizontal bounded segment \(\overline{V_2 V_3}\) of \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) (\(Y = 1\) for \(1 \le X \le 4\), direction \(\mathbf{u}_{23} = (1, 0)\)) at \(Y = 1 \implies X = 2.5\), so \(P_2 = (2.5, 1.0)\). Its tropical intersection multiplicity is \[ \operatorname{mult}_{P_2}\bigl(\mathcal{T}(C) \cap \mathcal{T}(L)\bigr) \;=\; 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \left|\det\!\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right| \;=\; |1 - 0| \;=\; 1. \]
  • The South ray \(R_S\) (\(X = 2, Y \le 0.5\)) travels vertically downward in the open region between \(X = 0\) and \(X = 4\) where \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) has no edges below \(Y = 1\).

Summing over all intersections gives \(\sum_P \operatorname{mult}_P(\mathcal{T}(C) \cap \mathcal{T}(L)) = 1 + 1 = 2 = \deg(C)\deg(L)\), confirming Tropical Bézout's Theorem!

Exemplar 3 (Combinatorial Optimization) · All-pairs shortest paths via min-plus tropical matrix multiplication

Problem. Consider a 3-node directed communication network with direct link latencies given by the min-plus matrix (\(W_{ii} = 0\), \(W_{ij} = +\infty\) if no direct link exists):

\[ W \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 7 & 12 \\ +\infty & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & +\infty & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

In the min-plus tropical semiring \((\mathbb{R} \cup \{+\infty\}, \oplus, \odot)\) where \(a \oplus b = \min(a, b)\) and \(a \odot b = a + b\), compute the tropical matrix square \(W^{\odot 2} = W \odot W\) and cube \(W^{\odot 3}\), and interpret the stabilized matrix \(W^* = W^{\odot 2}\).

Solution.

Step 1 (Why tropical matrix multiplication computes shortest paths). Translating the classical matrix product \((A B)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n A_{ik} B_{kj}\) into the min-plus semiring yields

\[ (A \odot B)_{ij} \;=\; \bigoplus_{k=1}^n \bigl(A_{ik} \odot B_{kj}\bigr) \;=\; \min_{1 \le k \le n} \bigl(A_{ik} + B_{kj}\bigr). \]

Because \(W_{ii} = 0\), the entry \((W^{\odot m})_{ij}\) is the minimum total weight of any directed walk from node \(i\) to node \(j\) using at most \(m\) edges!

Step 2 (Explicit calculation of \(W^{\odot 2}\) and stabilization at \(m = n - 1 = 2\)). Computing each entry \((W^{\odot 2})_{ij} = \min_{k \in \{1,2,3\}} (W_{ik} + W_{kj})\):

  • \((W^{\odot 2})_{13} = \min(0 + 12,\; 7 + 3,\; 12 + 0) = \min(12, 10, 12) = 10\) (the 2-hop path \(1 \to 2 \to 3\) of cost \(7 + 3 = 10\) beats the direct edge \(1 \to 3\) of cost \(12\)).
  • \((W^{\odot 2})_{21} = \min(+\infty + 0,\; 0 + \infty,\; 3 + 2) = 5\) (via \(2 \to 3 \to 1\)).
  • \((W^{\odot 2})_{32} = \min(2 + 7,\; +\infty + 0,\; 0 + \infty) = 9\) (via \(3 \to 1 \to 2\)).

Collecting all nine entries gives

\[ W^{\odot 2} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 7 & 10 \\ 5 & 0 & 3 \\ 2 & 9 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \;=\; W^{\odot 3} \;=\; W^*. \]

Because every simple path in a 3-node graph with nonnegative cycle weights uses at most \(n - 1 = 2\) edges, the tropical Kleene star \(W^* = I_{\text{trop}} \oplus W \oplus W^{\odot 2} \oplus \cdots\) stabilizes at \(W^{\odot 2}\), solving the all-pairs shortest path problem by pure linear algebra over \((\min, +)\).

5 · When the trick breaks

Exhibit A — Leading-coefficient cancellation destroys the naive tropicalization when polynomials are not in general position. Consider the two linear equations \(f_1(x, y) = x + y + 1 = 0\) and \(f_2(x, y) = x + y + 2 = 0\). In \(\mathbb{C}^2\), these are parallel lines with zero intersection points! Yet both tropicalize to the exact same tropical line \(\max(X, Y, 0)\). Worse still, if we change \(f_2\) to \(x + y + (1 + t^{-3}) = 0\), subtracting \(f_1\) from \(f_2\) cancels \(x + y + 1\) completely and leaves \(t^{-3} = 0\).

Mitigation: Replace a single polynomial pair by a tropical basis of the ideal \(I = \langle f_1, f_2 \rangle\) (P32) — i.e., perform elimination to expose hidden cancellations before tropicalizing — or perturb coefficients generically when computing topological intersection invariants.

Price: Computing Gröbner bases over valued fields to detect cancellation.

Default: Check Obligation 4 in Box P40.A: tropical intersection equals classical intersection of individual hypersurfaces only when they meet transversely (or via stable intersection).

Exhibit B — Forgetting lattice edge weights \(w_e = \gcd(\Delta i, \Delta j)\) undercounts intersections and violates balancing. Consider \(\operatorname{Trop}(f)(X, Y) = \max(2X, 2Y, 0)\) (shown in Fig P40.3, right). Geometrically, its corner locus has the exact same three rays \((-1,0), (0,-1), (1,1)\) as a tropical line! If you forget that the Newton polygon edges connect \((0,0), (2,0), (0,2)\) with lattice length \(w_e = \gcd(2,0) = 2\), a tropical line will appear to intersect this conic in only \(1\) point instead of \(2\).

Mitigation: Always compute \(w_e = \gcd(i_2 - i_1, j_2 - j_1)\) from the dual Newton subdivision (Obligation 3 of Box P40.A).

Price: Tracking integer weights alongside the polyhedral skeleton.

Default: Never draw a tropical curve without labeling any edge weight \(w_e > 1\).

Exhibit C — Tropical addition is idempotent (\(a \oplus a = a\)), so additive inverses do not exist. In the max-plus semiring, the equation \(5 \oplus X = 3\), i.e., \(\max(5, X) = 3\), has no solution \(X \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{-\infty\}\). Consequently, tropical matrices do not have additive inverses, and standard Gaussian elimination by row subtraction is impossible.

Mitigation: Replace \((I - A)^{-1}\) by the positive Kleene star series \(A^* = I \oplus A \oplus A^{\odot 2} \oplus \cdots \oplus A^{\odot(n-1)}\) (when cycle weights are nonnegative in min-plus), or use tropical Cramer's rule / Hungarian bipartite matching for tropical linear systems.

Price: Checking the no-negative-cycle condition before iterating \(A^*\).

Default: Solve tropical linear recurrences via shortest-path / dynamic-programming operators rather than subtraction.

Honest scope of this chapter. For a single hypersurface \(V(f) \subset (\mathbb{C}^*)^n\) over an algebraically closed non-Archimedean field \(K\) (such as the field of Puiseux series \(\mathbb{C}\{\!\{t\}\!\}\)), Kapranov's Theorem (2000) guarantees that the valuation image \(\operatorname{val}(V(f))\) coincides set-theoretically and with multiplicities with the corner locus \(\mathcal{T}(f)\). For higher-codimension varieties \(V(I)\) defined by multiple equations \(f_1 = \cdots = f_r = 0\), \(\operatorname{val}(V(I)) = \bigcap_{f \in I} \mathcal{T}(f)\) requires intersecting over a finite tropical basis of the ideal \(I\), and lifting tropical curves to count algebraic curves of genus \(g\) requires Mikhalkin's Correspondence Theorem (2005).

7 · Lineage interlude

From Newton's Puiseux polygons to Imre Simon's tropical semiring and Mikhalkin's curve counts (1676 – 2005).

  • Isaac Newton (1676) & Victor Puiseux (1850): In his epistola posterior to Oldenburg, Newton introduced the "Newton ruler" — plotting exponent pairs \((i, j)\) of a polynomial \(f(x, y) = 0\) and laying a straightedge against the lower convex hull to read off the leading fractional exponent \(y \sim c x^\alpha\) of an algebraic branch. Puiseux proved in 1850 that every algebraic curve admits fractional power-series expansions classified by these slopes.
  • George Bergman (1971) & Robert Bieri–John Groves (1984): Studying the logarithmic image \(\operatorname{Log}_t(V) = \{(\log_t |x_1|, \dots, \log_t |x_n|)\}\) of an algebraic variety \(V \subset (\mathbb{C}^*)^n\) (the amoeba of Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky, 1994), Bergman proved that as \(t \to \infty\) the amoeba shrinks onto a piecewise-linear polyhedral spine determined by non-Archimedean valuations.
  • Imre Simon (1978–1988): Why is this geometry called "tropical"? The Hungarian-born Brazilian mathematician and computer scientist Imre Simon at the University of São Paulo pioneered the min-plus semiring \((\mathbb{N} \cup \{+\infty\}, \min, +)\) to solve decidability problems in finite automata. French mathematicians (beginning with Dominique Perrin and Jean-Éric Pin) coined the name tropical semiring in honor of Simon's work in Brazil.
  • Victor Maslov (1987) & Oleg Viro (1983): Simultaneously, the physicist Victor Maslov observed that sending Planck's constant \(\hbar \to 0\) via \(S \mapsto \hbar \ln(e^{S_1/\hbar} + e^{S_2/\hbar}) \to \max(S_1, S_2)\) ("Maslov dequantization") turns the linear Schrödinger equation into the piecewise-linear Hamilton–Jacobi equation, while Oleg Viro used dual subdivisions of Newton polytopes ("patchworking") to construct real algebraic curves with prescribed topology (Hilbert's 16th problem).
  • Mikhail Kapranov (2000), Grigory Mikhalkin (2005) & Diane Maclagan–Bernd Sturmfels (2015): Kapranov proved the fundamental correspondence identifying non-Archimedean hypersurfaces with tropical corner loci; Mikhalkin proved that Gromov–Witten invariants of the projective plane can be computed by counting weighted planar tropical curves; and Maclagan and Sturmfels unified the algebraic foundations via initial ideals and Gröbner complexes.

Exercise P40.1 (●○○ — Finding tropical roots and multiplicities from a lifted Newton polygon). Consider the family of classical cubic equations for large \(t > 1\):

\[ P_t(x) \;=\; x^3 \,-\, t^4 x^2 \,+\, t^5 x \,-\, t^6 \;=\; 0, \]

whose max-plus tropical polynomial is \(\operatorname{Trop}(P)(X) = \max\bigl(3X + 0,\; 2X + 4,\; X + 5,\; 6\bigr)\).

  1. Plot the four exponent-height pairs \((i, a_i) \in \{(0, 6), (1, 5), (2, 4), (3, 0)\}\) and find the facets (line segments) of the upper convex hull.
  2. Read off the tropical roots \(X\) from the negative slopes of those upper-hull facets, and determine the multiplicity of each tropical root from the horizontal lattice length \(\Delta i\) of its facet. Verify that the multiplicities sum to \(\deg(P) = 3\).
  3. Factor \(P_t(x)\) or check the classical roots as \(t \to \infty\) to explain why two classical roots share the same asymptotic valuation.
Hint

Notice that the first three lifted points \((0, 6), (1, 5), (2, 4)\) are collinear with slope \(\frac{4 - 6}{2 - 0} = -1\)! A collinear segment of horizontal span \(\Delta i = 2\) gives a tropical root of multiplicity \(2\).

Worked answer

1. Upper convex hull of the lifted Newton polygon. Evaluating the slopes between consecutive points \((0, 6), (1, 5), (2, 4), (3, 0)\):

  • From \(i = 0\) to \(i = 1\): slope \(= 5 - 6 = -1\).
  • From \(i = 1\) to \(i = 2\): slope \(= 4 - 5 = -1\) (collinear with the first segment!).
  • From \(i = 2\) to \(i = 3\): slope \(= 0 - 4 = -4\).

Thus the upper convex hull has exactly two facets: Facet 1 connecting \((0, 6)\) to \((2, 4)\) (passing through \((1, 5)\)) with slope \(-1\) and horizontal length \(\Delta i_1 = 2 - 0 = 2\); and Facet 2 connecting \((2, 4)\) to \((3, 0)\) with slope \(-4\) and horizontal length \(\Delta i_2 = 3 - 2 = 1\).

2. Tropical roots and multiplicities.

  • Facet 1 has slope \(-1 \implies\) tropical root \(X_1 = 1\) with multiplicity \(\Delta i_1 = 2\) (at \(X = 1\), the three pieces \(6 = X + 5 = 2X + 4 = 6\) all tie for the maximum!).
  • Facet 2 has slope \(-4 \implies\) tropical root \(X_2 = 4\) with multiplicity \(\Delta i_2 = 1\) (at \(X = 4\), \(2X + 4 = 3X = 12 > 9, 6\)).

The sum of multiplicities is \(2 + 1 = 3 = \deg(P)\).

3. Classical verification. Setting \(x = t^1 u\) in \(P_t(x) = 0\) gives \(t^3 u^3 - t^6 u^2 + t^6 u - t^6 = 0 \implies -t^6(u^2 - u + 1) + O(t^3) = 0\), which has two complex roots \(u \to e^{\pm i\pi/3}\) both of modulus \(|u| = 1\), so two classical roots satisfy \(|x| \sim t^1\) (\(\log_t |x| \to 1\)). Setting \(x = t^4 v\) gives \(t^{12}(v^3 - v^2) + O(t^9) = 0 \implies v \to 1\), giving the third root \(x \sim t^4\) (\(\log_t |x| \to 4\)).

Exercise P40.2 (●●○ — Why lattice edge weights are essential for zero-tension balancing). Consider the bivariate tropical polynomial

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(f)(X, Y) \;=\; \max\bigl(2X + 0,\; Y + 3,\; 4\bigr). \]
  1. Find the unique vertex \(V = (X_0, Y_0) \in \mathbb{R}^2\) where all three linear pieces tie, and write down the equations and primitive integer outgoing direction vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \mathbf{v}_3\) of the three rays leaving \(V\).
  2. Show that the unweighted sum \(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3\) is not zero! Then compute the integer lattice weights \(w_e = \gcd(\Delta i, \Delta j)\) from the three edges of the Newton triangle \(\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0), (2,0), (0,1)\}\) and verify that \(\sum_{k=1}^3 w_k \mathbf{v}_k = (0, 0)\).
Hint

Set \(2X = Y + 3 = 4\) to find \(V = (2, 1)\). For the ray where \(2X = 4 \ge Y + 3\), the dual Newton edge connects \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\), which has lattice length \(\gcd(2 - 0, 0 - 0) = 2\).

Worked answer

1. Vertex and primitive outgoing ray directions. Setting \(2X = Y + 3 = 4\) gives \(V = (2, 1)\). The three rays leaving \(V = (2, 1)\) correspond to pairwise ties that beat the third term:

  • Ray \(e_1\) (\(2X = 4 \ge Y + 3\)): Here \(X = 2\) and \(Y \le 1\), pointing straight down with primitive integer vector \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (0, -1)\).
  • Ray \(e_2\) (\(Y + 3 = 4 \ge 2X\)): Here \(Y = 1\) and \(X \le 2\), pointing straight left with primitive integer vector \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (-1, 0)\).
  • Ray \(e_3\) (\(2X = Y + 3 \ge 4\)): Here \(Y - 1 = 2(X - 2)\) for \(X \ge 2\), pointing along the primitive integer vector \(\mathbf{v}_3 = (1, 2)\).

2. Lattice weights and zero-tension balancing. Without weights, \(\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3 = (0, -1) + (-1, 0) + (1, 2) = (0, 1) \neq (0, 0)\)!

Now inspect the three boundary edges of the Newton triangle \(\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0), (2,0), (0,1)\}\):

  • Dual to \(e_1\) (\(2X = 4\)) is the segment from \((0,0)\) to \((2,0)\), with lattice weight \(w_1 = \gcd(2 - 0, 0 - 0) = 2\).
  • Dual to \(e_2\) (\(Y + 3 = 4\)) is the segment from \((0,0)\) to \((0,1)\), with lattice weight \(w_2 = \gcd(0 - 0, 1 - 0) = 1\).
  • Dual to \(e_3\) (\(2X = Y + 3\)) is the segment from \((2,0)\) to \((0,1)\), with lattice weight \(w_3 = \gcd(-2, 1) = 1\).

Forming the weighted tension sum at \(V = (2, 1)\) yields

\[ w_1 \mathbf{v}_1 + w_2 \mathbf{v}_2 + w_3 \mathbf{v}_3 \;=\; 2(0, -1) + 1(-1, 0) + 1(1, 2) \;=\; (0, -2) + (-1, 0) + (1, 2) \;=\; (0, 0). \]

Exercise P40.3 (●●● — The tropical determinant, optimal assignment, and tropical singularity). For an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A = (A_{ij})\) over the max-plus semiring, the tropical determinant (or tropical permanent) drops alternating signs and replaces \((+, \times)\) with \((\max, +)\):

\[ \det\nolimits_{\text{trop}}(A) \;:=\; \bigoplus_{\sigma \in S_n} \bigl(A_{1, \sigma(1)} \odot \cdots \odot A_{n, \sigma(n)}\bigr) \;=\; \max_{\sigma \in S_n} \sum_{i=1}^n A_{i, \sigma(i)}. \]

A matrix \(A\) is called tropically singular if the maximum in \(\det_{\text{trop}}(A)\) is achieved by at least two distinct permutations \(\sigma \in S_n\).

  1. Compute \(\det_{\text{trop}}(A)\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 4 & 1 \\ 5 & 9 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 & 7 \end{pmatrix}\) by evaluating all \(3! = 6\) permutations, and determine whether \(A\) is tropically singular or non-singular. What is the leading asymptotic power of \(t\) in the classical determinant \(\det\bigl(t^{A_{ij}}\bigr)\) as \(t \to \infty\)?
  2. Show that \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 5 & 1 \\ 6 & 6 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\) is tropically singular, and explain how this reflects the classical singularity of \(\det\bigl(t^{B_{ij}}\bigr) = 0\).
Hint

Sum the entries along each of the 6 permutation diagonals. For \(B\), compare the identity permutation \((1)(2)(3)\) against the transposition \((1\,2)(3)\).

Worked answer

1. Evaluating \(\det_{\text{trop}}(A)\) across all 6 permutations.

  • \(\sigma = (1, 2, 3)\): \(A_{11} + A_{22} + A_{33} = 8 + 9 + 7 = 24\).
  • \(\sigma = (1, 3, 2)\): \(A_{11} + A_{23} + A_{32} = 8 + 3 + 6 = 17\).
  • \(\sigma = (2, 1, 3)\): \(A_{12} + A_{21} + A_{33} = 4 + 5 + 7 = 16\).
  • \(\sigma = (2, 3, 1)\): \(A_{12} + A_{23} + A_{31} = 4 + 3 + 2 = 9\).
  • \(\sigma = (3, 1, 2)\): \(A_{13} + A_{21} + A_{32} = 1 + 5 + 6 = 12\).
  • \(\sigma = (3, 2, 1)\): \(A_{13} + A_{22} + A_{31} = 1 + 9 + 2 = 12\).

Thus \(\det_{\text{trop}}(A) = 24\), achieved uniquely by the identity permutation \(\sigma = (1, 2, 3)\). Hence \(A\) is tropically non-singular, and in the classical determinant \(\det(t^{A_{ij}}) = t^{24} - t^{17} - t^{16} + \cdots \sim t^{24}\), the leading term \(t^{24}\) cannot be cancelled!

2. Tropical singularity of \(B\). For \(B\), the identity permutation \(\sigma_1 = (1, 2, 3)\) gives \(B_{11} + B_{22} + B_{33} = 5 + 6 + 4 = 15\), while the transposition \(\sigma_2 = (2, 1, 3)\) also gives \(B_{12} + B_{21} + B_{33} = 5 + 6 + 4 = 15\) (all other permutations give \(\le 8\)). Because the maximum \(15\) is achieved twice, \(B\) is tropically singular — matching the fact that columns 1 and 2 of \(t^{B_{ij}}\) are identical, so \(\det(t^{B_{ij}}) = t^{15} - t^{15} = 0\) cancels!Notice also that finding \(\det_{\text{trop}}(A)\) is the classical linear assignment problem (maximum-weight bipartite matching).

Exercise P40.4 (◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis). Consider the nonlinear system of two equations in two positive / complex variables \((x, y)\) depending on a large parameter \(t \to \infty\):

\[ \begin{cases} x y^2 \,-\, t^3 x \,+\, t^4 \;=\; 0, \\[4pt] x^2 y \,-\, t^3 y \,+\, t^4 \;=\; 0. \end{cases} \]

Without solving a degree-4 elimination polynomial by radicals, determine the exact number of solutions \((x(t), y(t)) \in (\mathbb{C}^*)^2\) (counted with multiplicity) and find the asymptotic scaling \(x(t) \sim t^X,\; y(t) \sim t^Y\) of every solution as \(t \to \infty\).

Worked answer

1. Pattern diagnosis and tropicalization (P40 · Tropicalize). Both equations are sums of monomials with powers of \(t\) as coefficients. Substituting \(x = t^X\) and \(y = t^Y\) as \(t \to \infty\) converts the system into the intersection of two tropical curves \(\mathcal{T}(f_1) \cap \mathcal{T}(f_2)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\):

\[ \operatorname{Trop}(f_1)(X, Y) \;=\; \max\bigl(X + 2Y,\; X + 3,\; 4\bigr), \qquad \operatorname{Trop}(f_2)(X, Y) \;=\; \max\bigl(2X + Y,\; Y + 3,\; 4\bigr). \]

2. Skeletons of \(\mathcal{T}(f_1)\) and \(\mathcal{T}(f_2)\).

  • For \(\mathcal{T}(f_1)\), all three terms tie when \(X + 2Y = X + 3 = 4 \implies V_1 = (1,\; 1.5)\). The three rays leaving \(V_1 = (1, 1.5)\) are:
    • Ray \(A_1\) (\(X + 3 = 4 \ge X + 2Y\)): \(X = 1,\; Y \le 1.5\), primitive direction \(\mathbf{u}_1 = (0, -1)\), weight \(w = 1\).
    • Ray \(B_1\) (\(X + 2Y = X + 3 \ge 4\)): \(Y = 1.5,\; X \ge 1\), primitive direction \(\mathbf{u}_2 = (1, 0)\), weight \(w = 2\) (since the Newton edge from \((1,2)\) to \((1,0)\) has \(\gcd(0, 2) = 2\)!).
    • Ray \(C_1\) (\(X + 2Y = 4 \ge X + 3\)): \(X + 2Y = 4\) for \(Y \ge 1.5\) (\(X \le 1\)), primitive direction \(\mathbf{u}_3 = (-2, 1)\), weight \(w = 1\). Check balancing at \(V_1\): \(1(0,-1) + 2(1,0) + 1(-2,1) = (0,0)\).
  • By \(X \leftrightarrow Y\) symmetry, \(\mathcal{T}(f_2)\) has its vertex at \(V_2 = (1.5,\; 1)\) and three rays:
    • Ray \(A_2\): \(Y = 1,\; X \le 1.5\), direction \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (-1, 0)\), weight \(1\).
    • Ray \(B_2\): \(X = 1.5,\; Y \ge 1\), direction \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1)\), weight \(2\).
    • Ray \(C_2\): \(2X + Y = 4\) for \(X \ge 1.5\) (\(Y \le 1\)), direction \(\mathbf{v}_3 = (1, -2)\), weight \(1\).

3. Intersections and determinant multiplicities. Where do the rays of \(\mathcal{T}(f_1)\) and \(\mathcal{T}(f_2)\) cross?

  • Crossing 1 at \(P_1 = (1, 1)\): Ray \(A_1\) (\(X = 1, Y \le 1.5\), direction \((0,-1)\), weight \(1\)) crosses Ray \(A_2\) (\(Y = 1, X \le 1.5\), direction \((-1,0)\), weight \(1\)) at \(P_1 = (1, 1)\) with multiplicity \(1 \cdot 1 \cdot |\det\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}| = 1\). Thus \(1\) classical solution scales as \(x \sim t^1,\; y \sim t^1\).
  • Crossing 2 at \(P_2 = (1.5, 1.5)\): Ray \(B_1\) (\(Y = 1.5, X \ge 1\), direction \((1,0)\), weight \(2\)) crosses Ray \(B_2\) (\(X = 1.5, Y \ge 1\), direction \((0,1)\), weight \(2\)) at \(P_2 = (1.5, 1.5)\) with multiplicity \(2 \cdot 2 \cdot |\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}| = 4\)! Thus \(4\) classical solutions scale as \(x \sim t^{3/2},\; y \sim t^{3/2}\) (specifically \(y^2 \approx t^3 \implies y \approx \pm t^{3/2}\) and \(x^2 \approx t^3 \implies x \approx \pm t^{3/2}\), giving all 4 sign combinations \((+t^{3/2}, +t^{3/2}), (+t^{3/2}, -t^{3/2}), (-t^{3/2}, +t^{3/2}), (-t^{3/2}, -t^{3/2})\)!).

In total there are \(1 + 4 = 5\) solutions in \((\mathbb{C}^*)^2\) — matching Bernstein's mixed volume \(MV(\operatorname{Newt}(f_1), \operatorname{Newt}(f_2)) = 5\).

Exercise P40.5 (◐ — Exploration: Mikhalkin's Tropical Curve Counting and Gromov–Witten invariants). A classical question in enumerative geometry asks: how many rational curves (genus \(g = 0\)) of degree \(d\) in \(\mathbb{CP}^2\) pass through \(3d - 1\) general points? For \(d = 1\) (lines through \(2\) points) the answer is \(N_1 = 1\); for \(d = 2\) (conics through \(5\) points) \(N_2 = 1\); for \(d = 3\) (nodal cubics through \(8\) points) \(N_3 = 12\). In 2005, Grigory Mikhalkin proved that \(N_d\) equals the number of balanced planar tropical curves \(\Gamma\) of degree \(d\) passing through \(3d - 1\) general points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), provided each tropical curve \(\Gamma\) is counted with its Mikhalkin multiplicity:

\[ \operatorname{mult}(\Gamma) \;=\; \prod_{V \in \operatorname{Vert}(\Gamma)} \operatorname{mult}(V), \qquad \text{where } \operatorname{mult}(V) \;=\; \bigl|\det(w_{e_1} \mathbf{v}_{e_1},\; w_{e_2} \mathbf{v}_{e_2})\bigr| \;=\; 2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(V^*). \]

Verify that for our smooth tropical conic \(\mathcal{T}(C)\) in Exemplar 1 (Fig P40.2), every dual triangle \(V_1^*, V_2^*, V_3^*, V_4^*\) has Euclidean area \(\frac{1}{2}\), and compute the Mikhalkin multiplicity \(\operatorname{mult}(\mathcal{T}(C))\). When can a 3-valent tropical vertex have \(\operatorname{mult}(V) > 1\)?

Hint

Use \(2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(V_k^*) = 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1\) for each of the four unit lattice triangles in Fig P40.2. For \(\operatorname{mult}(V) > 1\), inspect the triangle in Exercise P40.2 with vertices \((0,0), (2,0), (0,1)\).

Worked answer

1. Mikhalkin multiplicity of the smooth tropical conic. In Fig P40.2 (right), the \(2 \times 2\) Newton triangle of total Euclidean area \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \cdot 2 = 2\) is subdivided into \(4\) unit lattice triangles \(V_1^*, V_2^*, V_3^*, V_4^*\), each of area \(\frac{1}{2}\). Thus at every vertex \(V_k\) (\(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\)),

\[ \operatorname{mult}(V_k) \;=\; 2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(V_k^*) \;=\; 2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \;=\; 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{mult}\bigl(\mathcal{T}(C)\bigr) \;=\; 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \;=\; 1. \]

Since a unique tropical conic passes through \(3(2) - 1 = 5\) generic points in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and has Mikhalkin multiplicity \(1\), Mikhalkin's theorem immediately gives \(N_2 = 1\)!

2. When \(\operatorname{mult}(V) > 1\). Whenever a 3-valent vertex \(V\) is dual to a lattice triangle \(V^*\) of Euclidean area \(A > \frac{1}{2}\) (by Pick's theorem, any lattice triangle containing interior lattice points or boundary edges with \(w_e = \gcd(\Delta i, \Delta j) > 1\)), it contributes \(\operatorname{mult}(V) = 2A > 1\) classical curves! For example, the vertex \(V = (2, 1)\) in Exercise P40.2 is dual to \(\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0), (2,0), (0,1)\}\) of area \(1\), so \(\operatorname{mult}(V) = |\det\bigl((0,-2), (-1,0)\bigr)| = 2 = 2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(V^*)\), corresponding to \(2\) classical branches differing by a sign choice \(\pm\).
