The Patterns Atlas · P02 · the second move of the spine

Experiment with small cases

Compute the first few cases honestly — the pattern is hiding in the table.

New word: conjecture. A conjecture is a precise mathematical guess: a statement you believe is true but have not yet proved. Every conjecture is on lease — a proof converts the lease into a deed, and a single counterexample burns the lease. This page is the book's main conjecture factory, so the word arrives on page two.

1 · Before we begin

P01 gave you the pinning checklist and the four claim types (Box P01.B). Pinning tells you what must be shown. It deliberately says nothing about what the answer looks like — and for compute-type claims, that is the real difficulty: nobody tells you the formula before you have earned it.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Staring at the general problem rarely produces formulas; they do not arrive by pure thought. The good news: if the problem has a whole-number size parameter n — cuts, people, sides, terms — then the smallest instances are computable by hand, cheaply, right now. The table of the first few cases is the fastest discovery engine you own. The bad news: tables lie. You watched it happen already — on P01, the polynomial n² + n + 41 produced forty primes in a row and then died at n = 40. So this page teaches two things at once: how to read a small-case table, and how much to trust what it says.

2 · Meet the problem

How many pieces can you cut a circular pancake into, using at most n straight cuts?

Do the small cases yourself, honestly, before reading on. Zero cuts: one piece. One cut: two. Two cuts, crossing: four. Three cuts — take a moment and draw it; the best you can do is seven, because the third cut can cross both earlier cuts, and each crossing turns one cutting segment into a fresh split.

So the table begins:

Now stare at the pieces row. If a formula is hiding in there, it is not obvious — 1, 2, 4, 7, 11, 16 does not sing. The answers refuse to speak. So do the thing this page exists to teach: subtract each row from the next.

The table just talked. The differences are exactly 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and there is a reason: cut number k crosses the k − 1 cuts before it, is chopped into k segments, and each segment adds one piece. So the n-th pancake number is 1 + (1 + 2 + ⋯ + n), which closes to (n² + n + 2) ⁄ 2. Check it against a case you have not used yet: n = 10 gives (100 + 10 + 2) ⁄ 2 = 56. The lab confirmed the formula against the row-by-row construction for every n up to 100 — zero mismatches.

Notice what just happened. The formula was not produced by genius; it was produced by arithmetic on small cases plus one honest look at why the differences behave. That is a machine you can operate on purpose. It has a name, and it is the second move of the spine.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P02 — Experiment with small cases. When a problem carries a whole-number size parameter n (or an object that comes in sizes 1, 2, 3, …), compute the smallest instances by hand, completely and honestly; read the table — its differences first — before guessing; state every guess as a conjecture and test it against fresh cases before believing it.

Or, in words: don't stare at the tenth floor. Build the first three floors by hand and watch how each floor is added — the building's rule is in the adding.

Box P02.A — the experimenter's protocol.

  1. Pin first. Be sure what "the answer for n" counts (P01's checklist) — a vague count makes a vague table.
  2. Compute small cases honestly. n = 0, 1, 2, 3 — to the end, with the arithmetic shown. "It obviously continues" is a lie you tell yourself.
  3. Tabulate with differences. Two columns minimum: the values, and the gaps between them (Box P02.B).
  4. Conjecture out loud, in symbols. "The number of pieces is (n² + n + 2) ⁄ 2" — a sentence that can be true or false.
  5. Test on fresh cases. A conjecture born from n ≤ 5 must face n = 10, or n = 100. If the fresh cases cost real work, choose informative ones, spread far apart.
  6. Label it what it is. Verified for the tested cases only — a conjecture until a proof tool (P66) or a stress test (P03) says otherwise.

Box P02.B — when the answers refuse to speak, subtract. The values themselves often hide their pattern; the differences between consecutive values expose it. If the differences don't speak either, subtract again: constant second differences are the signature of a quadratic formula in n (the pancake's n² term announces itself exactly this way). The algebra that makes differencing a real tool — sums that rebuild a quantity from its differences — arrives later as P69 · Telescoping and its bridge page B11 · the discrete derivative (both planned). Until then, differencing is a reading device, and an honest one.

When does P02 apply? Three-key diagnosis: the claim type is compute or find a formula (and, with the honesty rules of §5, a discovery aid for prove-for-all claims); the problem shape is a family indexed by a size n whose small members are computable by hand; the tools needed are arithmetic, a pencil, and the patience for six honest rows. Like P01, this move is available before you know any advanced mathematics — but unlike P01, it does real creative work: it is how formulas are found before anyone knows them.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. In each, the table does the discovering.

Example 1 (numbers) — the sum of the first n odd numbers

Add the odd numbers, cumulatively:

You know these numbers — 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 are squares. The conjecture practically proposes itself: the sum of the first n odd numbers is n². But a conjecture is not a reason, so we ask why, and the answer comes in the pattern of this very page:

  • By differences (P02's own reading device): the difference between consecutive squares is n² − (n − 1)² = 2n − 1 — exactly the n-th odd number. The squares are what you get by adding up the odds, so adding the odds rebuilds the squares.
  • By picture: build a 1×1 square; add an L-shaped strip of 3 cells to get a 2×2 square; add an L-strip of 5 cells to get 3×3; each new L is the next odd number. The picture is a proof — it shows the mechanism for every n at once.

The lab checked the conjecture exhaustively for every n up to 500 — zero mismatches (a detector, we remind you, not a proof; the picture is the proof). Moves used: P01 (pin "odd" as 2k + 1, "n-th odd" as 2n − 1), P02.

Example 2 (counting) — handshakes

At a party of n people, everyone shakes everyone's hand exactly once. How many handshakes?

Differences say: the n-th person to arrive shakes n − 1 hands. Conjecture: n(n − 1) ⁄ 2, and a fresh case agrees — 12 people manage 12 × 11 ⁄ 2 = 66 handshakes. Then the second, independent route: each of the n people reports n − 1 handshakes, so the total count of reported hands is n(n − 1) — but each handshake has two hands in it, so divide by 2. Two different experiments, one answer (when that happens, something structural is going on — P70 · Double counting will make it a move).

One honest aside: P01 counted 54 diagonals in a 12-sided polygon — which is 66 minus 12, the same "count all pairs, subtract the special ones" shape wearing different clothes. P16 (planned) exists to harvest such kinships. Moves used: P01, P02 — with a P70 cameo.

Example 3 (calculus) — a table of basic integrals

Compute ∫₀¹ xⁿ dx for n = 0, 1, 2, 3 — class-12 antiderivatives make this a one-liner each:

The conjecture is instant: the integral is 1 ⁄ (n + 1). And here we must be honest about what the experiment did and did not do. Class-12 already owns the power rule, which certifies the formula — the table was not needed for the proof. What the table trains is the reflex: when you meet a new integral and no rule is known, compute the small cases and read them. The lab cross-checked the table numerically (midpoint quadrature, two million steps) against 1/(n + 1) for n = 0…8 — agreement to five decimal places throughout, with the certificate coming from the antiderivative, not the quadrature. Moves used: P01, P02.

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

This is the section to read twice. P02 is the most honest move in the book precisely because it can fail so beautifully.

The classic lie: Moser's circle problem

Put n points on a circle and join every pair by a chord. Into how many regions do the chords cut the circle's interior?

1, 2, 4, 8, 16 — every instinct in your body now says 32. The actual count at n = 6 is 31. The lab computed it twice, by two independent routes (Euler's formula for the chord arrangement, and adding the chords one at a time, each chord with m crossings adding m + 1 regions), and both routes give 31, then 57, then 99. The true formula is C(n,2) + C(n,4) + 1. The doubling pattern held for five consecutive cases and then died — and note the deeper trap: even the differences doubled (1, 2, 4, 8, …) before producing a 15. Every reading device in Box P02.B would have lied to you here.

New word: general position. The Moser counts assume no three chords pass through one interior point — "general position". If three chords do meet inside, three crossing points collapse into one and the region count drops. The general-position count is the maximum; experiments (like tables) always need their assumptions pinned — that is P01's job, not P02's.

Failure shapes, honestly

  • Patterns lie (Moser's disease). A pattern confirmed by k small cases can still die at k + 1 — P01's Exercise 5 watched n² + n + 41 produce forty primes and then fail at n = 40. Mitigation: never let a table certify a prove-for-all claim (Box P01.B: one counterexample destroys a for-all claim, and only a universal argument completes it). Price: the conjecture must wait for a proof, and proofs take work. Default: a table is a lease, not a deed — conjecture freely, believe nothing without the certificate.
  • Trivial first rows. n = 0 and n = 1 are often degenerate (one piece, one handshake). Compute them anyway — they cost seconds and catch off-by-one errors cheaply — but don't let them suggest the pattern; nothing interesting has happened yet at n = 1.
  • Expensive cases. When each case takes an hour (a 100-vertex construction, a slow simulation), the temptation is to stop at three cases. Moser's disease thrives on small tables. Mitigation: choose informative cases — spread out (n = 1, 2, 5, 10), not adjacent. Default: six honest cases when cheap; when expensive, quality replaces quantity (the full discipline is P03's).
  • The table is right and the reading is wrong. Moser's table was honest — it was "doubling" that was the misread, an extrapolation the data never promised. The experimenter's humility cuts both ways: the data gets the benefit of the doubt, the reading does not.
Scope note. Experiment is a discovery engine and a detector — never a certificate. A small-case check can catch a wrong formula (it would have caught pancake errors instantly) and cannot prove a right one. The book is deliberate about this: every "verified for all n ≤ 500" in our labs is followed by "a detector, not a proof" for exactly this reason.

6 · What to try next

The experiment has produced a conjecture. Route it:

  • Conjecture in hand → P03 · Stress-test with special cases (planned): attack your own conjecture with adversarial cases — n = 0, n = 1, a big jump, a symmetric choice. Mitigation for the lying-pattern risk; price: minutes; default: after every conjecture, before any proof attempt.
  • Conjecture survived stress-testing → P66 · Prove by induction (planned): the for-all certificate, when the claim has a natural "next case". Price: the induction step is real work; the experiment was the cheap part.
  • The pattern lives in the differences → P69 · Telescoping and B11 · the discrete derivative (planned): the machinery that turns "sum of differences rebuilds the values" from a reading device into a proof tool.
  • Two independent experiments agree → P70 · Double counting (planned): the agreement itself is usually a theorem trying to get out.
  • Ahead on the ladder — P15 · Squeeze it (planned) builds on this page: trapping an answer between two computable bounds is experimentation with intent. And the multi-pattern problem M03 · Pick's theorem (planned) will let P02 and P09 work the same problem together.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. The move is old enough to be anonymous — every tradition that computed anything built tables first. It became a named phase of problem-solving in George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945), whose heuristic questions include precisely this: "Could you solve a simpler analogous problem first?" Number theory never outgrew it: the Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, now one of the Clay millennium problems, began as reams of numerical tables computed by Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer in the early 1960s. And the move has been institutionalized: Neil Sloane started collecting integer sequences in the mid-1960s to look up exactly these small-case tables, and the collection became the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences — the world's library of other people's first six cases. Moser's circle problem, the page's cautionary tale, is named for the mathematician Leo Moser. None of this is load-bearing; it says only that the professionals reach for the same pencil you do.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Use the pancake formula (n² + n + 2) ⁄ 2 to predict the number of pieces from 10 cuts, then say which cut is the first to add 11 pieces in one stroke — and how many pieces exist after 11 cuts.

Hint

Follow Box P02.A: compute the fresh case from the formula, then read the differences table (the k-th cut adds k).

Worked answer

For n = 10: (100 + 10 + 2) ⁄ 2 = 56. The differences are 1, 2, 3, …, so the cut that adds 11 pieces is the eleventh, and after 11 cuts there are 56 + 11 = 67 pieces (the formula agrees: (121 + 11 + 2) ⁄ 2 = 67). Machine-checked in the lab, both by formula and by the running sum.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Add the cubes, cumulatively: 1³, then 1³ + 2³, then 1³ + 2³ + 3³, up to five terms. Compare your table with the handshake numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15 (Example 2). What do you notice? Conjecture, then test on n = 6.

Hint

Don't compare the cube-sums to each other — compare them to a table you already own.

Worked answer

The cube-sums are 1, 9, 36, 100, 225 — and the handshake numbers squared are 1², 3², 6², 10², 15² = 1, 9, 36, 100, 225. The same numbers! Conjecture: 1³ + 2³ + ⋯ + n³ = (1 + 2 + ⋯ + n)². Fresh case: for n = 6, the sum is 441 and (21)² = 441 — it holds. The lab verified it for every n up to 200. We have not proved it: the honest status is a conjecture with 200 witnesses; the proof arrives with P66 (planned). This exercise is the whole page in miniature: two tables you own, compared, produce a theorem's shadow.

Exercise 3 ●●●

Build the table of digit counts of 2ⁿ for n = 1…12. Find the apparent pattern, then check it at n = 13 and n = 14. Finally: how many digits does 2¹⁰⁰ have?

Hint

Digit counts, not the numbers themselves. And when the pattern you found breaks, do not despair — compute the two awkward cases by hand (2¹³ and 2¹⁴ are small) and look for the honest rule underneath.

Worked answer

The digit counts are 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4 — a new digit every third n, or so it seems. But 2¹³ = 8192 (four digits) and 2¹⁴ = 16384 (five digits): the "every third n" reading is a small-case lie, exactly of Moser's kind. The honest rule uses class-12 logarithms: a positive whole number N has ⌊log₁₀ N⌋ + 1 digits (⌊x⌋ is the greatest integer ≤ x — the greatest-integer function [x] of school maths), and log₁₀ 2ⁿ = n log₁₀ 2 ≈ 0.30103 n, so the digit count of 2ⁿ is ⌊0.30103 n⌋ + 1. For n = 100: ⌊30.103⌋ + 1 = 31 digits. The lab certified this by computing 2¹⁰⁰ exactly (1,267,650,600,228,229,401,496,703,205,376 — count them: 31) and checked the log formula against every 2ⁿ for n = 1…100, zero mismatches. Note what saved us from the small-table trap: testing the reading at cases it did not cover.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the move

For each problem below, decide whether P02 is the first move to reach for. If it is not, say what to reach for instead. No hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

  1. Prove that √2 is irrational.
  2. Find the 100th term of the sequence 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, …
  3. Must two people in any group of 13 share a birth month?
Worked answer

(1) No. There is no size parameter to tabulate — the claim is a single prove-for-all statement. Pin it (P01); the move that proves it is P11 · Prove by contradiction (planned). A table has nothing to compute here.

(2) Yes. This is P02's home ground: differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, and the second difference is constant 2, so the terms follow n(n + 1); the 100th term is 100 × 101 = 10100 (the lab confirmed it two ways: by stepping the recurrence to the 100th term, and by the formula, agreeing exactly). Box P02.B is the tool; P66 turns the reading into a certificate.

(3) Partly — and the part matters. Experiment illuminates: 12 people can have 12 distinct months (the lab exhibits the witness: person i born in month i), so the boundary of the phenomenon is exactly 12 vs 13. But the certificate is not a table — it is P10 · Force a collision (planned): 13 people into 12 months forces a repeat. The experiment found where the phenomenon starts; the pigeonhole proves that it must occur. Knowing which job is yours is the diagnosis.

Exercise 5 ◐ Moser, by hand

Draw six well-spread points on a circle, join every pair, and count the regions honestly — expect 31, and expect to enjoy it less at attempt three. Then explore: (a) find the repair formula C(n,2) + C(n,4) + 1 by the incremental route — each chord adds one region, and each crossing of two chords adds one more; count the crossings by noticing that every four points produce exactly one crossing; (b) check it against the lab's two machine routes (both printed in the lab output); (c) and the question to sit with, no single answer: what made the doubling lie so convincing — five honest witnesses, a clean story, or the pattern in the differences? Compare with n² + n + 41's forty true cases. Write down your personal rule for when to stop trusting a table; P03 (planned) will hand you a sharper one.
