The Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity

Multiply two sums of two squares and the product is a sum of two squares again — usually in more than one way. Norms multiply.

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it opens the last bridge group — norms that multiply. You bring nothing but school squaring and adding; the small-case census habit will feel familiar from B01 and B02, and one worked aside will link back to a debt P05 left you. Take a pencil; you will find two answers to one question before any formula appears.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Two warm-up facts you can check in your head: 17 = 1² + 4², and 13 = 2² + 3². Both are sums of two squares. Now the pencil question: their product, 17 · 13 = 221 — is it a sum of two squares too?

Task one. Hunt: try 221 − 100 = 121 = 11². It lands: 221 = 10² + 11². Keep hunting, don't stop at the first hit: 221 − 25 = 196 = 14², so also 221 = 5² + 14². Two different answers — and that is stranger than one. The two factors had the property once each; the product seems to have it twice.

Task two. Same hunt on 65 = 5 · 13, with 5 = 1² + 2² and 13 = 2² + 3²: 65 = 4² + 7² (= 16 + 49) and also 1² + 8² (= 1 + 64). Again two.

Notice what is begging to be noticed: the property "is a sum of two squares" survives multiplication, and the number of representations seems to multiply along with it — one rep each in the factors, two in the product. This page proves the survival, exactly, with a formula you could have found yourself by insisting that the two answers of 221 are not a coincidence. It is called the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity, and it is about fourteen centuries old.

2 · The idea, named

Box B12 — the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity. For all whole numbers a, b, c, d:

(a² + b²)(c² + d²) = (ac − bd)² + (ad + bc)²

Or, in words: a product of two sums of two squares is itself a sum of two squares — and you can write the witness down directly from the four numbers you started with, by the recipe on the right: cross-multiply and subtract, cross-multiply and add.

Try it on Lecture 0's 221, with 17 = (a, b) = (1, 4) and 13 = (c, d) = (2, 3): (1·2 − 4·3)² + (1·3 + 4·2)² = (2 − 12)² + (3 + 8)² = (−10)² + 11² = 100 + 121 = 221. One of your two answers, produced instantly. Where is the other? Flip one sign:

Box B12.A — the other route. The same identity with a sign swapped:

(a² + b²)(c² + d²) = (ac + bd)² + (ad − bc)²

In words: the subtraction and the addition in the recipe can trade places. For 221: (2 + 12)² + (3 − 8)² = 14² + (−5)² = 196 + 25 = 221 — your second answer. The two representations you hunted for by hand are the two routes of the same identity.

New word: norm. Give the pair (a, b) its norm, written N(a, b) = a² + b² — think of it as "the size of the pair, squared". Then Box B12 says: N of the pair (ac − bd, ad + bc) equals N(a, b) · N(c, d). The recipe (ac − bd, ad + bc) is a multiplication for pairs, and the norm is not merely preserved by it — the norms literally multiply. That is this bridge group's name.

Examples and non-examples:

  • Yes: (1, 2) ⊙ (2, 3) = (2 − 6, 3 + 4) = (−4, 7), N = 16 + 49 = 65 = 5 · 13. A negative coordinate is fine; the norm only ever sees squares.
  • Yes: the other route gives (2 + 6, 3 − 4) = (8, −1), N = 64 + 1 = 65. Both answers of Lecture 0's second task, one formula.
  • No: there is no such rule for three squares. 3 = 1 + 1 + 1 and 5 = 4 + 1 + 0 are sums of three squares, but the product 15 is not a sum of three squares at all (the lab checks this exhaustively: no triple a² + b² + c² with a, b, c ≤ 3 reaches 15). The phenomenon is a special privilege of pairs — which is exactly why it is worth a page.
You have met this multiplication before. If you expand (a + bi)(c + di) — class-11 complex multiplication — you get (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i. The pair recipe of Box B12 is complex multiplication wearing integer clothes, and the norm N(a, b) is exactly ∣a + bi∣². So the identity says ∣z₁z₂∣² = ∣z₁∣² · ∣z₂∣². Hold that thought: it is the precise route P06 will take through this bridge. (The proof below does not use i — the complex reading explains why anyone would guess the formula; the expansion proves it.)

The name is honest double credit: Brahmagupta (India, 628 CE, Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta) stated the identity in a more general form we meet in section 4, and Fibonacci (Liber Quadratorum, 1225, "the book of squares") stated it in Europe while studying exactly this question — which numbers are sums of two squares. Diophantus had used special cases about a millennium before that. History as seasoning; the mathematics is proved below, not inherited.

3 · Worked by hand

Three examples, simplest first, each carried to the end.

(a) 65, and a complete census

5 = 1² + 2², 13 = 2² + 3², so route 1 of the identity promises 65 = (1·2 − 2·3)² + (1·3 + 2·2)² = (−4)² + 7² = 16 + 49, and route 2 promises 65 = (2 + 6)² + (3 − 4)² = 8² + (−1)² = 64 + 1. Both check. Now the census question — small cases first, always (P02): are there any OTHER representations? The lab scans every pair with 0 ≤ x ≤ y ≤ 8 and finds exactly two: (1, 8) and (4, 7). The formula produced both; there are no more. For a product of two one-representation primes, the two routes are the complete list.

(b) 4225 = 65², and four right triangles

4225 = 25 · 169 = (3² + 4²)(5² + 12²). Route 1: (15 − 48)² + (36 + 20)² = (−33)² + 56² = 1089 + 3136 = 4225. Route 2: (15 + 48)² + (36 − 20)² = 63² + 16² = 3969 + 256 = 4225. Now squint: 33² + 56² = 65² and 16² + 63² = 65² are both Pythagorean — the two routes of one product gave two right triangles with the same hypotenuse 65. The lab's full census of 4225 finds five representations with 0 ≤ x ≤ y: (0, 65), (16, 63), (25, 60), (33, 56), (39, 52) — four of them Pythagorean, four right triangles on hypotenuse 65. Where do the other three come from? Also from routes — the identity accepts trivial representations as inputs: 169 = 0² + 13² and 25 = 3² + 4², so route 1 of (0, 13) ⊙ (3, 4) = (0 − 52, 0 + 39) = (52, 39) ✓; 25 = 0² + 5² and 169 = 5² + 12², so route 1 of (0, 5) ⊙ (5, 12) = (−60, 25) ✓; and (0, 13) ⊙ (0, 5) = (−65, 0) ✓. Five census entries, five route outputs, all lab-verified.

(c) Representations multiply: 325 and 1105

325 = 5² · 13. The lab scans 0 ≤ x ≤ y ≤ 18 and finds exactly three representations: (1, 18), (6, 17), (10, 15) — 1 + 324, 36 + 289, 100 + 225, all equal to 325. One more prime factor, one more representation: 1105 = 5 · 13 · 17 has exactly four (scan 0..33): (4, 33), (9, 32), (12, 31), (23, 24). And the routes deliver them all. Take 65's two reps, (1, 8) and (4, 7), and compose with 17 = 1² + 4², i.e. (1, 4):

  • (1, 8) ⊙ (1, 4): route 1 gives (1 − 32, 4 + 8) = (−31, 12) → (12, 31); route 2 gives (1 + 32, 4 − 8) = (33, −4) → (4, 33).
  • (4, 7) ⊙ (1, 4): route 1 gives (4 − 28, 16 + 7) = (−24, 23) → (23, 24); route 2 gives (4 + 28, 16 − 7) = (32, 9) → (9, 32).

Four routes, four representations, complete list. The pattern taking shape — representation counts that grow with the factorization, produced by composing pairwise — is the seed of a classical and beautiful theory (which numbers are sums of two squares, M02, after P06 and P13). This page builds the engine; that page drives it.

4 · Why it is true

The proof is one honest expansion. Expand route 1:

(ac − bd)² + (ad + bc)² = a²c² − 2abcd + b²d² + a²d² + 2abcd + b²c²

The cross terms −2abcd and +2abcd cancel; what remains is a²c² + a²d² + b²c² + b²d², which is exactly (a² + b²)(c² + d²). Route 2 is the same expansion with the signs of the cross terms swapped — they cancel the same way. That is the whole proof, and it is complete. The lab does not take even an expansion on faith: it tests both routes exhaustively over every 0 ≤ a, b, c, d ≤ 12 (28,561 cases) and every −6 ≤ a, b, c, d ≤ 6 (28,561 more), all four quadrants included — zero mismatches. A small-case check is a detector, not a proof; here the proof is above and the detector confirms nothing was misread.

Brahmagupta's one level up. Replace the 1 in "two squares" by any N and the same expansion still works:

(x² + Ny²)(z² + Nw²) = (xz − Nyw)² + N(xw + yz)²

This is the form Brahmagupta actually wrote in 628 CE. The lab verifies it exhaustively for N = 1, 2, 3, 5 over 5,184 cases. One worked instance, N = 3: (1² + 3·1²)(2² + 3·1²) = 4 · 7 = 28, and (1·2 − 3·1·1)² + 3(1·1 + 1·2)² = (−1)² + 3·9 = 1 + 27 = 28. Keep this box in mind; it closes a debt two sentences after the next one.

The debt: P05's Pell recurrence, explained. In its failure catalogue, P05 studied the family s² − 5y² = 4 and verified — by machine — that the recurrence (s, y) → ((3s + 5y)/2, (s + 3y)/2) sends solutions to solutions: (3, 1) → (7, 3) → (18, 8) → (47, 21), the value 4 never budging. Verified, but unexplained. This page's idea explains it. Compose in Brahmagupta's form with N = 5 and the pair (3, 1): (s, y) ⊙ (3, 1) = (3s + 5y, s + 3y) — the recurrence's step, before halving. Composition multiplies the values (each = 4) by the value of (3, 1), which is 9 − 5 = 4: so the composed pair carries value 4 · 4 = 16; halving both coordinates divides the value by 4; result: 4 again. The lab checks the quadratic identity (3s + 5y)² − 5(s + 3y)² = 4(s² − 5y²) exhaustively over −5 ≤ s, y ≤ 5 and each witness step exactly: 3² − 5·1² = 4, 7² − 5·3² = 4, 18² − 5·8² = 4, 47² − 5·21² = 4. P05's recurrence is compose-with-(3,1), then halve. That is what "norms that multiply" buys: whole families of equations whose solutions compose without end.

Scope, honestly. What the identity does: it builds representations of a product from representations of its factors, in pairs of routes. What it does not do: (1) it does not tell you which numbers are sums of two squares in the first place — primes p ≡ 3 (mod 4) never are (Exercise 3 proves the easy obstruction; the converse for p ≡ 1 (mod 4) is Fermat's two-squares theorem, whose proof needs P13 — deferred to M02, named, not hidden). (2) It does not say that every representation of a product must come from its routes on some factorization — that is also true, and its proof needs the same deferred machinery (unique factorization of these very pairs). Default meanwhile: use the identity forward (build reps of products); ask the reverse question only after P13.

Exercise 1 ●○○

(a) Compute both routes of (1² + 2²)(2² + 3²) and verify each by direct squaring. (b) Compute both routes of (2² + 1²)(1² + 1²) — and say what surprised you about them.

Hint

Box B12 and Box B12.A, straight in. For (b), write down the pair each route gives before squaring.

Worked answer

(a) Route 1: (2 − 6)² + (3 + 4)² = 16 + 49 = 65 ✓. Route 2: (2 + 6)² + (3 − 4)² = 64 + 1 = 65 ✓. (b) 10 = (2·1 − 1·1)² + (2·1 + 1·1)² = 1 + 9, and route 2: (2 + 1)² + (2 − 1)² = 9 + 1 — the two routes gave the same pair {1, 3}, just written in the other order. Both routes are correct (10 = 1 + 9 either way); the coincidence happens because swapping the inner pair's coordinates and flipping a sign turns one route into the other here. Not luck-fearful: the lab confirms both routes land on norm 10 and the unordered pairs coincide. Lesson: count unordered representations (the (x, y)-vs-(y, x) convention of every census in section 3).

Exercise 2 ●●○

Verify that 1105 = 5 · 13 · 17 has exactly the four representations (4, 33), (9, 32), (12, 31), (23, 24) — then produce at least two of them yourself by routes, using 65's representations composed with (1, 4) for 17.

Hint

Section 3(c) did (1, 8) ⊙ (1, 4) and (4, 7) ⊙ (1, 4). Do any two of the four route computations yourself, and check each claimed square: 16 + 1089, 81 + 1024, 144 + 961, 529 + 576.

Worked answer

Each square check: 4² + 33² = 16 + 1089 = 1105 ✓, 9² + 32² = 81 + 1024 = 1105 ✓, 12² + 31² = 144 + 961 = 1105 ✓, 23² + 24² = 529 + 576 = 1105 ✓. Routes: (1, 8) ⊙ (1, 4) route 1 = (1 − 32, 4 + 8) = (−31, 12) → (12, 31); route 2 = (33, −4) → (4, 33). (4, 7) ⊙ (1, 4) route 1 = (−24, 23) → (23, 24); route 2 = (32, 9) → (9, 32). All four, by routes, matching the lab's exhaustive scan of 0 ≤ x ≤ y ≤ 33 (exactly four representations, none missed).

Exercise 3 ●●●

(a) Census the remainders that squares can leave on division by 4, and the remainders that sums of two squares can leave. (b) Conclude: no number ≡ 3 (mod 4) is a sum of two squares, and apply the conclusion to 2027 without any search. (c) Combine with Box B02.A to conclude: any product of sums of two squares is itself never ≡ 3 (mod 4).

Hint

(a) Only four classes exist — B01/B02 style, the census is complete after four squares: 0², 1², 2², 3². (c) B02's product preservation: the leftover of a product is the product of leftovers' leftover.

Worked answer

(a) Squares mod 4: 0² ≡ 0, 1² ≡ 1, 2² ≡ 4 ≡ 0, 3² ≡ 9 ≡ 1 — only {0, 1}. Sums of two of those: {0, 1, 2}. The class 3 is unreachable; by Box B02.A only classes matter, so the four-row census already covers all squares and sums thereof — the lab confirms the census and scans x = 0…45 for 2027 with zero hits. (b) 2027 = 4·506 + 3, so 2027 ≡ 3 (mod 4): not a sum of two squares, no hunt needed — the theorem saves the search. (c) A product of sums of two squares is a sum of two squares by Box B12, and sums of two squares never leave remainder 3 by (a) — the two theorems work as one machine: identity (this page) + class arithmetic (B02). This one-two punch is exactly how the deep story of M02 begins.

Exercise 4 ◐

Census the primes under 100: which are sums of two squares? Guess the rule, check your guess against every prime in your census, and say precisely what remains unproved.

What you should find

Sums of two squares: 2 = 1² + 1², 5 = 1² + 2², 13 = 2² + 3², 17 = 1² + 4², 29 = 2² + 5², 37 = 1² + 6², 41 = 4² + 5², 53 = 2² + 7², 61 = 5² + 6², 73 = 3² + 8², 89 = 5² + 8², 97 = 4² + 9² — and on the other side, every one of 3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31, 43, 47, 59, 67, 71, 79, 83 refuses, and each of those is ≡ 3 (mod 4). The rule you should guess: a prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is 2 or ≡ 1 (mod 4). Exercise 3 already proves the refusing half. What remains unproved — the deep half — is that every prime ≡ 1 (mod 4) is a sum of two squares: Fermat's two-squares theorem. Its proof needs infinite descent (P13), and the full story is M02's to tell, later in the book. The lab runs the entire census (25 primes, each scanned, every witness listed) so you can check your table against it.
