The Patterns Atlas · P48 · the third move of tier 5

The representable functor trick

Don't hunt the object; write its table of answers to every probe first. The table knows the object — and the hunt for a table's holder turns out to be where mathematics keeps its deepest problems.

New word: representable functor. A functor, for this page, is a table with laws: one row of answers per test object, plus an honest rule for how the answers restrict when a test object is replaced by a finer one. The table of an object \(X\) is the special one that answers each test \(Z\) with the set of structure-preserving maps \(Z \to X\), and restricts by composing those maps. A functor is representable when it is some object's table in this exact sense — not merely similar to one, but convertible to it by a dictionary that respects the restrictions. The heart of the page is that "is some object's table" is a property you can test, and a goal you can chase: write the table first, then hunt its holder.

1 · Before we begin

P31 taught the move this page pushes to its limit. Its closing door sentence promised exactly this page: "This page counted maps to tell objects apart; P48 upgrades the trick into an identification: an object is its map-counts, and every question about the object can be moved into a question about the counts." And its last exercise ended on the hinge: having counted maps \(\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\) under three different notions of "map", it asked does anything about the counting still depend on which maps are declared legal? — and answered that this page's first move is to make that fine print itself an object of study. This is that page.

The older habits are the scaffold. B05 taught that coordinates are readings off a measuring scheme; here the probe objects are the measuring scheme, and the table is the object's complete record of readings. P16 taught that a dictionary carrying the structure both ways makes two worlds one; here the dictionary is not built by hand but delivered by a theorem, and the two worlds are an object and its table. P25 taught the census discipline — when a claim says "always", build every small case and count; this page leans on it harder than any page so far, because the objects are small enough that every claim about them can be checked against a complete census. And P47, the sibling one rung below, ended its own page by promising that "the next move turns the questions themselves into the object."

Here is the tension. P31 counted maps into an object to tell objects apart — but it kept the objects primary and the counts as evidence. The extreme version of the move inverts that: keep only the answers, throw the objects away, and prove that nothing is lost. That proof is the Yoneda lemma, and it is the guarantee half of this page. The constructive half is bigger than P31 ever claimed: if you want an object you cannot yet build, you can write its table first — answers to every probe, honestly restricting — and then hunt for a holder. Sometimes the holder exists and the hunt is the construction. Sometimes the holder cannot exist, and the table's failure is the discovery. And sometimes, as §4 will show in a three-object world small enough to census completely, the tally of the answers is cheap and it is the table's fine print — the restrictions — that carries the object.

2 · Meet the problem

Try this one cold. A museum of mathematical objects has a strict policy: the cases are locked, and the registrar is not allowed to open them. The only instrument is a probe slot. You hand the case a test object; the case displays the set of structure-preserving ways your test fits into the case's contents. The tests come from a small, fully known world: the empty set \(0\), the one-point set \(1\), and the two-point set \(2\) — call it the coin, its two faces \(a\) and \(b\). A "way of fitting" is just a function from your test into the contents: there is exactly one way to fit anything into the one-point set, two ways to fit a point into the coin (point at \(a\), point at \(b\)), four ways to fit the coin into itself, and — this world being tiny — the registrar has counted every map between every pair of tests: eleven maps in all.

Three cases stand in the hall. The registrar's readings, one row of answers per test:

  • Case 1 answers \(1\), \(1\), \(1\) to the empty set, the point, the coin.
  • Case 2 answers \(1\), \(2\), \(4\).
  • Case 3 answers \(1\), \(0\), \(0\) — to the point and to the coin, it answers with the empty set of ways: nothing at all fits in. Only the empty question, "how do the contents fit into the empty set?", gets an answer, and it gets exactly one.

Three questions, and none of them can be answered by opening anything.

Q1 — the identification policy. Can the museum run its entire catalog on readings alone? If two cases answered every probe with exactly the same set of ways — not just the same number of ways, but ways that correspond under every change of test — could the holdings still differ in any way the probes could ever detect? If not, then "same readings" is a certified "same object", and the registrar never needs to open a case again. This is a faithfulness claim, and it wants a proof.

Q2 — the forger. A vendor arrives with a fourth case, already locked, and sells it to the museum on the strength of its readings: \(1\), \(1\), \(5\). "Five ways to fit the coin in, my friend — a rare piece." The registrar cannot open it. Is there a law that every honest case obeys — a law of the readings themselves, checkable without opening anything — that this case violates? And a harder version: a second vendor with readings exactly matching Case 2 — \(1\), \(2\), \(4\), the coin's own tally. If the tally alone cannot certify the contents, what in the readings can?

Q3 — the wanted poster. The curator dreams of an exhibit no case has held: the ways of fitting a test into two cases at once — a left-hand fit and a right-hand fit, counted as pairs. For the point paired with the coin, the readings come out \(1\), \(2\), \(4\): one way to fit the empty set, \(1 \times 2\) ways to fit a point, \(1 \times 4\) ways to fit the coin — and the museum already owns an object that produces those readings exactly. But for the coin paired with the coin, the readings come out \(1\), \(4\), \(16\) — and no case in the museum can produce them: honest cases answer the point-probe with \(0\), \(1\), or \(2\) ways, never \(4\). Is the curator's table a broken dream, or is the museum too small to hold its answer?

Everything in this page happens inside that little world, because the world is small enough that every claim above can be settled by a complete count rather than an appeal to authority — the census discipline of P25, applied to objects themselves. The answers, in short: same readings do force same contents (Q1, and the proof is worth a whole section); readings obey two laws every honest case satisfies, and both vendors violate them, the second one too — just not in the tally (Q2); and the curator's table is honest, the museum is too small, and the object that answers it exists exactly one world bigger (Q3). The general shape of all three answers is the single deepest move category theory ever taught.

3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P48 — The representable functor trick. Stop treating objects as things you must build before you can ask about them. For an object \(X\), its table is \(h_X(Z) = \mathrm{Hom}(Z, X)\) — the set of maps from each test object \(Z\) into \(X\) — with restriction along every map \(f: Z \to Z'\) of tests by pre-composition: \(h_X(f)\) sends a way \(g: Z' \to X\) to the way \(g \circ f: Z \to X\). (1) The table is honest data: it can be written down, compared cell by cell, and restricted without ever opening the case. (2) The guarantee (the Yoneda lemma): for any table-with-laws \(F\), the natural transformations \(h_X \to F\) correspond exactly to the elements of \(F(X)\); in particular \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_X, h_Y) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(X, Y)\) — tables compare exactly as objects do, so a dictionary of tables is a dictionary of objects, and nothing is lost by keeping only the tables. (3) The trick: to find or certify an object, write its table first — \(F(Z)\) = the answers an object of the wanted kind should give at probe \(Z\), with honest restrictions — then hunt the holder: an object \(R\) with \(h_R \cong F\). The hunt has three honest outcomes: the holder exists and the hunt is its construction; the holder is impossible and the obstruction is the theorem; or the holder exists only in a larger world, and the hunt tells you how far to grow.

Or, in words: an object is its answers-to-every-question, plus how those answers move when the question changes. Say the questions carefully and you have said the object — that is the guarantee. And you are allowed to run the logic backwards: write down the answers an object ought to give before you can build it, and then search the world for whoever can hold those answers. The search is not a formality; whether a table has a holder is, in the large, one of the deepest questions mathematics knows how to ask.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — probe with the whole family, through the family's own maps. The evidence for an object is not one reading but the full table, and the only allowed comparisons between readings are the restrictions along genuine maps of probes. Jam-mode: proof by single column — P31's Exhibit C already watched one probe count certify the wrong verdict; a tally without restrictions is not even evidence.

Key 2 — the table must be a genuine functor. Restrictions must compose honestly — restricting along \(g \circ f\) must equal restricting along \(f\) then along \(g\) — and every identity must restrict by the identity. Jam-mode: a table that tallies plausibly but restricts lawlessly passes the eye and fails every census; §4 will build ninety-six genuine tables with the coin's tally and still catch the fakes by their fine print.

Key 3 — representability is earned, not presumed. A table that wants a holder must obey the necessary laws — in this world: answer \(1\) to the empty question, and answer the coin with the square of the answer to the point — and even law-abiding is not sufficient: the census of §4 finds genuine, law-abiding tables that no object of the world holds. Jam-mode: the empty-question law refuses the empty case, the multiplying law refuses the five-answer vendor, and the whole world can be too small for an honest table — the curator's wanted poster.

Box P48.A — the proof, in miniature. Why does the guarantee hold? Two clauses, each one line at world scale. The element clause: a natural \(\alpha: h_X \to F\) is determined by the single answer \(s = \alpha_X(\mathrm{id}_X) \in F(X)\) it gives to the identity probe — because for any map \(g: Z \to X\), naturality along \(g\) forces \(\alpha_Z(g) = \alpha_Z(g \circ \mathrm{id}_X) = F(g)(\alpha_X(\mathrm{id}_X)) = F(g)(s)\): the whole transformation is "evaluate the table at the element". The bijection clause: conversely, every element \(s \in F(X)\) builds a natural \(\alpha\) by that same formula, and the two constructions are mutually inverse — so \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_X, F) \cong F(X)\), as sets, by a dictionary that never chose anything. This two-line argument is the Yoneda lemma; the exercises run it by hand.

Box P48.B — where the move appears. Elements are maps from the point: an element of a set is exactly a map from the one-point set — "point" is the name of the probe whose answers are elements. Subsets are maps into the coin: a subset of \(Z\) is exactly a \(0/1\)-valued function on \(Z\), so the subsets-of-\(Z\) table (restrict by preimage) answers every probe exactly as the coin does — §4's census catches the subsets table wearing the coin's tally, and proves the two tables are one. Products are pairing tables: \(\mathrm{Hom}(Z, X \times Y) \cong \mathrm{Hom}(Z, X) \times \mathrm{Hom}(Z, Y)\) — the product of P31 is, in this language, the holder of the pairing table; the curator's Q3 is the case where the holder outgrows the museum. Moduli at research scale: the objects geometers most want — moduli of curves, of bundles, of phases — are defined by writing the family table first ("families varying along a base") and asking who can hold it; the sketch in §4 says what that hunt looks like when it is the whole point.

4 · Three encounters

First, the world on the table. The probes are \(0\) (the empty set), \(1\) (the point), \(2\) (the coin). The maps between probes — all functions, since sets carry no further structure — number eleven, and the census needs names for the interesting ones: the two point-fits \(f_a, f_b: 1 \to 2\) (point at face \(a\), point at face \(b\)); the collapse \(u: 2 \to 1\) (both faces to the point); the coin's four self-maps — the identity, the swap \(s\), and the two constants \(c_a, c_b\) (everything to \(a\), everything to \(b\)); and the three empty fits. Composition facts the census will lean on: \(u \circ f_a = u \circ f_b = \mathrm{id}_1\) (collapse after point-fit is doing nothing), \(f_a \circ u = c_a\) (point-fit after collapse is the constant), and \(s \circ f_a = f_b\) (swap after point-fit flips the face).

Encounter 1 — the census. How many ways can one honest table be turned into another? A candidate is a choice of function \(\alpha_Z: h_X(Z) \to h_Y(Z)\) for each probe \(Z\), and it is natural — a genuine table-comparison — when every restriction square commutes: pre-composing then applying \(\alpha\) agrees with applying \(\alpha\) then restricting, for all eleven maps of the world. The world is small, so honesty is checkable: enumerate every candidate, test every square. The census of all nine pairs \((h_X, h_Y)\) says:

  • \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_0, h_0) = 1\), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_0, h_1) = 1\), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_0, h_2) = 1\).
  • \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, h_0) = 0\) — not "none survived": no candidate existed at all. The empty set's table answers the point-probe with the empty set of ways, and there is no function from a one-element set into it. The table's refusal to receive mirrors, exactly, the world's refusal to send: there are no maps \(1 \to 0\) either.
  • \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, h_1) = 1\), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, h_2) = 2\) (of \(8\) candidates), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, h_0) = 0\).
  • \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, h_1) = 1\) (the unique candidate survives), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, h_2) = 4\) — of \(1024\) candidates, four natural. This is the biggest census of the section: every way of re-labeling the coin's four coin-answers and the two point-answers and the one empty-answer, tested against eleven squares apiece.

Two readings of the table. First: every entry equals \(\mathrm{Hom}(X, Y)\), the number of maps \(X \to Y\) in the world — \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, h_2) = 2\) because there are two point-fits; \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, h_2) = 4\) because the coin has four self-maps. Comparing tables is comparing objects, with nothing lost. Second: the nine entries sum to \(1+1+1+0+1+2+0+1+4 = 11\) — the world's own map count, relocated one level up. The census of tables sees the whole world in the tables' own comparisons.

And the census catches a surprise the tally alone would miss. Build the subsets table \(P\): it answers probe \(Z\) with the subsets of \(Z\), and restricts along \(f: Z \to Z'\) by preimage — a table through and through, with no object anywhere in sight. Its tally is \(1, 2, 4\): the coin's tally. Suspicious — until the census of \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, P)\) returns \(4\) of \(1024\), exactly \(P(2)\), the four subsets; each natural \(\alpha\) acts as preimage of the single subset \(S = \alpha_2(\mathrm{id}_2)\); and the dictionary \(S \mapsto \)(its \(0/1\)-valued membership function) commutes with every one of the eleven restriction squares. The subsets table is the coin's table — the same table under a change of names, the dictionary delivered by the lemma. "How many elements land in this region?" is the same question as "which face of the coin does each element show?" — the yes/no question table was the coin all along.

Encounter 2 — the forgers who pass. The tally \(1, 2, 4\) is easy to counterfeit, so the museum issues two authentication laws that every representable's tally must obey. The empty-question law: ask any table \(R\) about the empty probe and the answer is one way every time, \(|h_R(0)| = 1\), because there is exactly one map \(0 \to Z\) no matter the target. The multiplying law: for the honest tables of this world, \(|h_R(2)| = |h_R(1)|^2\) — the coin's answers are pairs of point-answers. Counterfeiters fail fast. A museum's empty display case — the table answering every probe with the empty set, restricting by the empty function — is a perfectly genuine table, functorial to its bones, and it fails the empty-question law at the first probe: \(E(0) = 0\). A table that answers \(1, 1, 5\) — one way from the empty probe, one from the point, five from the coin, with the swap acting trivially and the constants acting constantly — is likewise genuine as a table, passes the empty-question law, and dies on the multiplying law: \(5 \neq 1^2\). Neither has a representor. The laws are cheap; that is the point: two probes already sort the obvious fakes.

But the museum's real problem is the sophisticated forger, and here the census delivers its finest result. Build every table that claims to be the coin: the claim fixes the tally \(1, 2, 4\), so the forger's freedom is the restriction structure — how the point-answers pull back along the two point-fits, how the coin-answers restrict, what the swap does. Parameterize all of it and the space of claims is finite and small: twelve injective placements of the point-answers into the coin-answers \(G(u)\), four retractions \(r_a\) undoing each placement as the point-fit pulls coin-answers back, two behaviours for the swap \(G(s)\) — \(12 \times 4 \times 2 = 96\) candidates in all. The census runs every one, and the verdict has three floors:

  • All ninety-six are genuine. Every candidate is a real table — restrictions compose, every square commutes. Functoriality is cheap; the claim "I am the coin" is not a matter of being a table at all.
  • Twenty-four are the coin. Those twenty-four are naturally isomorphic to \(h_2\) — same table under a change of names — and they fall into \(4\) isomorphism classes of \(24\) each, which is to say: up to renaming, exactly one of the ninety-six is the coin.
  • Seventy-two are chimeras. Genuine tables, coin tally, both laws passed — and still not the coin. One deserves a name: the table with \(G(u) = [0, 1]\) (the two point-answers tucked into the first two coin-answers), restriction \(r_a = [0, 1, 0, 0]\), and — the tell — the swap acting as the identity on \(G(2)\). It is genuine, it counts \(1, 2, 4\), and no renaming can make it the coin. The obstruction is one line: a renaming is carried by the correspondence \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha_2(\mathrm{id}_2)\), which forces the swap's action to travel with it — \(G(s)(\alpha_2(\mathrm{id}_2)) = \alpha_2(s)\) — and an identity action can never carry a genuine swap's nontriviality across. Conjugating an identity never yields a swap. The chimera is stuck being a different table forever, and the census, not the tally, is what caught it.

Encounter 3 — the shadow of a count. The tier's opening move [p31] told two worlds apart by counting: the clock \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) and the double-flip \(V\) admitted different numbers of maps into \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) — two against four — and the objects were distinguished without being opened. From this page's vantage the shadow resolves: those counts were one table \(h_{\mathbb{Z}/2}\) answering two probes, \(2\) at the clock and \(4\) at \(V\); telling objects apart was the table differing from itself across probes. The identification is the same instrument, read all the way: an object is its map-counts.

And the lemma's oldest shadow is Cayley's. Take a one-object world — the group \(V = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2\) as a category with one object and four self-maps. A table on this world is a set with a compatible \(V\)-action. Ask how many ways the single representable table \(h_\bullet\) — the table of \(V\)'s own maps — maps into a table \(T\): the census of equivariant functions from \(V\)'s four maps to \(T\)'s elements. Out of \(256\) candidate functions, exactly \(4\) are equivariant — the four translations, one per group element — and all \(16\) composition pairs add their offsets, with the map from group elements to translations injective. That is Cayley's theorem verbatim, a group acting on itself by translation; the general statement — a representable's transformations into anything, and everything into a representable — is the same theorem run on worlds with more than one object.

SKETCH — the family functor, honestly incomplete. The deepest reach of the trick, sketched but not run: in geometry, "the moduli space of shapes" wants to be a representable — a space whose points are the shapes — and families of shapes "varying along" a base should be exactly maps into it. The Grassmannian is the classical success: families of subspaces of a vector space [b05] are represented by an honest space, and cohomology rides along. But for genuinely rigid families — curves with automorphisms — the sketch frays at three named seams: what "varying along" means when fibres change type is left undefined here; the construction and the proof have not been run anywhere in this book, so the identification is asserted, not demonstrated; and automorphisms — isotropy — break representability outright, and the repair (stacks) is a structure this page only points at. The sketch is a promise about where the trick goes, and an honest note that this page stops short of it.

5 · When the trick breaks

Every pattern page owes an honest list of failure modes. The representable trick fails in three distinct ways, and each has a repair, a price, and a default.

Exhibit A — the chimera that counts. A table with a perfect tally can still fail to be any object at all: of the ninety-six coin-claimants, seventy-two pass both authentication laws and are still not the coin. The tally is a checksum, not a signature.

Mitigation: stop trusting tallies; run the census. A candidate is the coin exactly when it is naturally isomorphic to \(h_2\), and the census decides — the obstruction that froze the named chimera was one line of naturality, not any count.

Price: a census is brute force. This world is small enough to enumerate — every candidate tested against every square — but the same census over a world with a ten-element probe would drown: the candidate count grows as a tower of powers. The mitigation is honest work, not cheap work.

Default: when identification matters — when a claim of "same object" is load-bearing — demand the naturality check and refuse the tally. When only a rough size comparison matters, the tally is fine, and this page will keep using it for that and nothing else.

Exhibit B — the world too small. Build the pairing table \(\mathrm{Pair}(2,2)\): it answers each probe with the ways to assign each face of the coin a face — \((1, 4, 16)\) at the three probes, one way from the empty set, four from the point, sixteen from the coin. Both laws pass — \(16 = 4^2\), and the empty probe answers one — so the tally says: representable, go find the object. The census returns sixteen natural transformations from \(h_2\), of \(1{,}048{,}576\) candidates — exactly the sixteen that maps into a four-element object would give. But the lineup is short: every object of this world answers the point-probe with at most two — the honest probes are \(0, 1, 2\), never \(4\). No object in the world has the wanted tally. The table is genuine, the laws pass, and the representing object simply is not there: the world is too small to hold it.

Mitigation: enlarge the world. The representing object is a four-element set — the world's probes stop at two elements, so the table points past the edge; adding the object (which the census already counts: sixteen ways, from the coin, into it) repairs the identification.

Price: the enlarged world is no longer the world you started with — new probes mean new tables everywhere, and every census run on the old world must be run again.

Default: read representability claims relative to a fixed world, and treat "the table is fine but the object is missing" as a fact about the world, not a failure of the table. This is the honest form of the classical phenomenon: a functor can be perfectly good and still not be representable on the site you were given.

Exhibit C — the bookkeeper's burden. The identification says the object is the table — the whole table: its value at every probe and its restriction along every map. The tally is three numbers; the table is a network of answers with compatibility squares between them. Anyone who replaces an object by its table inherits the bookkeeping: every construction on objects must be re-checked as a construction on tables, and naturality is a real condition to verify, not a formality.

Mitigation: work with finitely many probes at a time, and let the lemma carry the rest — this is exactly what the classical theory does when it proves a property at one well-chosen probe and reads it off everywhere.

Price: the savings are only as good as the lemma's hypotheses; drop functoriality and nothing carries.

Default: accept the bookkeeping as the actual cost of the trick. The alternative — pretending the tally is the object — is Exhibit A waiting to happen.

Scope, honestly. The identification works for tables that are genuine functors, and the census method works for worlds small enough to enumerate. This page's worlds are deliberately tiny; the classical lemma holds for worlds of any size, but the brute-force census is not how it is used out there — it is how it is checked here. Nothing on this page proves that a specific mathematical structure is representable; the labs verify the correspondence on finite worlds and the sketch section says so out loud.

6 · Doors out

The representable trick is a hub: once objects are identified with their tables, several later patterns become readable as the same move in different clothes.

  • Door — straighten, then unstraighten (the straightening trick): replacing a structure by a plainer one that carries the same information is a representable-style identification, and the page needs this page's correspondence to say what "same information" means.
  • Door — sheafify (the sheafification trick): the objects over a site are already tables; sheafification decides which tables are honest, and representability is the first question asked of an honest table.
  • Door — complete the group (the group completion trick): the Cayley shadow of this page is the one-object case; group completion is the same identification run after adding inverses, and the comparison belongs there.
  • Door — algebras for an operad (the operad trick): an operad algebra is a table whose restrictions respect compositions; the representable viewpoint — structures as probes-into-a-universal — is the shape of that pattern's universal objects.
  • Door — the literature: the classical statement and proof live in Mac Lane's Categories for the Working Mathematician and Leinster's Basic Category Theory, both under the lemma's own name; this page's museum is a finite shadow of their general statement, and the proof there is two diagram chases where this page's is a census.

7 · Lineage

The trick is old, and its name is younger than its content.

  • 1854 — Cayley. Cayley's theorem embeds every group into its own translations: a group is faithfully present in how it shuffles itself. This page's one-object census — four equivariant functions of 256, the four translations — is that theorem run as a table census. The representable trick is the many-object version of the same idea.
  • 1945 — Eilenberg and Mac Lane. The founding paper of category theory needs the one universal property it can state without the lemma's language; maps and composition arrive as primary, objects as afterthoughts. The identification this page dramatizes — objects are their map-counts — is the founding pair's viewpoint pushed to its logical end.
  • 1954 — Yoneda. Nobuo Yoneda's paper on homology of modules contains the lemma in embryonic form; the general statement and the observation that it classifies everything about an object circulated in Tokyo seminars around that time. The mathematics predates the name.
  • 1957 — Grothendieck. The Tôhoku paper rebuilds homological algebra on representable functors and abelian categories: the identification becomes the working language of a whole field, and "a group is its characters" stops being an analogy and becomes a method.
  • The name. Saunders Mac Lane coined "Yoneda's lemma" after a conversation with Yoneda in a Paris café near the Gare du Nord in 1954, reportedly pressed by Yoneda to write the observation down; the anecdote is preserved in a memoir by Kinoshita (1996). The lemma was named for the person who insisted on it, by the person who wrote it — a fitting name for a theorem that says an object is whatever its maps insist it is.
  • The repair — Mumford, then Artin. When families carry automorphisms — the sketch's frayed seam — representability fails, and the repair is due to Mumford's geometric invariant theory and Artin's stacks, where the identification is deliberately weakened to accommodate the symmetry. The repair is a modern research topic, not a settled chapter; this page only marks the door.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Write the three honest tables by hand. For each of \(h_0, h_1, h_2\), list the answers at the three probes and verify both authentication laws, the empty-question law and the multiplying law, line by line.

Hint

The answers at probe \(Z\) are the maps \(Z \to X\); count them for each pair. For the empty-question law, ask why the empty set admits exactly one map into anything.

Worked answer

Tallies: \(h_0\) answers \((1, 0, 0)\), \(h_1\) answers \((1, 1, 1)\), \(h_2\) answers \((1, 2, 4)\). Empty-question law: each table answers one way at probe \(0\) — there is exactly one map \(\emptyset \to X\) for every \(X\), degenerate row included. Multiplying law: \(0 = 0^2\), \(1 = 1^2\), \(4 = 2^2\). Both laws pass on all three tables; the point of the exercise is that passing both laws costs nothing and buys only a first filter.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — Pairing with one free coordinate. Build the table \(\mathrm{Pair}(1,2)\) that answers probe \(Z\) with pairs \((\ast, g)\) where \(\ast\) is a fixed element of \(1\) and \(g: Z \to 2\). Show it is the coin's table in disguise, and that its census matches the coin's arithmetic.

Hint

The renaming to try is \(g \mapsto (\ast, g)\). For the census, count candidates as products of the answers at each probe and check the naturality squares on the two point-fits \(f_a, f_b\).

Worked answer

The map \(g \mapsto (\ast, g)\) is a bijection at every probe, and it commutes with restriction because pre-composing \(g\) does not touch the frozen coordinate: the square on \(f_a\) sends \((\ast, g)\) to \((\ast, g \circ f_a)\) on both routes, and similarly for \(f_b\). So \(\mathrm{Pair}(1,2) \cong h_2\). The censuses agree: \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, \mathrm{Pair}(1,2))\) keeps \(2\) of \(8\), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, \mathrm{Pair}(1,2))\) keeps \(4\) of \(1024\), matching \(h_2\)'s own arithmetic. For contrast, \(\mathrm{Pair}(2,2)\) answers \((1, 4, 16)\), and the product object \(2 \times 2\), if the world had it, would answer \((1, 4, 16)\) at exactly these probes — the arithmetic of the missing object is already in the table.

Exercise 3 ●●● — Prove the correspondence by hand, then run it on the subsets table. Take a natural \(\alpha: h_X \to T\), set \(s = \alpha_X(\mathrm{id}_X)\), and show \(\alpha_Z(g) = T(g)(s)\) for every probe \(Z\) and map \(g: Z \to X\); then check the construction reverses. Finally: the four natural transformations \(h_2 \to P\) into the subsets table are four subsets — say which, and verify the naturality square for the collapse \(u\) when \(S = \{a\}\).

Hint

For the forward direction, naturality in the probe \(X\) itself, along \(g\), is the whole proof: chase \(\mathrm{id}_X\) around the square. For the subsets, recall that \(\alpha_2(\mathrm{id}_2)\) is a subset of the coin's faces.

Worked answer

Naturality of \(\alpha\) along \(g: Z \to X\) says \(T(g) \circ \alpha_X = \alpha_Z \circ h_X(g)\); evaluate at \(\mathrm{id}_X\): the left side is \(T(g)(s)\), the right side is \(\alpha_Z(g \circ \mathrm{id}_X) = \alpha_Z(g)\). So \(\alpha_Z(g) = T(g)(s)\): the value at the identity determines every value. Conversely, any \(s \in T(X)\) defines \(\alpha_Z(g) = T(g)(s)\), and functoriality of \(T\) makes the squares commute, so the construction inverts. For \(P\): the four natural transformations correspond to the four subsets \(S \subseteq \{a, b\}\), each acting as \(\alpha_Z(g) = g^{-1}(S) \subseteq Z\). The square for the collapse \(u: 2 \to 1\) with \(S = \{a\}\): start with \(f_a \in h_2(1)\); one route gives \(\alpha_2(f_a \circ u) = \alpha_2(c_a) = c_a^{-1}(\{a\}) = \{a, b\}\); the other gives \(u^{-1}(\alpha_1(f_a)) = u^{-1}(\{\ast\}) = \{a, b\}\). Both faces land in the same subset; the square commutes.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Diagnose the student who says the counts look recursive-ish. A student hands you a table with tally \((1, 2, 3)\) and restrictions \(r_a = r_b = [0, 1, 0]\), \(G(s) = \mathrm{id}\), \(G(u) = [0, 1]\), and claims that since the counts grow, the table must be representable "by recursion". What is true, what is false, and what would the census say?

Hint

Check the table for genuine functoriality first — restrictions compose? — then check the multiplying law, then ask what the honest probes can answer at probe \(2\).

Worked answer

True: the table is genuine — \(r_a \circ G(u) = \mathrm{id}\), the constants restrict consistently, the swap acts trivially, and every square commutes. False: representability fails twice. The multiplying law reads \(3 \neq 2^2\), so the tally is already wrong; and even ignoring the law, the honest probe-2 answers of this world are \(0, 1, 4\) — never \(3\) — so no object has this tally. The "recursion" was a pattern in the student's three numbers, not a constraint from the world. The census would return no representor, and the deeper lesson from the ninety-six claimants is that even a law-passing tally leaves seventy-two chimeras uncaught: only the naturality census, not any sequence of counts, decides.

Exercise 5 ◐ — Table-world exploration, open end. In the world with probes \(0, 1, 2\), run the censuses \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_1, \mathrm{Pair}(1,2))\), \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, \mathrm{Pair}(1,2))\), and \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_2, \mathrm{Pair}(2,2))\), and line up the survivors against the pairing tables' own answers. What single arithmetic rule predicts all three counts? What object is missing from the world, and which later page of this atlas handles the question of which tables deserve to be called honest?

Hint

The candidate pools are \(2^3 = 8\), \(4^5 = 1024\), and \(16^5 = 1{,}048{,}576\). Compare each survivor count with the table's answer at the probe being counted from.

Worked answer

The censuses return \(8 \to 2\), \(1024 \to 4\), and \(1{,}048{,}576 \to 16\): each survivor count equals the target table's answer at the source probe — \(\mathrm{Nat}(h_Z, T) = T(Z)\), the correspondence running with any honest table in the target slot, representable or not. The missing object is a four-element product object whose table would answer \((1, 4, 16)\); the pairing table carries its full arithmetic while the object sits outside the world. Which tables deserve honesty — and what repairs a world that is too small — is the business of the sheafification page, and the open end of this exercise is its front door.
