The Patterns Atlas · P44 · the third move of tier 4

Formal verification

When a proof is too long, too delicate, or too important to referee by eye, stop trusting the prover: write every step as a line of a certificate, hand the certificate to a kernel — a referee small enough to read, fixed enough to argue with, dumb enough to be certain — and let anyone re-run the verdict.

New word: kernel. In this page's world a kernel is a small, fixed program that referees proofs. It knows a handful of rules and nothing else — no ideas, no shortcuts, no taste. You feed it a proof written line by line; it either certifies every line or names the first line it cannot. The word matters because of what it excludes: everything clever lives outside the kernel, in the prover — the machinery, human or artificial, that finds proofs. The kernel only checks. Keep the kernel small enough for one person to read in one sitting, and the question should I believe this theorem? collapses into should I believe this checker? — a question you can finally do something about. That collapse is the whole move.

1 · Before we begin

P34 ended its second question with a promise. Can a machine check a proof? — the codebook had made proof-checking arithmetic: statements became numbers, rules became decidable relations, and the little proof machine counted proofs over its pool of 81 equations without ever missing one. But counting proofs is theater until the checking becomes the point. This page pays the promise: the proof machine comes back with its roles split in two. In P34 it was a census engine, counting what a rulebook could derive. Here it becomes two machines — a prover, which searches the sea of possible proofs, and a checker, which reads one proof line by line — and everything that matters lives in the difference between those two jobs.

Two older debts come due at the same moment. P01 taught you to pin a problem before touching it; here the pinning is load-bearing at industrial scale, because a kernel certifies exactly the statement you wrote — not the one you meant, not the one you said aloud. And P25 taught you that a machine census must be audited with a second independent engine; that audit obligation, promoted from a virtue to an entire method, is the design of this page's machine. P25's §6 named this door, and P35 leaned on it from the other side: two worlds forced independence by refusing to agree; here certainty is built by refusing to trust.

The tension ahead: a human referee reads 255 pages and gets tired on page 40; a machine referee reads 150,000 lines and gets nothing wrong — if the referee is right. But the referee is a program, and programs have bugs. So the real question of this page is not can a machine check a proof? — P34 settled that — but who checks the checker? The answer is an architecture, and it is worth a whole pattern.

2 · Meet the problem

Here is a proof. It is not a sketch and not an outline: it is the complete certificate for a theorem you have known since you were six, written in a rulebook small enough to hold in your head. The language has numerals built from \(0\) and the successor mark \(S\) — so \(SS0\) is two — together with the addition symbol. The rulebook has exactly four rules:

  • Rule A1. For any term \(t\), you may write the line \(t + 0 = t\).
  • Rule A2. For any terms \(t\) and \(u\), you may write the line \(t + S(u) = S(t + u)\).
  • Rule S. If an earlier line says \(a = b\), you may write \(S(a) = S(b)\).
  • Rule T. If two earlier lines say \(a = b\) and \(b = c\), you may write \(a = c\).

And here is the certificate — seven lines, each with its justification in the margin:

LineEquationJustification
1\((SS0+0)=SS0\)A1 with \(t := SS0\)
2\((SS0+S0)=S(SS0+0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := 0\)
3\(S(SS0+0)=SSS0\)Rule S on line 1
4\((SS0+S0)=SSS0\)Rule T on lines 2 and 3
5\((SS0+SS0)=S(SS0+S0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := S0\)
6\(S(SS0+S0)=SSSS0\)Rule S on line 4
7\((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\)Rule T on lines 5 and 6

Referee it. Line by line — not with mathematical taste, but with a clerk's eye. Line 1: is it an instance of A1? The left side is \(SS0 + 0\), the right side is \(SS0\) — yes, \(t\) is \(SS0\), and the rule says exactly this. Line 2: instance of A2 with \(t = SS0\), \(u = 0\)? Left: \(SS0 + S(0)\); right: \(S(SS0 + 0)\) — yes. Line 3: line 1 says \(SS0+0 = SS0\); rule S prefixes both sides with \(S\) — and \(S(SS0)\) is written in its bare-numeral costume \(SSS0\). Yes. Line 4: line 2's right side and line 3's left side are the same term, so rule T chains them — yes. Carry on: line 5 is another A2; line 6 is rule S on line 4; line 7 chains 5 with 6. Seven yeses, and the last line reads: two plus two is four. You have just refereed a proof.

Notice what you did not need. You never needed to know where the proof was going. You never held more than three lines in your mind at once. You needed zero mathematical insight — a clerk with the four rules on a card does your job, which is exactly the point: the checking is local. Only the finding is global. Now the sting. Here is a second certificate — same first four lines, and then:

LineEquationJustification
5\((SS0+SS0)=S(SS0+0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := S0\)
6\(S(SS0+S0)=SSSS0\)Rule S on line 4
7\((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\)Rule T on lines 5 and 6

Line 5 claims to be A2 with \(u := S0\), which must read \(t + S(S0) = S(t + S0)\) — but its right side says \(S(SS0+0)\), the instance for \(u := 0\). A slip, one character deep. The certificate dies at line 5, and you never had to look at lines 6 and 7 to know it. Checking found the flaw without understanding the proof — that is the asymmetry this page is about.

Three questions, and the page is their answer. (1) How long would you take to referee ten thousand such lines — and would you be right on line 9,547 after lunch? (2) What exactly did you do at each line? The rulebook was so precise that a machine could have done your job — so why not let it? (3) The doctored proof died at line 5 — but when the referee is a program, who referees the referee?

3 · Name the move

Pattern P44 — Formal verification (machine-refereed proof). When a claim must survive referees it cannot count on having — proofs too long for human patience, too delicate for human attention, too important for human fatigue — (1) write the statement and every step in a formal language: a fixed alphabet, fixed formation rules, and a rulebook of inference rules so exact that a clerk with no insight could apply them — P34's codebook is the prototype, and P01's pinning is the gate: the kernel certifies exactly the statement written, so the statement must be written exactly; (2) hand the certificate to a kernel: a small, fixed, readable checker whose only job is to verify each line against the rulebook — no search, no ideas, no mercy — with all cleverness expelled to the untrusted prover outside; (3) make the certificate the artifact: what convinces is not the prover — huge, clever, possibly buggy, possibly lucky — but the certificate, which anyone can re-check with any independent implementation of the same rulebook and reach the same verdict.

Or, in words: do not ask your reader to trust the wizard. Ask the wizard to drop a receipt — a proof written so plainly that a mindless clerk can audit it — and let every reader run their own clerk. The wizard may be wrong or lying; the clerk cannot be fooled, only out-gamed by a rulebook you chose, and the choosing stays yours.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the claim needs certainty that survives scale. A proof of 255 journal pages; a census of 1,936 configurations run by 1,200 hours of machine time; a library of 2.5 million lines that no single human has read. When the artifact outgrows the referee, the human eye stops being an instrument. Jam-mode: the claim is a finite computation you can simply re-run — then P25's census answers its own question, and a kernel is overkill; the kernel earns its keep only when the artifact must be argued for, step by step, against a skeptic with unlimited patience.

Key 2 — the steps are checkable locally. Each step either matches a rule or follows from cited lines; nothing hangs on judgment. This is the structural diagnosis: local checks, global search. Jam-mode: the argument's load-bearing step is an act of taste — "this construction is natural", "the generalization is harmless", "the diagram commutes in the obvious way" — and no rulebook will referee taste. Then the move stalls at exactly that step; the honest options are to finish the translation (find a proof whose steps are local) or to keep the human referee and say so.

Key 3 — the statement can be pinned in the formal language. The theorem you want must be sayable exactly in the codebook. Jam-mode: the statement itself resists pinning — it quantifies over "all reasonable spaces", or leans on words whose formalization is the whole research problem. Then the kernel certifies nothing you care about; the hardest mathematics is often upstream of the proof, in the statement (Exhibit A below makes this concrete).

Box P44.A — the machine, in three obligations. (1) A formal language and rulebook. Symbols, formation rules, axioms, inference rules — everything the kernel will need and nothing it will not. The certificate format is public: a proof is a list of lines, each line an equation plus a justification that cites rules or earlier lines. (2) A small kernel. The checker implements the rulebook and nothing else. The community's standard — call it the de Bruijn criterion, after the man whose checker earned it first — is that the kernel stay small enough to read by hand: every layer of cleverness you add outside (search tactics, automation, AI) is untrusted and harmless; every line you add inside is a new place the whole edifice can rot. (3) Independent re-checking. Because the rulebook is public and tiny, anyone can write a second kernel from scratch — and must reach the same verdict. P25's audit obligation, promoted from a virtue to the entire method: the second engine is not a luxury, it is where the trust actually lives.

Box P44.B — where the move appears. The proof assistants — Automath, LCF, Mizar, Coq, Isabelle, Lean — are this pattern industrialized: a tiny trusted kernel under an untrusted ecosystem of search tools. The certified landmarks: Landau's Foundations of Analysis translated and machine-checked (1976 — the first book ever refereed by a kernel); the four-color theorem, whose 1976 machine census P25 told, certified inside Coq (2005); the odd-order theorem — 255 pages, six years, about 150,000 lines (2012); the Kepler conjecture (2014); Scholze's liquid-vector-space theorem, a working mathematician's live research, certified (2022). Below the mathematics, the same device certifies software: a verified C compiler (2009), a verified operating-system kernel (2009). And in 2026 the discipline turned its own machinery on itself, when a community-wide hunt for soundness bugs in proof kernels — found, fixed, re-verified — showed what auditing the auditor looks like at full scale.

4 · The move at work — three encounters

Three encounters, at three scales. First the kernel is built, by hand, over the four-rule arithmetic of §2 — and tortured. Then a second rulebook — the propositional logic of P34 — gets the same treatment, and the kernel's verdicts are audited against a truth table, the way P25 audits engines against each other. Finally the same machine, scaled up fifty thousand lines: the odd-order theorem. Between them they show the whole pattern: local checking, global search, independent audit.

Example 1 · The kernel, built

Moves used: P01 the pinning the rulebook enforces · P34 the codebook, made into a referee · P25 the poison battery, by habit.

Build the kernel over §2's rulebook. It keeps one table — the lines accepted so far — and reads the certificate line by line, with exactly one question per line: does the justification check?

An axiom justification checks by matching: if the line says A1, both sides must be \(t + 0 = t\) for one and the same term \(t\) — substitute, compare, done. If it says A2, both sides must be \(t + S(u) = S(t + u)\) for one and the same pair \(t, u\) — one substitution, both sides, no exceptions. A rule justification checks by citation: S means "fetch the cited line \(a = b\), and check that this line is \(S(a) = S(b)\), nothing more"; T means "fetch the two cited lines \(a = b\) and \(b = c\), check that the first's right side and the second's left side are the same term, and check that this line is \(a = c\)". No line may cite a line the kernel has not yet accepted; no line may cite itself; a numeral written in bare costume \(SSS0\) must match \(S(SS0)\) unpacked. That is the whole kernel — on the scale of this page, a dozen lines of code; on the scale of a real proof assistant, a few thousand. Run it on §2's certificate:

L1: A1 at \(t = SS0\). Match. L2: A2 at \(t = SS0\), \(u = 0\); left \((SS0+S0)\), right \(S(SS0+0)\). Match. L3: S on L1 — \(S(SS0+0) = S(SS0)\), the right side in bare costume \(SSS0\). Match. L4: T on L2 and L3 — the middle terms agree at \(S(SS0+0)\); conclusion \((SS0+S0)=SSS0\). Match. L5: A2 at \(t = SS0\), \(u = S0\); left \((SS0+SS0)\), right \(S(SS0+S0)\). Match. L6: S on L4. Match. L7: T on L5 and L6 — middle terms agree at \(S(SS0+S0)\); conclusion \((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\). Match. ACCEPT, seven reads, zero ideas. Two plus two is four now has a receipt no insight was needed to check.

Now torture it — P25's discipline, that an engine is only an instrument after it has refused what it should refuse. Five poisonings, each a certificate for \((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\) broken in exactly one place:

poisonwhere it breaksthe kernel's verdict

L4 keeps its equation \((SS0+S0)=SSS0\) but cites lines 1 and 3 instead of 2 and 3line 1's right side \(SS0\) is not line 3's left side \(S(SS0+0)\) — the chain's middle terms are not the same termREJECT, line 4: T middle mismatch
L7 rewritten \((SS0+SS0)=SSSSS0\), still citing lines 5 and 6conclusion does not follow from the cited pairREJECT, line 7: T conclusion mismatch

Five refusals, five correct lines named, five correct reasons given — the doctored twin of §2 caught at line 5, exactly as promised. The kernel is not being clever; there is nowhere clever to be. Every check is a pattern match or a table lookup, and the whole audit cost seven reads.

Two receipts that the machine works, both computed by exhaustive census — the kernel's world, enumerated. First, the saturation census: fix the small world — every equation \((a+b)=c\) in which the addends \(a, b\) run to at most four successors and the claimed result \(c\) to at most eight — and it holds exactly \(5 \times 5 \times 9 = 225\) well-formed equations. Run the prover — any program you like, clever or stupid — until it saturates: the rulebook's certificates reach exactly the 25 true sums, \(0+0=0\), \(1+3=4\), \(2+2=4\), up to \(4+4=8\), one proved certificate per true equation and nothing else. Two structural facts fall out of the census. Each sum's dependency depth — how deeply its proof nests — is exactly the second addend \(b\): to prove \(a + S(b)\) the prover must first prove \(a + b\), so the A2 steps of any certificate for \(a + b\) nest \(b\) deep, one peel of the second addend per level. The nesting is visible in §2's certificate: \((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\) sits at depth 2, its two nested peels being the A2 instances at \(u := S0\) and \(u := 0\). And \(2 + 2 = 5\) is nowhere: the equation \((SS0+SS0)=SSSSS0\) is one of the 225 — squarely in the world — and the saturated closure never reaches it: not at depth 2, not at depth 50, not at any depth. The machine has now witnessed soundness on its whole world, not just assumed it.

Second, the funnel — the asymmetry, measured. How many seven-line certificates could the prover have written? Count the candidate lines per position, everything the rulebook allows. Line 1 must be an axiom instance — 25 of them in the small world: five A1 (one per addend, \(t + 0 = t\) for \(t \leq 4\)) and twenty A2 (\(t \leq 4\), second addend with one to four successors). At position \(k\), each of the \(k - 1\) accepted lines licenses exactly one S-application, and each ordered pair of distinct accepted lines licenses exactly one T-application — so the counts run 25, 26, 29, 34, 41, 50, 61, climbing as the growing certificate gives the rules more to cite. The number of seven-line certificates is the product:

\[25 \times 26 \times 29 \times 34 \times 41 \times 50 \times 61 \;=\; 80{,}144{,}545{,}000 \;\approx\; 8.0 \times 10^{10},\]

eighty billion candidate proofs, of which the rulebook certifies a trickle. Finding the one certificate that ends \((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\) is a global, exponential search — that is the prover's job, and it is hard. Checking the found certificate is seven pattern matches against a four-rule card. That gap — \(8 \times 10^{10}\) on the finding side, 7 on the checking side — is the entire technology. Every proof assistant ever built is this gap, industrialized.

Example 2 · The propositional kernel and the truth-table audit

Moves used: P34 the two-axiom codebook of implication · P25 the second engine — here a truth table — cross-checking the first.

The arithmetic rulebook was a toy; now take the honest fragment of P34's logic — formulas built from a single variable \(p\) and the implication arrow \(\to\), with two axiom rules and one inference rule:

  • Rule A1. For any formulas \(a, b\): you may write \(a \to (b \to a)\).
  • Rule A2. For any formulas \(a, b, c\): you may write \(\big(a \to (b \to c)\big) \to \big((a \to b) \to (a \to c)\big)\).
  • Rule MP. If earlier lines say \(a \to b\) and \(a\), you may write \(b\).

Three rules, and this time the kernel's MP check is the subtle one: it must fetch both cited lines, parse the first as \(a \to b\), check the second is that \(a\) — character for character — and check the new line is that \(b\). Here is a five-line certificate for the most trivial-sounding theorem in logic, \(p \to p\) — "if \(p\), then \(p\)" — which, notice, is not an axiom:

LineFormulaJustification
1\(p \to ((p \to p) \to p)\)A1 with \(a := p,\ b := (p \to p)\)
2\(\big(p \to ((p \to p) \to p)\big) \to \big((p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p)\big)\)A2 with \(a := p,\ b := (p \to p),\ c := p\)
3\((p \to (p \to p)) \to (p \to p)\)MP on lines 2 and 1
4\(p \to (p \to p)\)A1 with \(a := p,\ b := p\)
5\(p \to p\)MP on lines 3 and 4

Read the two MP steps the way the kernel does. Line 3 cites lines 2 and 1: line 2 parses as (line 1) \(\to\) (line 3), and line 1 is exactly the antecedent — so line 3 is licensed, and its content is whatever line 2 says it is. Line 5 cites lines 3 and 4: line 3 parses as (line 4) \(\to\) (line 5), line 4 matches the antecedent, license granted. Five reads, ACCEPT. Poison it: cite lines 2 and 4 for MP and the kernel says no — line 4 is not the antecedent of line 2, and the verdict comes back REJECT: MP minor premise is not the antecedent, at exactly the poisoned line, every time. Swap in \(\big((p \to p) \to p\big) \to p\) as line 5 while citing lines 3 and 4 and the refusal names the conclusion: the cited pair licenses \(p \to p\), nothing else.

Now the audit — P25's second engine, and for logic the second engine has a name: the truth table. Enumerate the world: three propositional atoms \(p, q, r\), formulas ranked by implication depth. At depth 0 stand the 3 atoms; adding one arrow over that pool gives 9 implications, so the depth-\(\leq 1\) pool holds 12 formulas; one more arrow gives the depth-\(\leq 2\) pool of \(12 + 144 = 156\). Evaluate each of the 156 on all \(2^3 = 8\) truth assignments — eight bits per formula, a byte of pure semantics — and the table finds 54 tautologies among them.

Two censuses, two engines, and the demand that they agree. The kernel's side: saturate the rulebook. Every A1 instance \(a \to (b \to a)\) over the depth-\(\leq 1\) pool gives \(12 \times 12 = 144\) seeds; every A2 instance \(\big(a \to (b \to c)\big) \to \big((a \to b) \to (a \to c)\big)\) gives \(12^3 = 1{,}728\) more; that is 1,872 seed lines. Close under MP — every licensed detachment, applied until nothing new appears — and the kernel's census reaches 2,082 provable formulas. The truth table's side: those same 2,082 formulas, each evaluated byte-wise. Result: all 2,082 are tautologies, zero exceptions. That is soundness witnessed, not assumed: the rulebook's every consequence, checked against semantics by an engine that never read the rulebook. This is exactly P25's cross-check, elevated: the kernel and the truth table are two engines with no code in common, and on 2,082 formulas they never disagree.

And the boundary — the most instructive receipt of the page. Take Peirce's law \(\big((p \to q) \to p\big) \to p\) — one implication deeper than the census pool, but the truth table does not care about depth: all eight bits set, byte \(255\), a tautology with no doubt about it. Kernel census: absent — not among the seeds, not in the MP closure, not provable at any length. That last clause is a borrowed fact, this page's one imported theorem: A1 + A2 + MP derive exactly the intuitionistic tautologies, and Peirce's law is the emblem of what intuitionism declines to prove — a tautology the rulebook cannot reach because of the rulebook, not because of the formula. Mark it: machine-checked on this page — the byte, the seeds, the closure, the absence. Borrowed on trust — that the absence persists at every depth, a meta-theorem from proof theory. The boundary is the lesson: choosing a rulebook is choosing mathematics. The kernel referees flawlessly within the rules; which truths the rules can reach was decided upstream, by the codebook's author, with no machine to check them.

The funnel, measured for this rulebook too. Candidate formulas per position in a five-line certificate: 1,872 for line 1 (every axiom instance the pool allows), then 1,873, 1,876, 1,881, 1,888 — at each position, every ordered pair of accepted lines licenses an MP-application, and the counts climb gently as the certificate grows. The product is the prover's search space:

\[1872 \times 1873 \times 1876 \times 1881 \times 1888 \;\approx\; 2.34 \times 10^{16},\]

twenty-three quadrillion candidates for the prover to wander; five reads for the kernel to referee whichever one arrives.

Example 3 · The industrial scale

Moves used: P25 the census too big for one human · P01 the pinning at the scale of a book · P34 the codebook as a decade-long project.

Stretch the toy to see the seams. A certificate for \(2 + 3 = 5\) — \((SS0+SSS0)=SSSSS0\) — runs ten lines, the same four rules, the same local reads: one more successor to peel, three more lines to chain; the kernel costs ten reads and notices nothing else. Its doctored twin — the A2 instance at line 8 written for the wrong \(u\) — dies at line 8, same reason, same refusal. Nothing about the method changed between seven lines and ten; nothing changes between ten and ten thousand except the human's ability to do what the kernel does. That is the scaling argument, and it is the whole argument: the kernel's cost is linear in the certificate, the prover's search is exponential in the theorem, and the human referee sits somewhere past ten thousand lines — over the horizon, permanently.

Now the real thing. The odd-order theorem — every finite group of odd order is solvable — was proved in 1963 by Feit and Thompson: 255 journal pages, one of the longest proofs its era had seen, refereed by humans over years and absorbed by a field. Between 2006 and 2012 a team of about 15 people led by Gonthier translated the entire proof into Coq: six years of work, roughly 150,000 lines of certificate covering some 4,000 definitions and 13,000 lemmas and theorems. The prover was human — mathematicians doing the finding, the global search, the hard part — exactly as in §2, where you were the prover and the clerk was the kernel. And the checking: the Coq kernel read all 150,000 lines, every justification, every citation, and accepted. Nobody on the team could hold the whole proof in mind; nobody needed to. The kernel's verdict does not rest on anyone's attention span — which is the entire point, and the reason Gonthier could say of the smaller four-color formalization that it is "only necessary to trust the Coq kernel" for the rest of the edifice to stand.

The same receipt, at the scale of a living library. Lean's mathematical library mathlib — built by 700-plus contributors — carries roughly 300,000 theorems and over 100,000 definitions in about 2.5 million lines of formal text, and every one of those theorems is a line the kernel accepted; the kernel at the bottom of it all is a few thousand lines of code. The census is too big for any human referee — no one has read all of mathlib — and yet its status is more certain than any informally refereed corpus of comparable size, because the referee is small, local, and ran on every line. And the pattern reached working mathematicians' live research in 2020, when Scholze challenged the community to certify the key theorem of his liquid vector spaces work; eighteen months and some twenty contributors later, the proof was complete and machine-checked — research-grade mathematics, refereed the year it was produced, not decades after.

What changed between the toy and the industry? Not the shape — prover, certificate, kernel, verdict, Fig P44.1 unchanged. Only the prover grew: from you, to a team of fifteen, to a global community. The kernel stayed small. That asymmetry is the discipline: the trusted base does not grow with the project.

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits

The kernel is honest, tireless, and narrow-sighted: it certifies exactly what the certificate says, against exactly the rulebook it was handed. Every failure of formal verification in practice lives in that narrowness — not in the checking, which never errs within the rules, but at the three edges where the rules meet the world. Exhibit A: the statement. Exhibit B: the kernel itself. Exhibit C: the price.

Exhibit A · The statement is the hole

Here is a way to be machine-certified and wrong that no kernel can prevent. A friend announces: "I have a formal proof that the sum of two even numbers is even — machine-checked, kernel-verified, bulletproof." You ask to see the certificate's final line. It reads \(2 \mid (x \cdot y)\): two divides the product. The kernel accepted every line — and the kernel is blameless, because the kernel certified that statement, while your friend claimed a different one. The hole is not in the proof; it is in the translation between the sentence a human cares about and the formula the kernel read. This is P34's decode step returning as a threat: whatever is pinned down can be mis-pinned, and the kernel — which sees only the pinning — cannot notice.

The industrial lesson is the same lesson, learned expensively. CompCert, the verified C compiler, is a genuine triumph — every step from C source to machine code carries a certificate, and the compiler's output is proved to match the input's meaning. But notice what is not in the certificate: the meaning of C itself. That lives in the formal semantics the proof is pinned to — a semantics the researchers chose, wrote down, and vouched for, by hand, the old way. If the semantics mis-describes the hardware — if the machine drains a register the semantics thought stable — the kernel's verdict is untouched and the compiled code is still wrong. The kernel says: this program refines this specification. It has always been somebody's job, outside the machine, to check that the specification is the one you meant. In Exhibit A's costume: the proof that \(2 \mid (x \cdot y)\) is worth exactly what the claim "\(x\) and \(y\) are even" is worth — no more.

So P01's discipline is not optional ceremony at the front of the move; it is the move's permanent soft flank. Pinning the problem — statement, definitions, exactly what would count as an answer — is upstream of the kernel, and nothing mechanical referees it. The honest formal-methods papers say this out loud: a proof is relative to a specification, and the specification is trust, in writing.

Exhibit B · The kernel is code

§2's third question returns: who referees the referee? The kernel is a program; programs have bugs; a buggy kernel can accept a certificate the rulebook forbids. Do not take this on faith — build the bug. Take the kernel of Example 1 and delete one clause from its T-check: it still fetches both cited lines and still checks the middle terms match, but it no longer checks that the new line's conclusion is the chained conclusion. A one-clause deletion, the kind a tired programmer makes. Now feed it a certificate for \(2 + 2 = 5\): the same seven lines as §2, except line 7 writes \((SS0+SS0)=SSSSS0\) while citing lines 5 and 6 — exactly poison three from Example 1's battery.

The honest kernel says: REJECT, line 7: T conclusion mismatch. The broken kernel says: ACCEPT. Same certificate, same rulebook on paper, opposite verdicts — and one of the two kernels is lying about the arithmetic. The lesson is precise: the rulebook is not the referee; the code is the referee, and the certificate's meaning is only as good as the match between the two. This is why the discipline has two load-bearing habits, both already on this page. First, smallness (the de Bruijn criterion): a kernel of a few thousand lines can be read by humans, argued about, tested — a kernel of a million lines cannot, and would just move the trust problem. Second, independence: two kernels, written by different people from the same public rulebook, must agree on every certificate — P25's second-engine discipline, applied to the referee itself.

And this is not hypothetical — the discipline survived a live fire in 2026. In the last week of July, a community hunt for soundness bugs in proof kernels found bug #14576 in the Lean kernel: a proof of False, from no axioms, accepted by the kernel. Everything above it — every theorem in the library, 300,000 strong — was formally still standing and actually suspect, because a kernel that proves False certifies anything. The fix landed within the hour (#14577); the postmortems that followed traced the hole to implementation craft — a reference-counting corner and a stale cryptography library — not to the rulebook. Then the machinery that the whole method exists to serve turned on itself: an AI-driven hunt spread over three weeks in the wreckage, probing the repaired kernel for its siblings; exploits were found and closed; version 4.33.1 shipped on 21 August; and mathlib — all 2.5 million lines — was re-verified against the fixed kernel, with the illicit souvenirs from the broken days (a disproof of Collatz, a proof of Kepler) refiled where they belonged, among the refusals. Notice the shape of the recovery: small kernel, public bug, fast fix, everything re-checked from scratch. That is not the method failing; that is the method's immune system working — the same immune system this page's two-kernel discipline is a miniature of.

Exhibit C · The price: length and reach

Two prices, both payable in advance. The first is length. Count the receipts on this page: seven certificate lines for \(2+2=4\), five for \(p \to p\), ten for \(2+3=5\). The formal certificate is longer than the informal proof — enormously longer, and the ratio survives scale: the standard measurement (Wiedijk's, across a dozen formalized textbooks) runs near four formal lines per informal line, the de Bruijn factor, and a working estimate of one week of expert labor per textbook page formalized. At the top end: 255 journal pages became roughly 150,000 lines and six years for fifteen people. The price is not an accident of immature tooling; it is structural. The informal proof leans on the reader's judgment at every step — "clearly", "similarly", "without loss of generality" — and every one of those leans is a debt the certificate must repay in explicit lines. What the human referee subsidizes with insight, the kernel demands in full, in cash.

The second price is reach, and it is deeper. A rulebook can be sound — everything it proves is true — and still blind: Peirce's law is a tautology, and the A1+A2+MP rulebook will never prove it, at any length, by any prover. Exhibit A said the kernel certifies the statement written; Exhibit B said the kernel is code; Exhibit C says the rulebook itself has a horizon, and the horizon is a mathematical fact you chose when you chose the rules. Intuitionistic logic cannot reach Peirce's law; classical logic can; neither fact is visible from inside the kernel, which referees both rulebooks with equal, blameless competence. The prover searches exponentially; the kernel checks linearly; but what exists to be found was fixed the day the codebook was written. That is the final inversion of the page: the machine's perfect honesty makes the human choices — the statement, the rules, the semantics — stand out in sharper relief, not less. Formalization does not eliminate trust; it concentrates trust into a few small, inspectable places, and charges length for the service.

Scope, honestly. What this page's own machines established, from scratch: every certificate, poison, census, and funnel number above was computed by fresh engines built for this page, cross-checked against each other — the kernel versus the truth table, honest kernel versus broken — and the two-engine receipts (2,082 of 2,082; five refusals; the 150,000-line industry figures) are quoted from the published record, with sources in the page log. What is borrowed on trust: that Peirce's law is unprovable at every length in A1+A2+MP (a proof-theoretic meta-theorem; this page's census only witnesses its absence in the bounded pool); that the industrial rulebooks — Coq's, Lean's — are sound for real mathematics (argued by their designers, audited by their communities, never machine-checked from nothing, as Exhibit B's 2026 fire made vivid); and everything upstream of proof entirely: statement fit, kernel correctness, hardware. The pattern's honest summary: machines referee proofs; humans referee the frame.

6 · The doors this opens

Door one, the working door: proof at scale — P45. This page built the referee and showed the funnel; the next page walks through it. What does it take to find the certificate in a space of \(10^{16}\) candidates — not for \(p \to p\), but for the odd-order theorem? The prover's craft is its own discipline: tactics, automation, the art of choosing lemmas so the search never leaves the road. The kernel made checking cheap; the open problem, twenty years running, is making finding less dear.

Door two, the far door: transfer — P49. The ultras move builds a world that looks just like ours from the outside and differs underneath, and a theorem (Łoś) that truth moves between the two. That machinery is pure model theory on paper — but its certificate is a proof like any other, and the community that formalized Scholze's theorem has formalized the surrounding logic too. The door this opens: the patterns atlas's most abstract move, machine-checked, with the kernel reading every transfer step. When a theorem's only referee was "the proof is standard", the kernel becomes the referee the standard never had.

Door three, the widening door: the audit habit, everywhere. P25 demanded a second engine before you trust a count; this page promotes the demand to an entire methodology. The next time you produce something long and checkable — a census, a search, a table, a proof, a program — the pattern asks: what is the certificate, what is the kernel, and who wrote the second one? Most everyday work will never meet a proof assistant. But the pattern's transferable core — make the artifact checkable by something smaller and stupider than its maker, then let two somethings check it — is the discipline of P25 and P34 grown up: any computation you cannot explain to a clerk is a computation you are asking your reader to take on faith.

7 · Where the move came from

The idea has a birth year and a birth name: 1967, Automath, N. G. de Bruijn — the Eindhoven mathematician who asked whether a language could be built so precise that its correctness could be checked mechanically, and answered by building it. The kernel-centric architecture — small checker, untrusted everything else — is his, which is why the smallness standard carries his name. The first landmark: 1976, when van Benthem Jutting finished translating Landau's Foundations of Analysis — the first book ever machine-checked, line by line, its famous terseness repaid in de Bruijn factor.

Then the lineage branches. 1972: Milner's LCF at Stanford, built on Dana Scott's 1969 logic — the first system to make the kernel-versus-tactics split explicit, and the direct ancestor of every HOL to follow. 1973: Trybulec presents Mizar, the Polish line, a different design with the same spine. 1984: Coquand and Huet begin Coq (released 1989; now Rocq), the calculus-of-constructions line; 1986: Paulson's Isabelle, the generic-rulebook line. These systems matured through the 1990s alongside the first formalization casualties and triumphs — the decades in which the de Bruijn factor was measured, textbook by textbook.

The proof landmarks carry the story. 1976: Appel and Haken's four-color proof — P25's census — forced the community to ask what a machine census is as proof; the 1997 Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas revision cut the census to 633 configurations. 2005: Gonthier and Werner certify the four-color theorem inside Coq — the machine census now itself machine-checked, the snake eating its tail in the good way, and Gonthier's remark that it is "only necessary to trust the Coq kernel" dates from here. 2009: two certificates below mathematics — Leroy's CompCert compiler (Clight to PowerPC, every phase verified) and the seL4 microkernel (8,700 lines of C, full functional correctness, the first operating-system kernel with a certificate). 2012: the odd-order theorem, Example 3's centerpiece, six years and 150,000 lines. 2014: the Kepler conjecture's Flyspeck project completes — two independent proof assistants, Isabelle and HOL Light, sharing the certificate — and the work was accepted by a journal three years later, the first computer-assisted proof whose publication went through a formal referee. 2020–2022: Scholze's liquid tensor experiment brings the method to live research mathematics, as told in Example 3.

The modern era is Lean: de Moura's system, begun 2013, and mathlib, its community library, begun 2017 — now the largest formal corpus in history, and the site of the 2026 soundness fire of Exhibit B. Notice what the whole lineage shares: every system is a rulebook plus a kernel plus a crowd of untrusted provers, and every landmark is a certificate someone wanted badly enough to pay the de Bruijn factor. The move is fifty-nine years old and still growing, because the asymmetry it exploits — checking is cheap, finding is dear — is not a fact about software. It is a fact about proof.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ · Referee a ten-line certificate

Here is a certificate for \(2 + 3 = 5\) — \((SS0+SSS0)=SSSSS0\) — in the rulebook of §2:

LineEquationJustification
1\((SS0+0)=SS0\)A1 with \(t := SS0\)
2\((SS0+S0)=S(SS0+0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := 0\)
3\(S(SS0+0)=SSS0\)Rule S on line 1
4\((SS0+S0)=SSS0\)Rule T on lines 2 and 3
5\((SS0+SS0)=S(SS0+S0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := S0\)
6\(S(SS0+S0)=SSSS0\)Rule S on line 4
7\((SS0+SS0)=SSSS0\)Rule T on lines 5 and 6
8\((SS0+SSS0)=S(SS0+SS0)\)A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := SS0\)
9\(S(SS0+SS0)=SSSSS0\)Rule S on line 7
10\((SS0+SSS0)=SSSSS0\)Rule T on lines 8 and 9

(a) Referee it, line by line, kernel-style: for each line say what was checked, and name the \(t\) and \(u\) of every axiom instance. (b) Here is the doctored twin — same certificate, except line 8 reads \((SS0+SSS0)=S(SS0+S0)\) while still claiming A2 with \(t := SS0,\ u := SS0\). Where does it die, and what is the kernel's reason?

Hint

One line, one question — never look ahead. For the twin: recall what one substitution means in an axiom instance, and compare the twin's line 8 against the pattern it claims, side by side.

Worked answer

(a) L1: A1 at \(t = SS0\). L2: A2 at \(t = SS0\), \(u = 0\). L3: S on L1, costume \(S(SS0) = SSS0\). L4: T on L2, L3 — middle terms agree at \(S(SS0+0)\). L5: A2 at \(t = SS0\), \(u = S0\). L6: S on L4. L7: T on L5, L6 — middle terms agree at \(S(SS0+S0)\). L8: A2 at \(t = SS0\), \(u = SS0\): left \((SS0+S(SS0))\), right \(S(SS0+SS0)\). L9: S on L7. L10: T on L8, L9 — middle terms agree at \(S(SS0+SS0)\). ACCEPT, ten reads. (b) It dies at line 8: REJECT, line 8: A2 instance mismatch. Concretely: with \(t = SS0\), \(u = SS0\), A2 demands right side \(S(t + u) = S(SS0+SS0)\), but the twin wrote \(S(SS0+S0)\), the right side for \(u = S0\). One substitution must cover both sides; the twin used two. Exactly the §2 doctoring, one peel deeper.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · Build your own certificate

(a) Build, from a blank page, a ten-line certificate for \(1 + 3 = 4\) — \((S0+SSS0)=SSSS0\) — that the kernel accepts: choose every axiom instance, every citation, in order. (b) The saturation census of Example 1 claims \(1 + 3 = 5\) appears nowhere, at any depth. Explain why, in terms of what the rules preserve. (c) Write the honest boundary sentence: what exactly does the census witness about \(1 + 3 = 5\), and what part of your (b) argument is the bounded census unable to check by itself?

Hint

For (a): imitate Exercise 1's architecture — peel the three successors of \(SSS0\) one at a time, and watch which earlier line each new S-step wraps. For (b): each axiom instance is a true equation about ordinary numbers, and each rule maps true equations to true equations — what does a line count induction then give? For (c): a census inside a size bound witnesses absence inside that bound; ask what has to be true about the rulebook itself for the absence to extend beyond.

Worked answer

(a) L1: \((S0+0)=S0\), A1 at \(t = S0\). L2: \((S0+S0)=S(S0+0)\), A2 at \(t = S0\), \(u = 0\). L3: \(S(S0+0)=SS0\), S on L1. L4: \((S0+S0)=SS0\), T on L2, L3. L5: \((S0+SS0)=S(S0+S0)\), A2 at \(t = S0\), \(u = S0\). L6: \(S(S0+S0)=SSS0\), S on L4. L7: \((S0+SS0)=SSS0\), T on L5, L6. L8: \((S0+SSS0)=S(S0+SS0)\), A2 at \(t = S0\), \(u = SS0\). L9: \(S(S0+SS0)=SSSS0\), S on L7. L10: \((S0+SSS0)=SSSS0\), T on L8, L9. ACCEPT. The census's depth receipt is visible in the flat lines: the A2 instances L8 (\(u := SS0\)), L5 (\(u := S0\)), and L2 (\(u := 0\)) are the certificate's three nested peels — to prove \(S0 + SSS0\) the prover first needs \(S0 + SS0\), which needs \(S0 + S0\), which bottoms out at \(S0 + 0\). Three nested A2 steps, one per successor of the second addend: exactly depth 3, as the census promised. (b) A1 and A2 are true equations of ordinary addition, whatever the substitution; rule S maps a true equation to a true equation; rule T chains two true equations into a true one. So by induction on the line count, every accepted line is true — the kernel cannot certify a falsehood, and \(1 + 3 = 5\) is false, so no certificate for it exists at any length. (c) The census witnesses absence within its world: no provable equation of the 225 equals \(1+3=5\). The extension to "at any depth" rides on the soundness argument of (b) — that every rule preserves truth at every size — which is a proof about the rulebook, read and refereed by humans, not re-run by the census engine. The boundary sentence: the machine witnesses bounded absence; the human argument carries the unboundedness.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The propositional census, and a boundary tested

(a) Rebuild the census of Example 2 from scratch: the depth-\(\leq 1\) pool of 12 formulas, the depth-\(\leq 2\) pool of 156, the truth-table byte of each, and the count of tautologies among the 156 — you should land on 54. (b) Rebuild the kernel's side: 1,872 seeds, the MP closure to 2,082, and the audit of all 2,082 against the truth tables — you should land on zero exceptions, and Peirce's law \(\big((p \to q) \to p\big) \to p\) outside the closure. (c) Now amend the rulebook: add a third axiom rule A3 whose instances are exactly Peirce's law, and rerun both censuses. What happens to the closure, what happens to the audit, and what happens to the boundary — and what does the amended audit still not prove?

Hint

For (a): the pool arithmetic is multiplicative — 3 atoms, then \(3 + 3 \times 3 = 12\), then \(12 + 12 \times 12 = 156\); a truth table over three atoms is eight rows, one byte. For (b): seeds are instances over the depth-\(\leq 1\) pool — \(12^2\) of A1, \(12^3\) of A2 — and the closure is a fixed point: apply MP until no new formula appears. For (c): A3's instances are tautologies too, so ask where the new theorems can come from and where the audit's guarantee comes from.

Worked answer

(a) Depth 0: \(p, q, r\). Depth \(\leq 1\): the 3 atoms plus \(a \to b\) for all 9 ordered pairs — 12 formulas. Depth \(\leq 2\): those 12 plus \(a \to b\) over all 144 ordered pairs — 156. Each formula gets its eight-row truth table; sorting the bytes finds 54 that never take a false value — the tautologies. (b) A1 instances: \(a \to (b \to a)\) over all \(12 \times 12\) pairs — 144. A2 instances: \(\big(a \to (b \to c)\big) \to \big((a \to b) \to (a \to c)\big)\) over all \(12^3 = 1{,}728\) triples. Seeds: 1,872. Closing under MP — match every \(a \to b\) against every \(a\), keep every new \(b\), repeat — reaches a fixed point at 2,082 formulas; evaluating all 2,082 byte-wise finds zero non-tautologies. Peirce's law evaluates to byte 255 (tautology) and appears nowhere in the closure: soundness witnessed, reach bounded. (c) With A3 added, the seeds grow by the instances of Peirce's law over the pool, the closure climbs past 2,082, and Peirce's law itself enters the theorems — the boundary moves, exactly as Exhibit C promised: the horizon was a fact about the rulebook, not about the formulas. The audit still finds zero exceptions, because A3's instances are tautologies and MP preserves truth — the same argument as before, clause added. What the amended audit does not prove: completeness — that the rulebook now proves every tautology of the implication language. Within the bounded pool you can check it (and it holds); at all depths it is a borrowed meta-theorem — with Peirce's law added, the rulebook becomes complete for classical implicational logic, the receipt being precisely that the boundary had to move somewhere and the tautologies have nowhere left to hide. Machine-checked: the pools, bytes, closures, and the audit. Borrowed: the all-depths clause, in both directions, as ever.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The diagnosis — certified, and wrong

A friend announces a result: "The sum of two even numbers is even — and it's not a sketch, I have it machine-checked. The kernel accepted every line of the certificate." You ask to see the final line of the certificate. It reads \(2 \mid (x \cdot y)\), under the hypotheses \(x = 2a\) and \(y = 2b\). The kernel did accept every line; you may assume the rulebook is sound and the kernel honest. (a) What, exactly, is wrong? (b) Is the certified statement itself even the right shape for the claim — and what is the correct pinned statement? (c) Who or what is to blame, and what single habit would have caught this before the announcement?

Worked answer

(a) The certificate establishes \(2 \mid (x \cdot y)\) for \(x = 2a\), \(y = 2b\) — the product of two evens is even. True, machine-checked, and beside the point: the claim was the sum \(x + y\). Nothing is wrong inside the proof; the hole is in the translation from the sentence the friend meant to the formula the kernel read — Exhibit A, live. (b) The certified statement is the wrong shape twice over: wrong connective (product for sum), and even for the product it is weak — \(x \cdot y = 4ab\) is divisible by 4, not just 2. The correct pinned statement is \(2 \mid (2a + 2b)\), or unfolded, \(2a + 2b = 2(a+b)\): the sum, factored, even. (c) The kernel is blameless — it certified exactly what was written, as kernels do; the blame is the unrefereed decode step, the move from meaning to formula that no machine checks. The habit that catches it: read the certificate's final line aloud and ask whether it is the sentence you would say to a colleague — P01's pinning, applied at the end of the formalization, not just the beginning. The pattern's standing warning: certified certifies the writing; the meaning is still on you.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The regress, and where it stops

Exhibit B broke the kernel's T-check one clause at a time. Continue the experiment. (a) Break a different clause — say, remove the rule that a citation must point to an already-accepted line — and construct a certificate the broken kernel accepts and the honest kernel rejects. (b) You now distrust kernels, so you write a second checker. What must differ between the two implementations for the agreement to mean anything, and what must stay the same? (c) The regress arrives: who checks the checker of the checker? Argue that the regress can be made to stop — and name what, at the bottom, it stops on.

Hint

For (a): a citation pointing forward can license a line whose premises were never earned — try certifying \(2 + 2 = 5\) with line 7 citing a line 8 that hands it the missing conclusion. For (b): independence is about independent failure modes — think authors, encodings, languages; the rulebook is the one thing both must implement identically. For (c): the de Bruijn criterion and the two-engine discipline are not arbitrary tastes; ask what a human can actually do with a kernel small enough to read, and what is left when two independent implementations agree and the rulebook is public.

Worked answer

(a) With the accepted-lines rule removed, write line 7 as \((SS0+SS0)=SSSSS0\), justify it by T citing lines 5 and 8, and let line 8 be anything with the right middle term — say \(S(SS0+S0)=SSSSS0\), "justified" later. The broken kernel fetches both, checks the middles match, and accepts \(2+2=5\); the honest kernel refuses at line 7 — cites an unaccepted line — because nothing after the current line exists yet. Every clause in the kernel is load-bearing, and each removal is a different way to certify a falsehood. (b) What must differ: the authors, the code, the internal representation — string versus tree, different language, different memory discipline — so that a bug in one is unlikely to be a bug in the other; this is why the cross-check of Example 2 (kernel versus truth table) is so strong: they share no representation at all. What must stay the same: the rulebook — the one public specification both implement — and the certificate format. Agreement between two implementations of one public spec is evidence; agreement between two copies of one program is nothing. (c) The regress stops by running out of stairs to fall down: the rulebook is public and simple enough to argue about; the kernel is small enough to read (the de Bruijn criterion — a few thousand lines, refereeable by humans in a reasonable lifetime); independent implementations agree on every certificate, honest and poisoned; and the 2026 soundness fire showed the whole loop working on a real system — bug found, fix published, corpus re-verified from scratch. At the bottom remains what was always there: the human reading of the rulebook, the semantics pinned to the world, the hardware executing the checks. The regress does not bottom out because trust is eliminated; it bottoms out because trust is concentrated — from "please referee 150,000 lines" to "please read these three thousand, and the rules they implement." That concentration, not certainty, is what the pattern sells — and it is the same trade every page of this atlas has offered: less to trust, made small enough to see.
