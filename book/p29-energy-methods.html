The Patterns Atlas · P29 · the fifth move of tier 3

Energy methods

When the question is qualitative — does it settle, is the forecast unique, does it blow up — you do not owe anyone the solution. Pair the field with one number that only moves one way, keep that number's books, and the books answer for the whole shape.

New word: energy. In this page's dialect, an energy is a single number that prices an entire field of numbers at once: the sum of squares of all the readings, \(E = \sum_i u_i^2\) on a grid or \(E = \int u^2\) along a continuum. The name is borrowed from physics on purpose — heat, waves, and voltages all carry quadratic budgets that the world accounts exactly — but the contract is purely mathematical: whatever the field does, this one number measures its size and ignores its arrangement. A one-sided quantity is an energy that the process can only push one way: it falls (or only rises, or only stays) at every step, forever. P09 taught the two-sided version — the invariant that never changes. One-sidedness is the stronger certificate: a quantity that only falls cannot fall forever without hitting zero or forcing something dramatic, and watching where it must go answers questions about the field without ever solving for the field.

1 · Before we begin

P09 taught the habit of asking what a process does not change — and showed that the answer, kept honestly, is a lever that pins the outcome without tracking the path. P26 taught the writing of equations of change, and its own rod ended with a passing observation: with the ends held at zero, the sum of squares \(\sum_i u_i^2\) of the slabs' temperatures fell at every step, five hundred steps in a row, with nothing forcing it to. P11 taught that a claim meeting an impossible book is dead on arrival. And P15 taught that a rate bounded from one side traps the answer — no solving required.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P26's answer to "how fast" was a closed form, and closed forms are a luxury: most equations worth writing cannot be solved at all, and even P26's rod needed Fourier's whole apparatus to write its solution out exactly. Meanwhile the questions we actually ask of an evolving field are shockingly modest. Does the rod cool to nothing, or can heat slosh forever? Is the forecast unique — could a second, equally honest run disagree? How wrong can step 100 be, given that the start was measured only to a tenth of a degree? Does the reactor run away — and by when, at the latest? Not one of these asks for the solution. Each asks for a verdict. And there is a certificate that delivers verdicts without solving anything: pair the field with a number that only moves one way, and read the verdict out of the number's books.

2 · Meet the problem

Take P26's rod again: twelve slabs, ends held at zero, started at the hot arch \(u_i(0) = 100\sin(\pi i/13)\) — a ledger of exactly 65,000. Ask a forecasting question with money on it: after one hundred steps of the rod's averaging, what does slab 6 read? Run the arithmetic — a hundred honest steps — and the answer is \(5.2017^\circ\). Call this forecaster A. But a second forecaster, B, runs her own hundred steps and reports \(5.7017^\circ\): half a degree higher at slab 6, identical on the other eleven (Fig P29.1). "A rounding difference," says B. "Sloppy arithmetic," says A. Both present complete, plausible runs. Who is right — and can you tell without re-running a hundred steps, without trusting either one's arithmetic, and without a closed form for the answer?

The instinct from P11 is to hunt for the impossible book. So keep one. With the ends held at zero, the ledger \(E = \sum_i u_i^2\) falls at every averaging step — and the fall is not luck but an identity: each step's change in \(E\) is a sum of squares with a minus sign in front, provable in two lines of algebra that §4 finally shows. This is the one-sided quantity of the onramp, and the rod has been keeping its books all along.

Now audit the two forecasts. If A and B ran the same rod equation, then their difference — A's profile minus B's, slab by slab — is itself a run of the same equation: each slab's step depends only on its neighbors, so the difference of two runs steps exactly like a run. Both forecasters claim the same start, so the difference starts at exactly zero. And a run started at exactly zero stays at exactly zero, forever — the averaging of nothing is nothing. Yet B's profile differs from A's by half a degree at slab 6, which forces the difference's ledger to read at least \(0.5^2 = 0.25\) at step 100. A number that started at zero, is forbidden to rise, and yet reads 0.25: impossible. B's run is wrong, and the certificate never opened anyone's arithmetic. That is an audit — a contradiction in the books, priced by a one-sided number.

B has one honest defense left: "perhaps my start differed — say I measured slab 6 a full degree high." Fine; price that too. A difference started with a one-degree error at one slab carries \(E_w(0) = 1\), and the ledger falls with a known worst-case rate — every shape falls at least as fast as the slowest shape, the arch — so by step 100, \(E_w \le \cos^{200}(\pi/13) = 2.75 \times 10^{-3}\), with the actual arithmetic giving \(8.3 \times 10^{-4}\). And a ledger prices pointwise size as well: a profile with books \(E_w\) can read no higher than \(\sqrt{E_w}\) anywhere. So any disagreement at step 100 that started from a one-degree error is bounded by \(\sqrt{E_w(100)} \le 0.029^\circ\). A one-degree error at the start cannot buy half a degree at step 100; it buys three hundredths. The truth is unique inside a certified corridor 0.029 degrees wide, B's claim sits seventeen times outside it — and nobody, at any point, solved the rod.

3 · Name the move

§2 convicted a forecast with a number that was never allowed to rise. The move, named:

Pattern P29 — Energy methods. When the question about an evolving field is qualitative — does it settle, is the forecast unique, how wrong can it be, does it blow up, by when — do not solve for the field. Pair the field with a single number that the dynamics only push one way, keep that number's books exactly, and read the verdict out of the books: a quantity that only falls cannot fall past zero (settling, boundedness), cannot fall while two runs pretend to agree (uniqueness), cannot fall faster than its slowest shape (rates and envelopes), and cannot be forced to fall forever without something giving way (blow-up deadlines). The number is called an energy; the books are two lines of algebra; the verdict is a theorem.

Or, in words: find the quantity that only moves one way, and watch where it must go. Do not ask "what is the field doing" — ask "what is its ledger allowed to do". The answer to the second settles the first.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — a quantity pairs with the dynamics. You can name a number \(E\) — a sum of squares, a count, a weighted total — whose rate the equation signs on sight: substituting the equation into the rate of \(E\) yields something of fixed sign, a sum of squares with a minus in front. Jam-mode: the equation signs nothing you have tried — then the field genuinely moves both ways, and the pairing must be rebuilt around what the dynamics truly conserves (§5's Exhibit B shows the jam and its repair).

Key 2 — the rate survives shifting by parts. Computing the rate of \(E\) means moving derivatives (or differences) off one factor and onto the other — summation by parts on the grid, integration by parts along the continuum — and the leftover boundary terms must die against the setup: walls held at zero, probability summing to one, decay at infinity. Jam-mode: the boundary terms survive and carry material in and out — then \(E\) is transported, not one-sided, and the honest certificate is a balance sheet, not a verdict.

Key 3 — what you discard can be signed. Turning "the rate is negative" into a rate you can use ends by throwing terms away — "the integral of \(u^3\) is at least \(E^{3/2}\)", "every shape falls at least as fast as the arch" — and every discard must point the safe way: throwing away more must still leave the conclusion standing, with the equality case named. Jam-mode: the inequality reverses and the discarded terms could dominate — then the certificate prices nothing, and §5's Exhibit C shows a ledger blind exactly where a budget was wanted.

Box P29.A — the ledger, in three obligations. Every energy argument is earned the same three-step way, and each step carries its own proof duty:

1 · Pair. Choose \(E\) to pair with the equation: on the grid, \(E = \sum_i u_i^2\); for two rival forecasts, pair their difference; for a reactor, pair the ledger with the growth term. Duty: state the pairing before any computing, and be honest about what it measures — size, never arrangement. A ledger that cannot tell a smooth arch from a sawtooth is not a defect of the bookkeeping; it is the bookkeeping.

2 · Shift by parts. Compute the rate by moving the second derivative across the product. On the grid: \(\sum_i u_i \, (u_{i-1} - 2u_i + u_{i+1}) = -\sum_i (u_i - u_{i-1})^2\), walls at zero. In the continuum: \(\int u\,u_{xx} = -\int u_x^2\), ends held. The rate comes out a negative square: one-sidedness as an identity, not an observation. Duty: account for every boundary term at the wall — this is where Dirichlet ends, probability conservation, or decay at infinity gets spent.

3 · Sign the discards. To make the rate usable, compare it with powers of \(E\) itself: Hölder's inequality trades \(\int u^3\) up from \(E^{3/2}\); the shape analysis trades every profile down to its slowest shape. Duty: state each inequality with its equality case — constants for Hölder, the single arch for the shape bound — because the equality case is the honesty certificate: it prices exactly how tight the verdict is, and §4's gallery reads the price off it.

Box P29.B — where the move appears. Uniqueness — two solutions' difference pairs with the same equation and dies at zero (§2's audit). Stability — the error's ledger starts at the measurement error and only falls (§2's 0.029° corridor). Settling and rates — the ledger falls at least at the slowest shape's rate: envelopes, half-times, the copper stopwatch (§4). Blow-up — the Jensen floor turns "the ledger cannot fall" into "the ledger cannot exist past \(T_E\)" (§4's reactor). Probability — the meeting ledger counts coincidences exactly, and a diverging sum certifies recurrence (§4's walkers). Schemes — the quantity a discrete scheme really conserves certifies stability, and only damping buys decay (§5's Exhibit B). And the far doors of §6: minima of energies, mountain passes, flows that simplify. The failure signatures are §5's: the certificate points one way in time (Exhibit A), conservation without decay (Exhibit B), size blind to place (Exhibit C).

4 · The move at work

Three workings, one per costume of Box P29.B: a rod (the books, derived, and what they buy), a pair of walkers (a ledger made of probability), and a reactor (the guarantee that runs out of time). Every number below is exact arithmetic — identities holding to machine precision, runs carried to the step — and each is re-derivable by hand where two lines suffice.

Example 1 (the rod) — the books, derived, and what they buy

The pairing, derived. Here at last are §2's two lines. Write \(d_i\) for the thirteen differences across the boundaries (walls included, \(u_0 = u_{13} = 0\)), and \(\Delta u_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(u_{i-1} - 2u_i + u_{i+1})\) for one slab's step. The ledger's change per step is \[E_1 - E_0 \;=\; 2\sum_i u_i \Delta u_i \;+\; \sum_i (\Delta u_i)^2.\] Shift the first sum by parts — the walls kill the boundary terms, and \(\sum_i u_i (u_{i-1} - 2u_i + u_{i+1}) = -\sum_i d_i^2\) — so \[E_1 - E_0 \;=\; -\sum_i d_i^2 \;+\; \sum_i (\Delta u_i)^2.\] Now \(\Delta u_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(d_{i+1} - d_i)\), and since \((a-b)^2 \le 2(a^2 + b^2)\), the moved squares cannot exceed the standing ones: \(\sum (\Delta u_i)^2 \le \sum d_i^2\). The ledger only falls — an identity, not a hope. A sharper book fuses the two terms, \[E_1 - E_0 \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i d_i^2 \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i d_i\,d'_i,\] with \(d'\) the new differences: exact for every profile (it agrees to fifteen digits on fifty random starts), and still negative, because each new difference is the average of two old ones — \(\sum d'^2 \le \sum d^2\) — while Cauchy–Schwarz pins the cross term. Obligations 1 and 2 of Box P29.A, in four displays.

What the books cannot see. The ledger prices size, never arrangement. The arch's sign-alternating twin — the sawtooth \(\sin(12\pi x/13)\), hot and cold on alternating slabs — carries the same ledger as the arch and falls at the same quotient: \(2(1 - \cos(12\pi/13)) = 0.05811636515 = 2(1 - \cos(\pi/13))\). No audit built on \(\sum u_i^2\) can tell a smooth rod from a zigzag of the same budget. This is not a defect; it is the trade the method makes — and §5's Exhibit C shows what the trade costs.

The envelope: a settling theorem. Which profile falls slowest? A pure shape — one wave of P26's decomposition — falls at its own fixed quotient \(2(1-\cos(k\pi/13))\), and the slowest is the arch, \(k = 1\). So for every start, \[E(n) \;\le\; E_0 \cos^{2n}(\pi/13),\] the envelope: obligation 3, with the arch as the named equality case. The books confirm both halves: the arch itself rides the envelope to \(2.5 \times 10^{-14}\) at step 50, while a mixed, noisy start rides strictly beneath it for 500 straight steps, down to 0.920 of the envelope at step 100 — faster than the worst case, exactly as the inequality demands (Fig P29.2).

What the envelope buys. Settling, with a date: the ledger falls below one percent of its start by step 79 at the latest (\(\ln 100\) divided by the rate \(0.0589\)). Half-times, with a factor: the ledger halves exactly twice as fast as the amplitude — squaring doubles the exponent — so P26's copper rod, whose arch halves in 158.2 seconds, sees its ledger halve in 79.1. And settling to the hundredth, in real units: a copper rod of half a meter drops its ledger a hundredfold by \(t = \ln(100) L^2/(2\alpha\pi^2) = 525.5\) seconds — the simulated hundred-cell rod sits at 0.9996 percent of its start there, inside the promised one percent with grid slack to spare. Stability, with a budget: an error of \(\delta\) degrees in the start is worth at most \(\delta \cos^{100}(\pi/13) = 0.0525\,\delta\) at step 100 — the envelope-only corridor of §2 — and the actual arithmetic of §2's one-degree probe tightens it to \(0.029\,\delta\). Uniqueness, uniqueness of the corridor: every honest run, however sloppily started, is trapped in it together.

One-sidedness is a property of the pair, not of the physics. Solve the mug's cooling with crude forward Euler and the ledger \(E = (T - A)^2\) falls by the factor \((1 - 2k\,\Delta t)\) per step: at \(\Delta t = 30\) minutes the factor is \(0.0751\) and the books still close; at \(\Delta t = 1/k\) it is exactly \(-1\) and the ledger alternates — two-sided, certifying nothing; at \(\Delta t = 100\) it is \(-2.08\) and the scheme's ledger grows while the physics still decays. The certificate must be re-earned per scheme, and when it is earned, the discrete books are self-consistent to the digit: Euler's ledger at \(t = 100\) minutes reads 224.4160155 against its own closed form to \(3 \times 10^{-15}\), while drifting from the physics by a disclosed \(4.75 \times 10^{-4}\) — the scheme's honest price, shrinking with the step.

Moves used: P09 the invariant, made one-sided · P26 the equation whose books are kept · P15 the rate trapped under an envelope · P25 the runs, budgets, and stopwatch carried to the digit.

Example 2 (the walkers) — a ledger made of probability

The ledger as overlap. Two walkers leave the same origin, each flipping an honest coin per step. Write \(p_n(x)\) for the chance one walker stands at \(x\) after \(n\) steps. They meet at step \(n\) exactly when both stand at the same \(x\), so the meeting chance is the overlap \[M(n) \;=\; \sum_x p_n(x)^2,\] a ledger in every disguise — here the "energy" is probability mass and the falling quantity is the chance of a rendezvous.

One-sidedness, same lemma as the rod. Each step replaces \(p_n\) by the average of its two neighbors, and the same \((a+b)^2 \le 2(a^2+b^2)\) that felled the rod's ledger fells this one: \(M(n+1) \le M(n)\), always. The meeting chance only shrinks — and the exact book is a Vandermonde away: expanding the two binomials gives, digit for digit, \[M(n) \;=\; \binom{2n}{n} \frac{1}{4^n},\] from \(M(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\) on up — the probability of meeting is the probability of a return.

The dictionary. That last sentence is an identity, not a simile. The difference of the two walks — stay with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), jump two with probability \(\tfrac{1}{4}\) each way — has exactly the law of a single walker seen at even times: meeting at step \(n\) is returning at step \(2n\). So the pair's meeting chance at fifty, \(M(50) = 0.0795892\), is the very number P26 computed for the lone walker's return at one hundred — the same digit, by the same binomial, from two different stories. One ledger, two pages.

The rate, priced. Stirling sharpens the binomial: \(M(n) \approx 1/\sqrt{\pi n}\), with the approximation overshooting the exact value by just 0.250, 0.125, and 0.063 percent at \(n = 50, 100, 200\) — halving its error as \(n\) quadruples, exactly as a \(1/\sqrt{n}\) law should. The meeting chance decays, but only like a power — never like an exponential — and that slowness is the whole verdict.

What the rate buys. Sum the ledger: the expected number of meetings by step \(N\) is \(S(N) = \sum_{n \le N} M(n)\). The books read \(S(50) = 7.038\), \(S(100) = 10.326\), \(S(200) = 14.988\), \(S(400) = 21.589\). The leading estimate \(2\sqrt{N/\pi}\) says 15.958 at \(N = 200\); trimming by the known constant term \(-0.824\) lands at 15.134, and the exact sum sits at 0.9903 of that — the ledger prices its own accuracy. And each doubling of the horizon multiplies the take by 1.451, then 1.440, easing toward \(\sqrt{2}\): a divergent sum. The pair does not merely fail to drift apart; it meets, in expectation, infinitely often. Recurrence was P26's verdict for one walker; here the same binomial, read as a ledger, extends it to two — for free.

Example 3 (the reactor) — the guarantee that runs out of time

The law. A rod of reaction, \(u_t = u^2\): every point grows at the rate of its own square — cold spots barely stir, hot spots race away. This law is exactly solvable point to point: \(u(x,t) = u_0(x)/(1 - u_0(x)\,t)\), checkable by differentiating in place. Read the denominator: whichever point starts hottest, say \(100\) degrees on the arch-shaped start, hits the wall of division by zero at \(T^* = 1/\max u_0 = 0.01\) seconds. At \(t = 0.0099\) the top slab already reads \(10{,}000\) degrees — the exact law, to a relative \(4 \times 10^{-8}\). This is blow-up: not divergence of a scheme, but of the physics itself.

The guarantee. Now audit it blind — no shapes, no solving, only the ledger. Take \(E = \int_0^1 u^2\,dx\) and pair as always: \(E' = 2\int_0^1 u^3\,dx\). Sign what is discarded: since \(s \mapsto s^{3/2}\) bends upward, Jensen trades the whole arrangement of the profile for one number, \(\int u^3 = \int (u^2)^{3/2} \ge \left(\int u^2\right)^{3/2} = E^{3/2}\), so \[E' \;\ge\; 2E^{3/2}.\] Shift by parts — in time now: the inequality says \(\left(E^{-1/2}\right)' \le -1\), so \(E^{-1/2}(t) \le E_0^{-1/2} - t\). And there is the cliff: by \(T_E = 1/\sqrt{E_0}\) the right-hand side reaches zero, while the left-hand side — an inverse square root of a positive number — cannot. A contradiction (P11's move, executed by a ledger): no solution of finite temperature can exist past \(T_E\). The books run backward here — the one-sided quantity bounds the ledger from below, and the certificate reads "cannot survive past," the mirror image of the rod's "settles by."

How loose is the promise. The floor is honest: at \(0.3\), \(0.6\), and \(0.9\) of the true lifetime, the actual ledger sits at \(1.13\), \(1.52\), and \(5.50\) times the certified floor. It is sharp where it should be: on a constant profile nothing was discarded, and the ratio is exactly \(1\). On the arch, \(T_E / T^* = \sqrt{2} = 1.414213562\) to every digit shown — the arch holds \(E_0 = 5000\), and \(100/\sqrt{5000}\) is \(\sqrt{2}\) exactly. And across a thousand random profiles the ratio stays between \(1.007\) and \(1.317\): never vacuous, nearly tight whenever the data is spike-like (a spike concentrates its ledger where its maximum lives, \(\sqrt{E}\) close to \(\max u\)), looser when the heat is spread. One number in, one date out (Fig P29.3).

What it buys. A doom date without solving anything — and, when the story grows a rival term, a regime test. Let diffusion fight back, \(u_t = u_{xx} + u^2\): the ledger now carries two signed contributions, one for each term of the pair, and which sign wins is the whole question. An arch scaled to \(a = 0.5\) sees its ledger fall to \(0.690\) of its start after \(0.02\) seconds — diffusion wins, the rod cools. The same arch at \(a = 20\) sits at \(1.436\) times its start — reaction wins, the rod blows. The boundary between those regimes is not a number but a theory, the Fujita threshold, and the ledger is what locates you on either side of it.

Moves used: P09 the one-sided habit, pointed the other way · P11 the contradiction the certificate forces · P26 the ledger, inherited and spent.

5 · When the move fails

Every certificate fails somewhere; the honest page prices its failures. Three exhibits, one per jam-mode of Box P29.A, and each was run, not imagined — the numbers are exact arithmetic.

Exhibit A — run the ledger backward

Undo the rod's averaging — solve the un-smoothing recurrence — and the audit's premise dies: the ledger's one-sidedness is a property of the forward direction, and every inequality flips with the arrow of time. On the mode-2 profile, ten undo-steps leave the ledger at \(11.3936\) times its start, matching \(\cos^{-20}(2\pi/13)\) to every digit shown, and it rises at every single step. The certificate does not merely weaken — it converts: run backward, the honest ledger certifies instability. The continuum version is theatrical: a mode-2 iron rod (\(\alpha = 1\)) read one second into the past has its amplitude multiplied by \(e^{4\pi^2} = 1.397 \times 10^{17}\), its ledger by \(e^{8\pi^2} = 1.95 \times 10^{34}\); two presents differing by a millionth of a degree differed by \(1.4 \times 10^{11}\) degrees a second ago.

What broke: the sign — a property of the pairing, not of the arithmetic, and the inversion itself is real (ten steps forward, ten back, returns to within \(5.5 \times 10^{-9}\) of the start). Mitigation: none inside the backward problem; either state the instability as the theorem — a one-sided certificate, just an ugly one — or accept a smoothed past and audit forward from it. Price: the undo amplifies the mode whose average sits closest to zero — the mid-grid wave at \(k = 6\) — by \(1/\cos(6\pi/13) \approx 8.3\) per step, compounding; no amount of double precision buys a century of rod-time. Default habit: never audit backward; re-pose the question forward, from the smoothed candidate to the present, and audit that.

Exhibit B — the wave that certifies nothing

Hang the same ledger, \(\sum u_i^2\), on P24's undamped string and it certifies nothing: over 2000 leapfrog steps it rises 996 times and falls 1004 times — two-sided. The pairing is at fault: a wave trades energy between displacement and velocity without spending any, and the cross term that the rod's dissipation made negative is here a swinging sign, sometimes source, sometimes sink. Re-pair to what the physics actually conserves — the full energy, kinetic plus potential — and on the staggered grid the honest discrete book is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum v_i^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\,u_{\mathrm{new}}^{\mathsf{T}}(-L)\,u_{\mathrm{prev}}\), a pairing earned, not assumed: it holds to \(9.99 \times 10^{-16}\) of its start over 2000 steps, machine roundoff.

What broke: the pairing, silently assumed instead of earned. Mitigation: re-pair, then re-audit — and when real damping (\(\gamma = 0.01\)) is added, the fresh audit delivers: the damped ledger falls in 20 of 20 checkpoint windows, its tail rate measuring \(-0.01001\) per second against the coefficient's \(-0.01\). Price: what the conserved book buys is exactly stability yes, decay no — decay must be paid for separately, with physics, and certified with its own ledger. Default habit: name the quantity the pairing supports, not the one you wish fell; "energy" is a role, not a name, and on waves the role-player conserves.

Exhibit C — the ledger blind to a spike

The ledger prices size, never location. Tent spikes of half-width \(0.1\), \(0.01\), and \(0.001\) all top out at \(1.000000\) on the thermometer while their ledgers read \(0.0667\), \(0.0067\), \(0.00067\) — within two percent of \(2\varepsilon/3\) each time — and as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks the ledger goes to zero while the worst case does not move. Worse, the blindness is invisible from the books alone: a flat one-degree field and a half-width-\(0.001\) spike carry the same ledger, \(E = 1.000\) to four digits, while their maxima are \(1.0\) and \(38.7\) degrees.

What broke: the discard — Box P29.A's third sign threw away arrangement, and arrangement is exactly where the spike hides its heat. Mitigation: on a fixed grid the ledger still buys one pointwise bound, \(E \ge (\max u)^2 \Delta x\), so \(\max u \le \sqrt{E/\Delta x}\) — honored by all 200 random profiles checked (worst ratio to the bound \(0.0565\); the bound is generous), saturated exactly by the one-cell spike (ratio \(1.000000\)), and almost untouched by the flat field (ratio \(0.0100\), the grid's \(\sqrt{\Delta x}\) to the digit shown). Price: the bound is grid-bought — refine the grid and the same ledger permits ever-taller spikes, and in the continuum no pointwise promise survives; a coarser grid is a blinder ledger, and the ledger itself never changed. Default habit: state the grid with the guarantee; a pointwise promise quoted without its \(\Delta x\) is not a certificate, it is a mood.

Scope, honestly. What P29 gives: a one-sided quantity that turns questions about the long run — where does this settle, can this run forever, how wrong can the start be — into arithmetic on a single number, with dates, rates, and corridors attached. What it never gives: the profile itself. It cannot tell a smooth arch from a same-budget sawtooth (§4), will not run backward (Exhibit A), certifies stability where you wanted decay (Exhibit B), and makes no pointwise promises off a fixed grid (Exhibit C). Those are not bugs to patch; they are the trade the method makes — size in exchange for everything else — and the three obligations of Box P29.A are the terms of that trade, signed before the mathematics begins.

The default habit. When a question says "where does this settle", "can this run forever", or "how wrong can a start be": before solving anything, look for the one-sided quantity. Pair the equation's terms, shift by parts, sign what you discard — and if the signed remainder has a direction, you are holding the answer's skeleton; dates, rates, and corridors fall out of it without ever producing the profile. The method is not a theorem to cite but a reflex to try first, and like P09's invariant it costs one question: what does this process never do?

7 · Lineage

The idea is old enough to have grandchildren. Riemann, in the 1850s, named and used Dirichlet's principle — solve a boundary problem by minimizing an energy integral, on the promise that what is bounded below is attained; Weierstrass, in 1870, broke the promise with a functional whose infimum no function reaches, and it lay broken for thirty years until Hilbert, in 1900, mended it with the direct method — the energy itself made to certify existence, this page's logic in its founding costume. The Poincaré inequality — a ledger bounds the wiggle it contains, the settling-rate constant of §4 and Exhibit C's grid-bound cousin — carries Poincaré's name from the 1890s and generalizes into the scale Sobolev built in the 1930s. Leray, in 1934, met the Navier–Stokes equations head-on and, unable to solve them, did something better: he defined what he could not control, and let the energy inequality carry the theory where derivatives could not go — the ledger as an existence proof for objects that had never been proved to exist. And Fujita, in 1966, made §4's regime test into a theory: for reaction-diffusion \(u_t = \Delta u + u^{1+\alpha}\), a sharp exponent separates data that cools from data that blows, with no size threshold to save you — the guarantee of Example 3, sharpened into a phase boundary. The dates are anchors, not load-bearing: every claim above stands on this page's own mathematics.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — The mug's second ledger. Take P26's calibrated mug (\(k = \tfrac{1}{10}\ln\tfrac{7}{6} = 0.0154\) per minute, room \(20^\circ\), start \(90^\circ\)) and hang this page's ledger on it: \(E = (T - A)^2\). (a) Show the ledger falls at exactly twice the gap's rate constant — that is, \(E(t) = 4900\,e^{-2kt}\). (b) Compute the ledger's half-life and compare it with the gap's half-life of 44.97 minutes. (c) At the gap's half-life, where does the ledger stand?

Hint

Square the gap law: the excess \(T - A\) starts at \(70^\circ\); the ledger is its square. A halved square is a quartered ledger.

Worked answer

(a) \(T - A = 70\,e^{-kt}\), so \(E = (T-A)^2 = 4900\,e^{-2kt}\) — squaring doubles the exponent, which is the whole exercise. (b) The ledger halves when \(e^{-2kt} = \tfrac{1}{2}\): \(t = \ln 2/(2k) = 22.4828\) minutes — exactly half the gap's 44.9656. (c) At \(t = 44.9656\), the gap is \(35^\circ\) and the ledger is \(35^2 = 1225 = 4900/4\): one quarter, exactly — the factor of two in the rate, made visible as a factor of four in the reading.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — A three-slab ledger, by hand. The rod shrunk to three slabs, walls at zero: start \((2, 4, 2)\). (a) Run one averaging step by hand and check the ledger \(24 \to 12\). (b) Run four more steps and write the ledger's whole history. (c) Explain why this particular start halves exactly every step, while a generic start would not.

Hint

(c) The three-slab rod's shapes are \((1, 2, 1)\)-symmetric, \((1, 0, -1)\)-antisymmetric, and \((1, -2, 1)\)-symmetric. Which of these does \((2,4,2)\) contain — and what is each shape's per-step quotient \(2(1-\cos(k\pi/4))\)?

Worked answer

(a) New profile \((\tfrac{4}{2}, \tfrac{2+2}{2}, \tfrac{4}{2}) = (2,2,2)\): ledger \(4+16+4 = 24 \to 4+4+4 = 12\). (b) \((2,2,2) \to (1,2,1) \to (1,1,1) \to (\tfrac{1}{2},1,\tfrac{1}{2}) \to (\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2})\); ledger \(12 \to 6 \to 3 \to 1.5 \to 0.75\) — halving at every single step, to the digit. (c) The start is symmetric, so it contains only the two symmetric shapes, and each carries the per-step energy quotient \(\cos^2(\pi/4) = \tfrac{1}{2}\): one step multiplies the ledger by \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) exactly, and so does every step after. The antisymmetric shape \((1,0,-1)\) has quotient \(\cos^2(2\pi/4) = 0\): any antisymmetric part of a start vanishes in one step — the ledger's blindness to shape (§4), working overtime.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The meeting ledger, summed. Two walkers leave the origin together. (a) Derive the meeting chance \(M(n) = \binom{2n}{n}/4^n\) from the overlap \(M(n) = \sum_x p_n(x)^2\) — the Vandermonde identity does it in two lines. (b) Compute the expected number of meetings by step \(N = 200\) exactly, and compare with the leading estimate \(2\sqrt{N/\pi}\) and with the estimate trimmed by the known constant term \(-0.824\). (c) Show the growth \(S(200)/S(100) = 1.451\), \(S(400)/S(200) = 1.440\), argue that the take grows like \(\sqrt{2}\) per doubling forever, and state the verdict.

Hint

(a) Multiply the two binomials in \(\sum_x p_n(x)^2\) and collect the coefficient of a single power. (c) A sum of terms that each shrink like \(1/\sqrt{n}\) grows like \(\sqrt{N}\); what does that say about the total number of expected meetings as \(N \to \infty\)?

Worked answer

(a) \(p_n(x) = \binom{n}{(n+x)/2}/2^n\) where the sum runs over \(x\) of the right parity; \(\sum_x \binom{n}{\frac{n+x}{2}}\binom{n}{\frac{n+x}{2}} = \binom{2n}{n}\) by Vandermonde, giving \(M(n) = \binom{2n}{n}/4^n\) — hand-check \(M(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\): two one-step walks meet only if they step face-to-face, probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). (b) The exact sum is \(S(200) = 14.988\); the leading estimate says \(2\sqrt{200/\pi} = 15.958\), overshooting by a near-constant gap; trimming by the constant term gives \(15.958 - 0.824 = 15.134\), and the exact sum sits at \(0.9903\) of that — the ledger prices its own accuracy to a fraction of a percent. (c) \(S(50) = 7.038\), \(S(100) = 10.326\), \(S(200) = 14.988\), \(S(400) = 21.589\): the ratios \(1.467, 1.451, 1.440\) ease toward \(\sqrt{2} = 1.414\), exactly as a \(\sqrt{N}\) growth must. The sum diverges, and the verdict is recurrence for two: the pair meets, in expectation, infinitely often — never because either walker aims, only because the meeting chance shrinks too slowly to sum.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the claim (pattern-unknown). A blogger reports: "I ran the same simulation twice — same code, same numbers — and the two runs ended up completely different. Deterministic systems just diverge; chaos means identical runs give different answers." Diagnose. What can you already rule out? What did the blogger almost certainly measure? And what single question would you put to him?

Hint

Two runs of the same deterministic arithmetic on genuinely identical inputs are one run wearing two names. So something was not identical. This page's §2 built the tool for exactly this question.

Worked answer

Rule out first: arithmetic itself. Identical inputs through identical deterministic steps produce identical outputs — §2's difference ledger keeps a zero difference at machine zero for a hundred steps, and no amount of "chaos" changes that, because chaos is deterministic too. What the blogger almost certainly measured: two starts that differed slightly — a rounding at the last decimal, a different summation order, a grid shifted by one cell — inside a process whose difference ledger rises. The numbers of §5's Exhibit A price how fast: run the ledger backward and a millionth of a degree becomes \(1.4 \times 10^{11}\) degrees in one second; undo the rod's averaging and the error grows \(8.3\times\) per step at worst. Forward, well-posed, the difference ledger falls and the outputs stay close (§2's corridor: a one-degree start error is worth at most \(0.029^\circ\) a hundred steps on). The single question: "how close were the two starts, and in which direction does the difference ledger run?" If it falls, the runs cannot have diverged — so the starts were not what he thinks they were. If it rises, "chaos" is not the diagnosis; ill-conditioning is — and the direction of the ledger is the whole story.

Exercise 5 ◐ The damped string, explored (exploration). Take §5's Exhibit B string with damping \(\gamma\) added, \(u_{tt} + \gamma u_t = u_{xx}\). (a) Convince yourself the staggered book needs the \(u_{\mathrm{prev}}\) pairing: what goes wrong pairing \(u_{\mathrm{new}}\) with \(u_{\mathrm{new}}\)? (b) Verify the \(\gamma = 0.01\) readings: 20 of 20 checkpoint windows fall, tail rate \(-0.01001\) against the coefficient's \(-0.01\). (c) Explore: at \(\gamma = 0.5\) the tail measures \(-0.4970\); at \(\gamma = 20\) it measures \(-1.016\), against the slow-mode floor \(2\omega_1^2/\gamma = 0.9822\). Forty times the damping bought about twice the decay. Find, by experiment, the \(\gamma\) that kills the string fastest, and explain the physics of why more damping past it slows the kill.

Hint

(a) Leapfrog holds \(u\) at whole steps and \(v\) at half steps; the potential term is a pairing of two different times. (c) A heavily damped mode decays at the rate \(\omega^2/\gamma\), not \(\gamma\) — write the modal equation \(a'' + \gamma a' + \omega^2 a = 0\) and look at the smaller root when \(\gamma\) is large.

Worked answer

(a) Pair \(u_{\mathrm{new}}\) with itself and the book counts the potential energy of a time the velocity does not belong to; the honest leapfrog book pairs displacement across the step — \(u_{\mathrm{new}}\) against \(u_{\mathrm{prev}}\) — exactly as Exhibit B wrote, and only then is it conserved to roundoff on the undamped string. (b) Both readings reproduce to the digit: the ledger falls at every checkpoint (20 of 20), and the tail's slope measures \(-0.01001\) per second, within a tenth of a percent of the coefficient. (c) The fastest kill sits near critical damping, \(\gamma \approx 2\omega_1 \approx 6.3\) on this grid, where the tail rate approaches its ceiling; beyond it the slow root of \(a'' + \gamma a' + \omega_1^2 a = 0\) is roughly \(-\omega_1^2/\gamma\), so the energy rate is roughly \(2\omega_1^2/\gamma\), which falls as \(\gamma\) grows — an overdamped mode can only drain through the very damping that pins it, and forty times the damping (0.5 → 20) buys about twice the decay (0.4970 → 1.016). The ledger saw all of it before the physics was narrated — which is the point of the page.
