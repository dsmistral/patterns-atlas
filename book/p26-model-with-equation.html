The Patterns Atlas · P26 · the fourth move of tier 3

Model it with an equation

When the question is "how fast", do not fit a curve to the past — write the sentence the physics obeys. The equation is that sentence spelled in rates, and once written, it answers questions your data never saw coming.

New word: differential equation. A differential equation is a sentence about rates of change: "the temperature falls at a rate proportional to how far above the room it is", "the heat flowing through a bar is proportional to how steeply the temperature drops along it". It is an equation because the rate of change of a quantity is written as the derivative of that quantity — the slope-readers of school calculus, now doing honest work. An ordinary differential equation (ODE) has one clock and one state: the coffee's temperature \(T(t)\) is one number per instant, and the equation says how that number moves. A partial differential equation (PDE) has one clock and a whole continuum of positions: the rod's temperature \(u(x, t)\) is a reading at every point, changing in time — and the equation relates the time-rate \(u_t\) to the way the readings vary from point to point. "Partial" because with \(x\) and \(t\) both in play, each derivative holds the other fixed. Nothing else is needed: an equation about rates, a state to obey it, and (this page's real subject) the discipline of earning the right to write it.

1 · Before we begin

P15 taught the move of trapping an answer between two computable things — and, just as importantly, the habit underneath it: when a finite estimate improves without end, the error is squeezed to zero and the limit is certain. P24 taught that a complicated signal is a sum of simple ones — and showed the payoff of naming the pieces before touching the whole. P09 taught the question that opens every honest model: what does the process not change? And P16 taught that two problems sharing one shape are one problem wearing two costumes — a dictionary, honestly built, lets the solved one answer for both.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P25's census can count every case that ever happened; P02's tables can display every measurement you own. But a table has a defect no census fixes: it cannot be asked about tomorrow. Extrapolate a table and you are guessing; interpolate one and you are safe — and the two feel identical, which is the trap. The quadratic through this morning's three coffee readings will reproduce all three measurements to the last digit and then, asked about the afternoon, will cool the coffee to a minimum and start heating it back toward boiling — the fit is perfect and the prophecy is nonsense, and nothing in the data ever said otherwise.

The move is to stop summarizing the data and start encoding the law the data obeys. A law of change is a short sentence in words — the hotter above its surroundings, the faster it cools — and the sentence translates, almost mechanically, into an equation about rates. The equation then carries the present forward by itself: calibrate a parameter or two on measurements you trust, and predictions fall out — including predictions about regimes you have not visited, whose correctness is no longer a guess about curves but a theorem about the sentence. The sentence can still be wrong (§5 gives three ways), but wrong in a checkable, fixable, honorable way: a law that fits everything you have seen and is contradicted by the world is falsified physics, and you have learned something about the world, not just about your pencil.

2 · Meet the problem

A mug of coffee comes off the stove at \(90^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The room is \(20^\circ\mathrm{C}\). You measure the coffee at ten minutes: \(80^\circ\mathrm{C}\). That is the entire dataset — two knowns (room, start) and one reading — and one question with money on it: when can you drink it at \(60^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?

Try it the table way first, because that is the honest baseline. The first ten minutes lost \(10^\circ\), so a friend proposes the linear law \(T = 90 - t\): one degree per minute, forever. Under it, the coffee hits \(60^\circ\) at \(t = 30\) minutes, reaches the room's \(20^\circ\) at \(t = 70\), and continues, dutifully, to \(0^\circ\) at \(t = 90\) — the law predicts a frozen coffee, and the friend does not notice, because the law is silent about where it stops being true. A fit has no vocabulary for that.

Now wait twenty minutes and measure again: \(71.4^\circ\mathrm{C}\) — not the \(70^\circ\) the line promised. Three points determine a parabola, so a second friend fits the quadratic through \((0, 90)\), \((10, 80)\), \((20, 71.43)\), and it is a beauty: \(T(t) = \frac{t^2}{140} - \frac{15t}{14} + 90\) (the lab checks the algebra: the coefficient of \(t^2\) is exactly \(\tfrac{1}{140}\)). It reproduces every measurement you own — all three, to the last digit. Asked about the future, it says the coffee reaches \(51.4^\circ\) at \(t = 60\) (minutes or hours; a parabola does not say), bottoms out at \(49.8^\circ\) at \(t = 75\), and then warms without bound: \(161.4^\circ\) at \(t = 200\). The coffee boils at room temperature. Both friends did honest arithmetic; what neither had was a sentence about why the temperature moves at all — and without the why, no amount of fitting can tell the shape of tomorrow's curve from the shape of yesterday's data.

So write the sentence. Coffee is not obliged to lose a degree a minute; it is obliged to lose heat, and heat flows from hot to cold, faster when the gap is bigger. Say it in words: the rate of cooling is proportional to how much hotter the coffee is than its surroundings. This is Newton's sentence (he wrote it down in 1701, anonymously, in a table of heat measurements), and spelling it in rates takes one line: if \(T(t)\) is the temperature and \(A\) the room, \[\frac{dT}{dt} = -k\,(T - A),\] with \(k\) a positive constant the mug has yet to reveal — the parameter. One equation, one unknown letter.

Calibrate before computing. At \(t = 0\): \(T - A = 70\). At \(t = 10\): \(T - A = 60\). The equation's own mathematics (§4 solves it in three lines) says the excess over the room shrinks by a constant factor per unit time, so the factor for ten minutes is \(60/70 = 6/7\) — which pins the parameter to \(k = \tfrac{1}{10}\ln\tfrac{7}{6} \approx 0.0154\) per minute, an exact algebra the lab verifies to twelve digits. The parameter is not a fitting degree of freedom; it is a measured property of the mug — the same \(k\) for every morning, the same \(k\) for the question you have not asked yet.

Now the predictions, and this is the moment to feel the difference in kind. The model says \(T(20) = 71.43^\circ\) — and you have already measured \(71.4^\circ\), agreement by construction, worth little. But it also says \(T(30) = 64.08^\circ\), and \(T(60) = 47.76^\circ\), and — the money question — that \(60^\circ\) arrives at \(t = 36.30\) minutes, six minutes and eighteen seconds after the linear friend's answer of \(30\), and with every digit earned from the sentence, not guessed from a curve. The lab rounds the whole trip: the model's answer substituted back reproduces \(60^\circ\) exactly. A table extrapolated is a hunch; an equation extrapolated is a computation.

And the sentence scales to places tables cannot reach. Ask not "when is this mug drinkable" but "what does the whole rod do, hot at one end, cold at the other, every point with its own reading?" One state variable becomes a continuum of them, the sentence becomes a balance of flows — and the equation becomes partial. That is §4's second act. The third act is stranger: ask "where is a drunkard after a thousand coin flips?", a question with no temperatures anywhere in it, and the same partial equation reappears, wearing probability's clothes — P16's dictionary running between physics and chance. Three costumes, one sentence: what changes equals what flows.

3 · Name the move

§2 replaced a fitted curve with a stated law. The move, named:

Pattern P26 — Model it with an equation. When a quantity changes and the question is about its future, do not summarize the data — state the law of change as a sentence in words, then translate the sentence into an equation about rates: balance what changes against what flows, in or out or between neighbors. The equation's fixed numbers are parameters: calibrate each on measurements inside a regime you trust, and say which regime. The equation then predicts — forward in time, sideways to new initial conditions, and (with care) beyond the calibration data — and every prediction is a theorem about the sentence, checkable against the world. A wrong prediction falsifies the sentence, never the arithmetic: that is what makes the failure informative.

Or, in words: ask not "what curve fits my data" but "what rule does my data obey". Write the rule in plain words first — hot to cold, in minus out, spread to the neighbors — and only then spell it in derivatives. Calibrate, predict, and go check. The equation is a claim about the world, signed.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — you can state a balance. There is a quantity that changes because something flows: heat in and out of a piece, probability into a site from its neighbors, cars into a stretch of road. The sentence "what changes = what flows in − what flows out" (or "the rate ∝ the driving difference") is sayable in words without mathematics. Jam-mode: nothing is conserved or balanced in sight — the quantity just is, with no flow story — then no equation is waiting to be written, and P07's auxiliary object or P25's census owns the problem.

Key 2 — you can name the state. The future is determined by a snapshot you can describe: one number (the mug), a reading per point (the rod), a probability per site (the walk). The equation lives on the state, so the state must exist before the equation does. Jam-mode: the "state" is a feeling, a mood, a trend — nothing measurable — then there is nothing to write \(dT/dt\) of, and P01's pinning must come first.

Key 3 — you need predictions beyond the data. The question is "when", "how far", "how long until" — about times or places you have not measured. This is the key that separates modeling from fitting: interpolation inside owned data needs no law. Jam-mode: every question is answerable from the table alone — then fit away, honestly, and skip the physics; the equation would be ceremony.

Box P26.A — the derivation, in three obligations. Every honest differential equation is earned the same three-step way, and each step has its own proof duty:

1 · The balance, exact. Over a small piece of the world, over a small interval of time, the change in the state equals the net flow: in minus out, plus whatever the piece manufactures (a source) or destroys (a sink). This step is bookkeeping — conservation, P09's invariant made local — and it is exact, because it is a definition of "flow", not a guess about the world. Duty: every term is named; nothing sneaks in or out unnamed.

2 · The constitutive law, falsifiable. The balance mentions flows; the physics says what the flows are made of: flux proportional to temperature slope (Fourier), to concentration slope (Fick), cooling proportional to excess (Newton), density-flux a function of density (traffic's \(q(\rho)\)). This step is a law about the material — checkable, rejectable, occasionally wrong. Duty: say it in words, and own that it is an assumption. Every parameter of the model lives here.

3 · The limit, licensed. Shrink the piece and the interval to zero and the bookkeeping becomes a differential equation — the differences become derivatives, the sum becomes an integral. The license is P15's: the error of the finite statement shrinks without end, so the limiting equation is certain given steps 1 and 2. Duty: the limit is taken once, explicitly; the equation you compute with is the limit, not the approximation.

Fail an obligation and the model fails accordingly: unbalanced terms produce fake physics, wrong constitutive laws produce confident wrong predictions (§5's Exhibit B), and a limit taken carelessly produces an equation that cannot be run (§5's Exhibit C).

Box P26.B — where the move appears. Cooling — Newton's 1701 sentence, §2 and §4's mug. Heat — Fourier's balance on a bar, §4's rod, the pattern's mandated PDE. Diffusion of stuff — Fick's 1855 law, and Einstein's 1905 bridge from the random walk, §4's third act. Populations — Malthus's growth and Verhulst's crowding correction, §5's Exhibit B. Traffic — the Lighthill–Whitham–Richards continuum of cars, Exercise 5, jams as traveling shocks. Finance — Black–Scholes's 1973 option equation, the walk's equation with a drift (Bachelier had the shape in 1900; the world took seventy years to notice). Waves — d'Alembert's vibrating string of 1747, the first PDE anyone wrote. The three-key signature: a "how fast" question, a flow you can narrate, a parameter to calibrate, and a prediction to check. The failure signatures are §5's: the regime drifts (Exhibit A), the law is wrong but fits (Exhibit B), the equation runs but not the direction you asked (Exhibit C).

4 · The move at work

Three workings, one per costume of Box P26.B: a mug (one state), a rod (a continuum of states), a walk (chance itself). Each one runs Box P26.A's three obligations, and each number below was computed and re-checked by the page's lab — the engine receipts are cited as they appear.

Example 1 (the mug) — Newton's balance, calibrated

The sentence, spelled. The rate of cooling is proportional to the excess over the room: \(\frac{dT}{dt} = -k(T - A)\), with \(A = 20^\circ\mathrm{C}\) and the starting excess \(T(0) - A = 70^\circ\mathrm{C}\). Solve it the short way: the excess \(E(t) = T(t) - A\) satisfies \(E' = -kE\), so \(E\) shrinks by the factor \(e^{-k\,\Delta t}\) over any interval — constant factor per unit time, the very behavior §2 used to calibrate. Three lines, closed form: \[T(t) = A + (T_0 - A)\,e^{-kt}.\] Every exponential in this section is this one line wearing different numbers.

Calibrate, then predict. The ten-minute reading \(80^\circ\) forces \(e^{-10k} = 6/7\) exactly, so \(k = \tfrac{1}{10}\ln\tfrac{7}{6} = 0.0154\) per minute — and here is the honest check of what calibration means: the model reproduces \(T(10) = 80^\circ\) by construction, so that agreement is worth nothing. The predictions are the ones nobody fed it: \(T(30) = 64.08^\circ\), \(T(60) = 47.76^\circ\), and \(60^\circ\) at \(t = 36.30\) min. The lab runs the round trip both ways — the exact answer \(t = 10\ln\tfrac{7}{4}/\ln\tfrac{7}{6}\) substituted back returns exactly \(60^\circ\) — and then relaxes the math: a crude forward Euler with half-minute steps reaches \(59.79^\circ\) at \(t = 36.5\) where the exact curve reads \(59.88^\circ\), a scheme error of \(0.09^\circ\), disclosed as the price of a five-line integrator. The equation is robust enough to survive even bad arithmetic on it.

The rivals, audited. The linear law hits \(60^\circ\) at \(t = 30\) — six minutes early — and the room at \(t = 70\), and \(0^\circ\) at \(t = 90\): wrong in a way no data on hand could reveal, because it has no room in it. The quadratic \(T(t) = \tfrac{t^2}{140} - \tfrac{15t}{14} + 90\) fits all three measurements perfectly and fails more interestingly: audit it with the modeler's question — what is its constitutive law? Differentiate: \(\frac{dT}{dt} = \tfrac{t}{70} - \tfrac{15}{14}\). The "cooling rate" depends on the clock, not on the gap to the room, and at \(t = 75\) it turns positive: heat flowing from a \(20^\circ\) room into \(49.8^\circ\) coffee, and by \(t = 200\), coffee boiling at \(161.4^\circ\) in that same room. The quadratic does not have a wrong parameter; it has no physics at all — which is exactly why its perfect fit predicts nothing.

Moves used: P02 the honest baseline of tables and fits · P01 the state named before the equation · P15 the calibration as exact algebra, not curve-fitting · P25 the machine receipts (round trip, Euler audit, the rivals' arithmetic).

Example 2 (the rod) — Fourier's balance on a continuum

The three obligations, on a slab. Cut the rod into slabs of width \(\Delta x\); let \(u_i\) be slab \(i\)'s temperature. Obligation 1, the balance, exact: a slab's heat rises by what flows in from the left minus what flows out to the right — \(c\,\Delta x \cdot (u_i^{n+1} - u_i^n) = \Delta t\,(F_{i-1 \to i} - F_{i \to i+1})\), with \(F\) the flow per unit time. Obligation 2, the constitutive law: Fourier's sentence of 1822 — heat flows from hot to cold, at a rate proportional to how steeply temperature drops along the rod: \(F \propto -\,u_x\). Hotter to the left means flow to the right; the minus sign is the direction of heat, and the proportionality constant is the material's conductivity, the rod's version of the mug's \(k\). Obligation 3, the limit: shrink \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta t\) together, P15's license stamps the finite bookkeeping into \[u_t \;=\; \alpha\, u_{xx}, \qquad \alpha > 0 \text{ a constant of the material},\] the heat equation — the first PDE ever solved, and the pattern's mandated exhibit. Note what survived the limit: the balance is intact at every width, which is why the equation can be trusted on grids of any size — the lab runs the discrete version directly, on 12 slabs, with insulated ends, and after two thousand steps the rod's total heat has drifted by \(2.3 \times 10^{-13}\) degrees: conservation is not a hope, it is the arithmetic.

One wave, decaying — P24's subject in miniature. Start the rod at \(u(x, 0) = 100\sin(\pi x)\), a single arch: hot center, cold ends. Apply the equation: \(u_{xx} = -\pi^2 \sin(\pi x)\), so the arch's temperature obeys \(u_t = -\alpha \pi^2 u\) — Newton's mug-equation again, with the "room" at zero and the rate fixed by the arch's shape. The arch decays exponentially without changing shape: \(u(x, t) = 100\,e^{-\alpha \pi^2 t}\sin(\pi x)\). Choose the material's constant as \(\alpha = 1/\pi^2\) and the rate is exactly \(1\): the peak reads \(100 \to 70.71 \to 50\) at \(t = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln 2, \ln 2\). Fourier's grander claim — that any starting shape is a sum of such arches, each fading at its own rate, P24's waves with a clock on each — is the door this page points at and does not open; one arch suffices to see the mechanism.

The receipts. Simulation first, honest about its price: a 100-cell grid run to \(t = \ln 2\) (28,092 steps) reads \(50.43^\circ\) at the center, against the exact \(50\) — the \(0.43^\circ\) is the grid's coarseness plus the scheme's, disclosed, not hidden; it shrinks as the grid refines. Copper next, with real units: a copper rod of \(L = 0.5\) m has \(\alpha = 1.11 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), and its arch decays to half in \((L/\pi)^2 \ln 2 / \alpha = 158.2\) seconds — about two and a half minutes, a number you can check with a thermometer and a stopwatch. Double the rod to one meter and the wait quadruples: \(632.7\) s — the \((L/\pi)^2\) law, slow modes on long rods, the physics of why big things cool slowly. And one P09 receipt, foreshadowing a whole page: with the ends held at zero, the quantity \(\sum_i u_i^2\) never increases — the lab tracks it for 500 steps from its starting \(2{,}000{,}000\) and it falls monotonically. That one-sided quantity is called an energy, and the page it opens is P29.

Moves used: P09 conservation as the balance, exact · P24 the one-arch decomposition and its clock · P15 the limit licensed, once · P25 the simulation receipts (28,092 steps, the copper stopwatch numbers, the energy ledger).

Example 3 (the walk) — chance, the same equation in different clothes

A question with no temperature in it. A coin-flip walker starts at zero; each minute, one step left or right, each with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). After 100 flips: where is he? Name the state the modeler's way — not "where is he" but "with what probability is he at site \(x\)": a number \(p_n(x)\) per site, adding to \(1\) — a temperature profile made of chance. And the balance sentence writes itself: the probability of being at \(x\) at minute \(n+1\) is what flowed in from the two neighbors at minute \(n\), \[p_{n+1}(x) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,p_n(x-1) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,p_n(x+1),\] which is Example 2's slab-bookkeeping, word for word, with \(\Delta x = \Delta t = 1\) and the material constant \(\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}\). Nothing diffuses because anything is hot; probability diffuses because it must. P16's dictionary runs between the costumes — temperature ↔ probability, heat ↔ chance — and every theorem about the rod translates for free.

Exact receipts, no simulation needed. The lab runs the recursion exactly — every probability a sum of products, no randomness anywhere — and collects what the table way would never see. The walker's mean position after \(n\) flips: \(0\), to machine precision. The variance: exactly \(n\), for every \(n\) up to 200 — the walker strays like \(\sqrt{n}\), the fingerprint that (with the step size rescaled) Einstein read off Brownian pollen in 1905 to measure the size of atoms: the same equation, running in a suspension of dust. The chance of returning to the start after 100 flips: \(\binom{100}{50}/2^{100} = 0.0796\), computed two independent ways (the recursion, and factorials via logarithms) agreeing to twelve digits. And a parity receipt that a carelessly continuous eye would miss: at even \(n\), every odd site has probability exactly zero — the walk visits every other site only, a lattice fact the smooth curve cannot encode.

The continuum costume. Rescale the sites by \(\sqrt{n}\) and the bars of the histogram melt into a curve — the Gaussian arch of center \(0\) and variance \(n\), whose peak value \(\sqrt{2/(\pi n)}\) at the origin is the smoothed version of the return-chance above. The fit is auditable, not asserted: with the parity correction (the walker's mass shares itself over half the sites, so the curve doubles), the relative error at the origin is \(0.501\%\) at \(n = 50\), \(0.250\%\) at \(n = 100\), \(0.125\%\) at \(n = 200\) — halving each time \(n\) doubles, exactly as a \(1/n\) error should. Off center, at 30 steps out, the error has grown to \(2.61\%\): the Gaussian is an asymptotic costume, superb at the scale of \(\sqrt{n}\), honest about degrading in the tails. The model — one balance line, calibrated by nothing at all — has produced the most famous curve in probability, and the receipts say exactly how far to trust it.

Moves used: P16 the dictionary between heat and chance · P19 the state as a probability, counted not simulated · P25 the exact recursion receipts (variance \(n\), return chance two ways, parity, the error-halving audit).

5 · When the move fails

Three exhibits, one per way a model goes wrong: the world drifts out of the calibration regime, the sentence itself is false while fitting beautifully, and the equation — true in one direction of time — is unrunnable in the other. Each exhibit was run, not imagined: the numbers are the lab's.

Exhibit A — the room that moved (the regime drifts)

Model the mug exactly as §4 did — same sentence, same \(k = 0.0154\), calibrated on the ten-minute reading. But let the room be honest reality: it warms through the morning, from \(20^\circ\) toward \(30^\circ\) on a two-hour curve. The lab integrates the true world (fourth-order, small steps) and the model side by side. At \(t = 10\): world \(80.06^\circ\), model \(80^\circ\) — the fit is honest to a twentieth of a degree. At \(t = 240\): world \(29.08^\circ\), model \(21.73^\circ\) — off by \(7.3^\circ\), the model quietly asserting a room ten degrees colder than the real one.

What broke: not the arithmetic and not even the equation — the domain. The parameter \(k\) was calibrated inside one regime (this room, this mug, ten minutes of morning) and deployed outside it, into an afternoon the calibration never saw. The failure signature is a model that tracks beautifully and then diverges slowly, with no error visible near the data. Mitigation: say the regime out loud when you calibrate (Box P26.A's duty); when the regime moves, move the model with it — make the room a state variable with its own sentence, or recalibrate on recent readings. Price: every promoted parameter is a new quantity to measure, and a model of everything is a model of nothing. Default habit: report every prediction with its calibration regime attached, the way §2's model should have said "for a \(20^\circ\) kitchen, before noon".

Exhibit B — the wrong sentence, fitting perfectly (the law fails)

A population doubles nicely at first, and a modeler fits Malthus's sentence — growth proportional to population — to the census. Take the honest numbers: population \(10\), and the logistic truth \(x(t) = 1000/(1 + 99e^{-0.5t})\), whose early years mimic doubling. Fit the growth rate at \(t = 5\), where the census reads \(x = 109.57\): both models reproduce that reading exactly, by construction. Now audit the fits against the truth the modeler has not seen. At \(t = 5.5\) the exponential is \(2.0\%\) high; at \(t = 6\), \(4.9\%\); at \(t = 7\), \(13.9\%\). At \(t = 10\): truth \(599.9\), exponential \(1200.6\) — twice the truth. At \(t = 15\): \(948\) against \(13{,}155\), fourteen times. At \(t = 20\): \(995.5\) against \(144{,}145\), one hundred forty-five times — a model predicting a hundredfold more of everything, still on the strength of a fit that never saw a bad day.

What broke: the constitutive law. "Growth ∝ population" is a sentence about the world, and the world has more sentences in it — food runs short, space fills, crowding bites. Verhulst's correction (growth ∝ population times room left) is not a patch to the arithmetic but a new piece of physics, and it is the same pattern applied again: model it with an equation — a better sentence. The failure signature is a fit that is perfect on the data and structurally blind to the mechanism; no residual analysis saves you, because the data is not where the wrongness lives. Mitigation: audit the sentence, not the curve — ask what the law omits, and test the omission against the world, not against the fit. Price: every candidate sentence must earn its parameters again; richer models are harder to falsify. Default habit: when two sentences both fit, prefer the one whose failure you can narrate — Malthus fails by ignoring a named resource, and that nameability is what lets you repair it.

Exhibit C — the arrow of time, computed (the direction fails)

Forensics finds a body at \(34^\circ\) and wants the hour of death: run the mug's equation backwards. What could go wrong — an equation is an equation. Run the rod's instead, where the arithmetic is transparent. Fourier's arch of shape \(\sin(20\pi x)\) decays forward in time by the factor (e^{-3947.8,t}); over \(0.01\) seconds that is \(e^{-39.478} = 7.2 \times 10^{-18}\) — forward, the mode is simply erased, the problem forgets. Backward, the same factor multiplies in reverse: \(e^{+39.478} = 1.4 \times 10^{17}\), and a measurement error of one part in a million in today's profile becomes, one hundredth of a second in the past, a hundred billion — the reconstruction is noise wearing the model's clothes. And no mode is safe: forward, every one of the first fifty modes decays (the slowest by a factor of \(0.906\)); backward, every one amplifies. The equation is honest forward and unusable backward, and this is not a defect of the heat equation but a theorem about its shape.

What broke: the assumption that an equation is a model in whichever direction you run it. Mathematicians call a problem well-posed when a solution exists, is unique, and depends continuously on its data; the backward heat equation fails the third clause by seventeen orders of magnitude. The failure signature is a reconstruction whose output is dominated by noise, or (the sneaky version) by the smoothing you applied to hide the noise. Mitigation: before running any equation backwards, check what it does to each of P24's waves — a mode analysis is a one-line audit. Price: some questions are genuinely unreachable, and the honest answer to "when did the arch look like this?" is a distribution, not a number. Default habit: state which direction the model runs in, and treat the other direction as a new model requiring its own license.

Scope, honestly. What P26 gives: a computable object with parameters that name properties of the world; predictions that are theorems about the stated sentence; a target for what to measure next; and — when prediction fails — a falsified sentence, which is knowledge. What it never gives: truth of the constitutive law (Exhibit B — a law is checkable, not provable), validity outside the calibration regime (Exhibit A — the regime is part of the model), well-posedness in the direction you want (Exhibit C — some questions the equation cannot carry), or the sentence itself: the pattern turns a stated law into an equation, and the stating remains physics, biology, economics — somebody's honest guess. The default habit: say the sentence out loud before writing any symbol; if you cannot say it in words, you do not have a model yet, you have a curve.

6 · What to try next

Doors from this page:

The nearest, and the reason this page exists on the ladder: P29 — Energy methods. §4's rod ended with a quantity that only decreases — \(\sum_i u_i^2\), verified for 500 steps by the lab. P29 promotes that observation into a method: find the one-sided quantity, and let it settle your questions without solving anything. P29 lists this page as a prerequisite, and the promotion is exactly the step from "the lab watched it fall" to "the fall is a theorem, and here is what the theorem buys".

The pattern's own sequel, twice over: P37 — Itô calculus — what happens to Example 3's walk when the steps shrink but the noise never does, the calculus of a world that vibrates as it moves — and P43 — Flow with surgery, where the heat equation is run not on a rod but on the shape of space itself, smoothing geometry the way it smooths temperature. Both name P26 in their prerequisite ladders, and both are this page's sentence in wilder costumes.

The heavy customers: P57 — Nash–Moser fast Newton and P58 — Ergodic correspondence — the first solves equations whose solutions lose derivatives (a modeling problem from the operator side), the second trades counting for recurrence (Example 3's walk, run to its ergodic limit). Both are far doors; the ladder to them runs through this page.

The default habit. When a question says "how fast", "how long until", or "when will it reach": state the balance sentence in words; name the state and the parameters; calibrate inside a regime you can say out loud; predict one thing you have not measured; and go check it. If the check fails, you have falsified a sentence about the world — which is the rarest and most valuable output mathematics has. The equation was never the model; the sentence was.

7 · Lineage

The idea is old enough to drink. Newton published his cooling sentence in 1701, anonymously, in a table of heat measurements in the Philosophical Transactions — and, honestly, not in the form this page uses: the "law" as taught is a later cleanup of Newton's measured scale, a reminder that models are edited by their inheritors. D'Alembert wrote the vibrating-string equation in 1747 and touched off the first great fight about what a PDE may call a solution — the argument that eventually forced the definition of a function itself to widen. Fourier submitted his heat memoir in 1807 and published the book, Théorie analytique de la chaleur, in 1822: the heat equation, and with it the claim that any signal is a sum of waves — P24's subject — which the Paris Academy spent a decade refusing to believe. Fick, a physiologist, proposed the diffusion law in 1855 by analogy with Fourier's heat; Einstein in 1905 connected Fick's diffusion to the random walk, gave the \(\sqrt{n}\) law its constant, and handed Perrin the experiment that weighed atoms; Bachelier had already written option prices as walks in 1900, a thesis nobody read for half a century, until Black and Scholes wrote the 1973 equation that finance now runs on. Lighthill and Whitham in 1955, and Richards in 1956, let traffic be a fluid — Exercise 5's jams are their shock waves. The dates are receipts, not load-bearing: every claim above stands on this page's own mathematics, and the sources are in the page log.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — A second mug, calibrated. Coffee at \(95^\circ\mathrm{C}\), room \(25^\circ\mathrm{C}\); after 5 minutes it reads \(85^\circ\mathrm{C}\). (a) Show the calibration is the same exact algebra as §2's: \(e^{-5k} = 6/7\), and compute \(k\). (b) Predict \(T(30)\). (c) Predict when the coffee reaches \(40^\circ\mathrm{C}\), and round-trip your answer.

Hint

The excess over the room starts at \(70^\circ\) and reads \(60^\circ\) at \(t = 5\) — the same \(70 \to 60\) as §2, on a five-minute clock instead of ten.

Worked answer

(a) \(e^{-5k} = 60/70 = 6/7\), so \(k = \tfrac{1}{5}\ln\tfrac{7}{6} = 0.0308\) per minute — twice §2's rate on a twice-as-fast mug. (b) \(T(30) = 25 + 70\left(\tfrac{6}{7}\right)^6 = 52.76^\circ\mathrm{C}\). (c) \(T = 40\) means excess \(15\): \(t = \tfrac{5\ln(70/15)}{\ln(7/6)} = 49.97\) min. Round trip: \(25 + 70\,e^{-0.0308 \times 49.9656} = 25 + 70 \times (6/7)^{10} \approx 40.00\) ✓ — the lab (D01) checks both predictions and the exact substitution.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The long copper rod. §4's copper rod, \(L = 0.5\) m, halves its arch in \(158.2\) s. (a) Explain, from the decay rate \(\alpha\pi^2/L^2\), why doubling the length must multiply the half-time by four. (b) Compute the half-time for a one-meter rod. (c) A ten-meter rod of the same material: predict the half-time, and say what this predicts about large objects cooling.

Hint

The rate is \(\alpha(m\pi/L)^2\) for the \(m\)-th mode; the slowest is \(m = 1\). Halving means \(e^{-\mathrm{rate}\cdot t} = \tfrac{1}{2}\).

Worked answer

(a) Half-time \(= \ln 2 \cdot L^2/(\alpha\pi^2)\): the \(L^2\) comes from the mode's decay rate \(\alpha\pi^2/L^2\), so doubling \(L\) multiplies the rate by \(4\) and the half-time likewise. (b) \(4 \times 158.2 = 632.7\) s — the lab (D02) checks the quadruple. (c) \(100 \times 158.2 = 15{,}818\) s \(\approx 4.4\) hours. Large things cool slowly, quadratically: the physics of why a roast rests, a planet keeps its heat, and a small cup betrays its temperature to the first sip.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The biased walk, exactly. Let the walker step right with probability \(p = 0.6\), left with \(0.4\). (a) Write the balance sentence and its equation — which of §4's three costumes has changed? (b) Compute the mean and variance of \(X_{1000}\) exactly, by running the recursion. (c) Explain the variance's coefficient \(4p(1-p)\): why does bias slow the spread, and what does it do to the drift?

Hint

(b) Run the §4 recursion with weights \(0.6\) and \(0.4\) — no randomness needed, exactly as the symmetric case. (c) Write a step as \(D + B\): a fair coin's drift \(D = \pm 1\) plus a coin's bias \(B\) that is constant per step.

Worked answer

(a) The sentence is unchanged — probability still flows in from the two neighbors — but the weights tilt: \(p_{n+1}(x) = 0.6\,p_n(x-1) + 0.4\,p_n(x+1)\). The costume that changed is the constitutive law (obligation 2): the material is now anisotropic. (b) The lab (D03) runs it exactly: mean \(= 200 = 0.2 \times 1000\), variance \(= 960 = 0.96 \times 1000\). (c) A step \(= D + B\) with \(D = \pm 1\) fair (variance \(1\)) and \(B = +0.2\) constant (variance \(0\)); independence gives \(\operatorname{Var} = n \cdot \operatorname{Var}(D) = n(1 - 0.2^2)\cdot\ldots\) — more precisely \(\operatorname{Var}(\text{step}) = 4p(1-p) = 0.96 < 1\): bias converts spread into drift. The walker still wanders like \(\sqrt{0.96\,n}\) around a center now moving at \(0.2n\) — drift and diffusion, the two terms of the first stochastic differential equation you will meet at P37.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the claim (pattern-unknown). A consultant, handed the three coffee measurements \((0, 90)\), \((10, 80)\), \((20, 71.4)\), reports: "I fit a parabola through all three points — perfect fit, zero residual — and my model predicts the coffee bottoms out at \(49.8^\circ\) at minute 75 and warms thereafter, reaching \(161.4^\circ\) at minute 200. The data is fully explained." Diagnose: what is the model missing, which of this page's keys does it fail, and what single question would you put to the consultant?

Hint

A fit reproduces the past; a model commits to a mechanism. What sentence, in words, does the parabola state about why temperature changes?

Worked answer

The parabola fits — nothing about the fit is wrong. What is missing is a law: the consultant has failed Key 1 (no balance is stated; nothing flows in his model) and Key 3 (the questions asked — minute 75, minute 200 — lie far outside the data, where a fit has no authority and a sentence has a theorem). The single question: "what does your model say the cooling rate depends on?" Differentiate his curve: rate \(= t/70 - 15/14\) — the rate depends on the clock, not on the gap to the room, and goes positive at \(t = 75\): the model asserts heat flowing from a \(20^\circ\) room into coffee cooler than \(50^\circ\). That is not a wrong parameter; it is a wrong sentence — indeed, no sentence at all. The calibrated model of §2, for contrast, predicts \(47.76^\circ\) at minute 60 (the parabola: \(51.43^\circ\) — the lab, D04, computes both) and decays monotonically to the room, as any sentence "hot to cold" must. The consultant's model can be falsified by the world; the page's, so far, has not been — and that difference, not the residual, is what "model" means.

Exercise 5 ◐ One road, two jams (exploration). Model a road by density \(\rho \in [0, 1]\) (fraction of a car-length per car-length) and flow \(q(\rho) = \rho(1 - \rho)\) cars per second. (a) Verify the balance sentence: density changes at each point by the flow in from behind minus the flow out ahead. (b) A jam ahead: \(\rho = 0.9\) for the road ahead, \(0.4\) approaching. Show the boundary between them moves upstream — against the traffic — with speed \(s = \frac{q_r - q_l}{\rho_r - \rho_l} = 1 - \rho_l - \rho_r\), and compute it. (c) A standing queue: light traffic \(\rho = 0.1\) arriving at a stretch already packed at \(0.9\). Show the shock is stationary. (d) Explore: where does the maximum of \(q\) sit, what is the speed of cars there, and what does the model predict about the lane a selfish driver should choose?

Hint

(b) Flow is conserved across a moving boundary: over a second, the cars entering the boundary region from the left must equal those leaving to the right, plus the density the boundary swept. (c) Same formula, new numbers. (d) \(q'(\rho) = 1 - 2\rho\).

Worked answer

(a) The balance is \(\rho_t + q(\rho)_x = 0\) — density's rate of change at a point equals the net flow in, the same obligation-1 bookkeeping as the rod, with cars as heat. (b) \(s = (q(0.9) - q(0.4))/(0.9 - 0.4) = (0.09 - 0.24)/0.5 = -0.30\) road-lengths per second: negative — the jam's tail marches upstream into the arriving traffic, which every driver has experienced and no driver expected. The lab (D05) simulates the balance on a 400-cell road and measures the interface's speed at \(-0.30\), to two decimals. (c) \(s = (0.09 - 0.09)/(0.9 - 0.1) = 0\): the queue holds its ground — measured \(0.00\) in the same simulation (D06) — the queue discharges exactly as fast as it is fed, \(q(0.1) = q(0.9) = 0.09\). (d) \(q' = 0\) at \(\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}\), where \(q = \tfrac{1}{4}\) is maximal: the fastest road moves the most cars at half density — and \(q'\) there is zero, so small density waves stand still. The selfish driver's lane-change is exactly a perturbation that lowers everyone's \(q\); the model's honest, rude answer is that the fastest lane is the one nobody else thought was fastest — an exploration worth more road than this page owns, and the door to P43's shocks-with-surgery.
