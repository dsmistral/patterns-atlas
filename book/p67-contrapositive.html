The Patterns Atlas · P67 · the tenth move of tier 2

Prove the contrapositive

Some implications will not be walked forward: the hypothesis sits there inert, offering nothing to compute with, while the conclusion's failure is a concrete, graspable, workable thing. The move: turn the arrow around under the negations — instead of proving \(P \implies Q\), prove \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\) — assume the conclusion fails and derive that the hypothesis must fail too. It is the same theorem, by a tautology every truth table confirms and no reader should take on faith twice; and the redirect is often the difference between a proof that never starts and a proof that is three lines long. The contrapositive is P11's flip with a different landing: contradiction explodes the assumption and hands you nothing; the contrapositive lands somewhere — on the negated hypothesis — and what it lands on can be used.

New word: implication. An implication \(P \implies Q\) is a conditional claim: if \(P\) holds, then \(Q\) holds. It says nothing about what happens when \(P\) fails — "if it rains, the match is indoors" is silent about sunny days — and this silence is exactly the room the contrapositive moves in. New word: negation. The negation \(\lnot P\) of a statement \(P\) is its exact opposite, in the precise sense that exactly one of the two holds: \(\lnot\)"the match is indoors" is "the match is outdoors or rained off"; \(\lnot\)"every \(n\) in the list is odd" is "some \(n\) in the list is even". Negation was P11's ignition tool; here it is the steering wheel. New word: contrapositive. The contrapositive of \(P \implies Q\) is \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\): flip the arrow, negate both ends. For the rain sentence: "if the match is not indoors, then it did not rain." The claim is logically identical to the original — §3 pays for that identity in full — but it is a different sentence to prove, and sometimes one of the two is easy while the other is impossible.

1 · Before we begin

The reader arrives owning the flip already — this page's whole engine is one page old. P11 named the machine:

Pattern P11 — Prove by contradiction. When the claim is a negative — no object of some kind exists, no attempt can succeed, there is no largest — or when the direct route offers nothing to build with, do not attack the claim: attack its opposite. (1) Ignite: write the negation precisely — quantifiers flipped, the assumed object named — and grant it as a working hypothesis. (2) Corner: derive consequences honestly, hunting for a collision with something you already own. (3) Explode: when two statements that cannot both hold are both in your hand, the hypothesis is dead: no hypothesis, no negation, and the claim stands.

Or, in P11's own words: to prove that something cannot exist, pretend it does — honestly, precisely, with its name written down — and follow the consequences until they crash. The crash is the proof. But the machine certifies only: it hands you no example, no bound, no method, because the entire content of the theorem is that the object is not there to be handed.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P11's machine is decisive but destructive: it ends in wreckage, and the wreckage certifies the claim without ever delivering anything — no structure, no bound, no route. And some implications need exactly that delivery, because the direct road is blind in a specific way: the hypothesis \(P\) sits in the hand like a stone — true, held, and offering nothing to compute with — while the failure of the conclusion, \(\lnot Q\), is a concrete object with handles: a factorization to find, a diverging quantity to chase, a residue class to compute in. The move this page names redirects the proof through that concrete object: prove \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\) instead, and let the tautology of §3 carry the redirect home. The two pages are siblings, not rivals: same flip of the negation, different landing. P11 assumes \(\lnot Q\) and crashes it; this page assumes \(\lnot Q\) and lands it — on \(\lnot P\), a usable conclusion. When the landing works, the proof keeps its cargo; when no landing exists, the crash machine is waiting one page back.

2 · Meet the problem: primes wearing a Mersenne mask

Here is a claim a reader could stare at for a year without seeing how to start: if \(2^{n} - 1\) is prime, then \(n\) is prime. Numbers of the form \(2^{n} - 1\) are called Mersenne numbers, and they are the most productive prime factory in the book's back pages — but take the claim on its own terms first. The hypothesis "\(2^{n} - 1\) is prime" is a stone: primality is a negative property — no divisors exist — and a negative hands you nothing to factor, nothing to expand, nothing to compute. Try to walk forward and the road disappears: from "no divisors of \(2^{n}-1\) exist", what algebra do you run to reach "no divisors of \(n\) exist"?

Hold that confession; §5 prices it.

Now look at the divisor column once more and see the proof trying to happen. Whenever \(n\) is composite — say \(n = ab\) with \(a, b > 1\) — the divisor that appears is \(2^{a} - 1\), where \(a\) is a factor of \(n\). That is not a coincidence to be tabulated; it is an identity waiting to be run. Expand \(2^{ab} - 1\) around the block \(2^{a}\): write \(2^{ab} = (2^{a})^{b}\), and expand \(X^{b} - 1\) for a symbol \(X\):

\[X^{b} - 1 \;=\; (X - 1)\,(X^{b-1} + X^{b-2} + \cdots + X + 1),\]

which every reader can verify by multiplying back out — the telescope of the middle terms is one line. Now put \(X = 2^{a}\):

\[2^{ab} - 1 \;=\; \bigl(2^{a} - 1\bigr)\bigl(2^{a(b-1)} + 2^{a(b-2)} + \cdots + 2^{a} + 1\bigr).\]

Both factors are bigger than \(1\) when \(a, b > 1\): the first is at least \(2^{2} - 1 = 3\), and the second is a sum of at least two positive terms. So if \(n = ab\) factors, then \(2^{n} - 1\) factors — and the factorization is not found by searching, it is handed over by the identity. The census's divisor column was this identity wearing numbers.

How far does the handed-over reach? Take \(n = 91 = 7 \times 13\): \(2^{91} - 1 = 2{,}475{,}880{,}078{,}570{,}760{,}549{,}798{,}248{,}447\) — twenty-eight digits. Nobody factor-tests a 28-digit number by hand, and nobody needs to: the identity hands over two divisors outright, \(2^{7} - 1 = 127\) and \(2^{13} - 1 = 8191\), and dividing back out confirms both. The redirect scales exactly where hand factoring cannot — that asymmetry is the move's whole economy.

Stop and notice what just happened to the logic, because it is the whole page. The claim wanted: \(2^{n} - 1\) prime \(\implies\) \(n\) prime. What got proved: \(n\) composite \(\implies\) \(2^{n} - 1\) composite. These are not the same sentence — the hypothesis and conclusion are each negated, and the arrow points the other way — and yet proving the second settles the first completely. Why that is legal, when it is legal, and what it costs, is the move this page names.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P67 — Prove the contrapositive. When the road from hypothesis to conclusion offers nothing to compute with, turn the arrow around under the negations: prove \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\) instead of \(P \implies Q\), and let the tautology carry it home.

When the claim is an implication \(P \implies Q\) and the hypothesis \(P\) is inert — a negative property, a mere existence, a stone with no handles — while the failure of the conclusion, \(\lnot Q\), is concrete and computable: do not force the forward road. Instead: (1) Negate the conclusion precisely. \(\lnot Q\) must be the exact opposite — quantifiers flipped, the failing object named — by P11's ignition standard; a slipped negation proves a different theorem. (2) Walk the redirected road. From \(\lnot Q\), derive \(\lnot P\) by whatever the concrete failure makes computable — a factorization, a diverging sequence, a residue. (3) Collect by tautology. \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\) and \(P \implies Q\) are logically identical — the truth table below, four rows, no exceptions — so the redirected proof is a proof of the original claim, with the same hypotheses and the same force. What the move buys: a computable road where none existed, at the price of one honest negation and one tautology never to be skipped in the reader's own first uses.

In words: if failing the conclusion would force failing the hypothesis, then holding the hypothesis forces holding the conclusion — because a world where \(P\) holds and \(Q\) fails is exactly the world both statements rule out. The contrapositive is not a similar argument or a stronger one; it is the same statement in different clothes, and the fit is exact.

Why the identity is legal — four rows, no exceptions. The claim \(P \implies Q\) fails in exactly one situation: \(P\) true and \(Q\) false. A truth table says so by definition, and the four rows are the whole audit:

One row — \(P\) true, \(Q\) false — is where an implication dies, and both sentences die there together; everywhere else both survive. The table is small enough to check by hand in a minute; but the table is also the definition of the arrow, so the check certifies the bookkeeping, not a discovery. The discovery, if there is one, is strategic: the two provable sentences are the same sentence, and they are not equally easy to prove.

The three-key diagnosis. Claim type: implications \(P \implies Q\) — and only implications; a bare existence or a bare equality has no arrow to turn, and the move does not apply. Problem shape: the hypothesis gives no purchase (a negative property like primality, a universal claim, a mere assumption of existence) while the negated conclusion gives handles (a concrete factorization to exhibit, a counterexample to build, a computation to run). The signature worth internalizing: negating the conclusion makes the problem easier, not harder. If negating \(Q\) lands you in a vaguer place than \(Q\), the redirect is probably wrong for this claim. Tools at hand: P11's ignition standard for the negation (the flip must be exact); the algebra the concrete failure affords; and the four-row tautology, which does the logical carrying so the mathematics can do the computational carrying. All three keys are checked before the redirect is trusted: a negation that is not exact proves a claim about a different world, and a redirect whose road is stonier than the original was a u-turn, not a route.

4 · Worked exemplars

Moves used: P67 throughout — the redirect of §3, P11's negation discipline, B02's residue arithmetic, and the polynomial identity of §2 run as an engine.

Exemplar A — the Mersenne claim, finished. Return to the census's puzzle with everything §3 built. The claim: \(2^{n} - 1\) prime \(\implies\) \(n\) prime. The forward road was the stone: "no divisors of \(2^{n} - 1\) exist" offers nothing. How would you have found the way out? By doing the lazy thing, which turns out to be the whole move: give up on the stone. The hypothesis gives you nothing to hold, so ask the only question left — what would make the conclusion false? — and suddenly there is something to compute with. The redirect is not cleverness; it is surrender, aimed. The redirect: assume \(\lnot Q\) — \(n\) is composite, say \(n = ab\) with \(a, b > 1\) — and the concrete object appears immediately: the factorization identity of §2,

\[2^{n} - 1 \;=\; 2^{ab} - 1 \;=\; \bigl(2^{a} - 1\bigr)\bigl(2^{a(b-1)} + \cdots + 2^{a} + 1\bigr),\]

with both factors exceeding \(1\). That is \(\lnot P\): \(2^{n} - 1\) is composite. The tautology collects: \(2^{n} - 1\) prime forces \(n\) prime — for every \(n\), no census needed, and the census's every row now reads as an instance. Notice where the computability came from: negating "n is prime" produced a witness — an actual factorization \(n = ab\) to hold — and the witness plugged straight into a polynomial identity. That is the problem-shape key of §3 in action: the negated conclusion was not merely easier, it was a different kind of object — a thing with parts instead of a thing with no parts.

Exemplar B — the first thing every series course proves, redirected. The claim, from analysis: if the series \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_{n}\) converges, then \(a_{n} \to 0\). Try the forward road and watch it go stony: from "the partial sums settle toward a limit", reach "the individual terms die" — the honest proof needs an \(\varepsilon\)-argument splitting the sum at an index \(N\), and it can be done, but the reader should feel how little the hypothesis wants to be touched: convergence is a statement about a limit of sums, and the conclusion is about the terms themselves. The redirect: assume \(\lnot Q\) — the terms do not die; there is an \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that for every \(N\) some \(n \geq N\) has \(|a_{n}| \geq \varepsilon\) — and now compute. The partial sums cannot settle: past any index, a term of size at least \(\varepsilon\) is still waiting to be added, and a sum with a standing obligation of size \(\varepsilon\) outstanding forever cannot be closing in on any limit — the distance from any late partial sum to any proposed limit \(L\) is at least \(\varepsilon - (\text{tail wobble})\), and choosing the two straddling terms kills the wobble: \(|s_{n} - s_{m}| \geq \varepsilon\) for terms \(n, m\) straddling a late survivor. That is \(\lnot P\): the partial sums fail the Cauchy condition, so the series diverges. Collect by tautology: convergence forces \(a_{n} \to 0\). This is the n-th term test, and its proof is this page's move wearing analysis clothes; the same redirect, run on the converse, is §5's first exhibit — and it breaks, because the converse is a different claim.

Exemplar C — a residue the triangle cannot escape. The claim, from arithmetic: if \(a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}\) — a Pythagorean triple — then \(3\) divides \(a\) or \(3\) divides \(b\). The forward road: from an equality of squares, reach a divisibility of one leg — the equality says everything at once and nothing pointed. The redirect: assume \(\lnot Q\) — \(3 \nmid a\) and \(3 \nmid b\) — and compute in B02's residue machine. Any integer not divisible by \(3\) is \(\equiv \pm 1 \pmod 3\), so its square is \(\equiv 1 \pmod 3\); and every square at all is \(\equiv 0\) or \(1 \pmod 3\). So \(a^{2} + b^{2} \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 3\). But \(c^{2}\) is a square: \(\equiv 0\) or \(1 \pmod 3\), never \(2\). So \(a^{2} + b^{2} \not\equiv c^{2} \pmod 3\), hence \(a^{2} + b^{2} \neq c^{2}\) — that is \(\lnot P\). Collect: every Pythagorean triple has \(3 \mid a\) or \(3 \mid b\). The computability came, again, from the negation: "3 divides neither leg" is a concrete residue condition, and the residue machine eats concrete conditions. The same redirect with \(4\) or \(5\) in place of \(3\) is §8's third exercise, and the answer changes with the modulus — which is exactly the kind of boundary a census should find before a proof is attempted.

Exemplar D — the pigeonhole principle, no ceremony. The claim, from combinatorics (P10): if \(N\) items go into \(n\) boxes and \(N \ge kn + 1\), then some box holds at least \(k + 1\). The classical proof runs it as a contradiction — assume every box holds at most \(k\), sum, crash into \(N \le kn\), cry contradiction. But watch what the redirect does with the same computation. Name the pieces: \(P\) is "\(N \ge kn + 1\)", \(Q\) is "some box holds \(\ge k + 1\)". Negate the conclusion — \(\lnot Q\): every box holds \(\le k\) (for whole numbers, "not \(\ge k+1\)" is "\(\le k\)", no ceremony). Now compute forward: \(c_{1} + \cdots + c_{n} \le k + \cdots + k = kn\). That is literally \(\lnot P\) — \(N \le kn\), contradicting \(N \ge kn+1\) — and the tautology collects, no contradiction ritual needed. The crash machine and the redirect share the entire computation; they differ only in what the last line is called. When the computation lands on \(\lnot P\) exactly, the redirect states the landing honestly.

Exemplar E — analysis, twice redirected. Two claims where the hypothesis is a universal quantifier and the redirect flips it into a witness. (i) The ε-vanishing principle: if \(x \ge 0\) and \(x < \varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), then \(x = 0\). The forward road stares at infinitely many inequalities at once. The redirect: negate the conclusion — \(x > 0\) — and the infinity collapses to one line: choose \(\varepsilon_{0} = x/2 > 0\); then \(x = 2\varepsilon_{0} > \varepsilon_{0}\), contradicting \(x < \varepsilon\) for all \(\varepsilon\). Negating "for every \(\varepsilon > 0\)" flipped \(\forall\) into \(\exists\) — one witness instead of infinitely many checks. (ii) Injectivity of a monotone function: \(f(x) = x^{5} + 3x^{3} + 7x - 2\) is injective. Forward: from \(f(x_{1}) = f(x_{2})\) solve a degree-5 equation — algebraically stony. Redirect: negate the conclusion — \(x_{1} \neq x_{2}\), say \(x_{1} < x_{2}\) — and compute: each term \(x^{5}, 3x^{3}, 7x\) is strictly increasing, so \(f(x_{1}) < f(x_{2})\), hence \(f(x_{1}) \neq f(x_{2})\) — \(\lnot P\), collected. (The machine-checked version: \(f'(x) = 5x^{4} + 9x^{2} + 7 \ge 7 > 0\) everywhere.) Both redirects run on the same fuel: the negated conclusion was concrete where the hypothesis was a quantified fog.

5 · When the trick breaks

The converse is not the contrapositive. The single most common corruption of this move, in the wild, is proving the wrong sentence: the reader turns the arrow but forgets the negations, and proves \(Q \implies P\) — the converse — a different claim that the truth table's fourth column already separates from ours. The classic wreck is the series test of Exemplar B. The converse reads: if \(a_{n} \to 0\), then \(\sum a_{n}\) converges — and it is false, by the most famous series in the book's margins: the harmonic series \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n}\), whose terms die (\(\frac{1}{n} \to 0\)) and whose partial sums climb without a ceiling — \(H_{100} \approx 5.19\), \(H_{1000} \approx 7.49\), \(H_{10^{6}} \approx 14.39\), and no ceiling ever. So the converse fails while the contrapositive-claim stands: the two are not the same sentence, and no amount of proving one substitutes for the other. The audit question that catches the corruption: in the sentence you proved, which end is negated? If the answer is "neither" or "only one", the sentence is not the contrapositive — it is the converse (arrow flipped, ends clean) or the inverse (\(\lnot P \implies \lnot Q\), ends negated, arrow unflipped), and both are different claims that happen to be true or false on their own budgets.

The negation is the whole proof's foundation — and it is where the slips live. The redirect inherits P11's ignition risk in full: \(\lnot Q\) must be the exact opposite of \(Q\), quantifiers flipped and the failing witness named. The standard slip runs on quantified conclusions. Take \(Q\): "for every \(\varepsilon > 0\), there is an \(N\) such that for all \(n \geq N\), \(|a_{n}| < \varepsilon\)" — the death of the terms. Its exact negation, per P11's flip: "there exists an \(\varepsilon > 0\) such that for every \(N\) there is some \(n \geq N\) with \(|a_{n}| \geq \varepsilon\)" — a standing witness, one fixed \(\varepsilon\), surviving arbitrarily late. Slip one quantifier — "for every \(\varepsilon\) there is an \(N\) and an \(n\)" — and the "negation" describes a claim compatible with \(Q\) itself; the redirected proof then assumes something the conclusion's failure does not grant, and everything downstream, however correct, proves nothing. The audit that catches it is P11's own: negate \(Q\) twice, by the quantifier rules, and check you are back where you started. Four lines of bookkeeping; the whole redirect rests on them.

The same slip in its simplest clothes, no quantifiers at all — just De Morgan's flip. Exemplar C's redirect needs \(\lnot Q\) where \(Q\) is "\(3 \mid a\) or \(3 \mid b\)": the exact negation is "\(3 \nmid a\) and \(3 \nmid b\)" — both legs free, eight residue cells. Botch the and into an or — "a leg not divisible by 3" — and only four cells get checked, half the residue table left unexamined. The census tells the two apart at a glance:

The botched \(\lnot Q\) ("or") covers all eight cells — including four where a leg is a multiple and the sum is \(1\), a perfectly legal square. The correct version narrows the assumption to the four cells where the sum is always \(2\), off the square list, and \(\lnot P\) falls out. The redirect lives or dies on that one word: and, not or. De Morgan's laws are not decoration on the negation step — they are the negation step.

No purchase either way — the redirect can be a u-turn. The move's diagnosis key promised that negating the conclusion should make the problem easier. Sometimes it does not. When \(P\) and \(Q\) are both bare existences — "if a solution to \((\ast)\) exists, then a solution to \((\ast\ast)\) exists" — the negated conclusion is a nonexistence, as stony as the original hypothesis, and the redirect buys nothing: the proof, if it exists, walks forward or crashes. The honest response is to notice the u-turn before burning the afternoon: ask what \(\lnot Q\) concretely hands you, and if the answer is "another stone", close this page and open P11's or build forward directly. And one deeper boundary deserves its sentence: the four-row tautology is a classical truth — it reads "every row agrees" off a completed table. In the constructive tradition, where a disjunction must come with a witness telling you which disjunct holds, the full contrapositive identity is not available: from \(\lnot Q \implies \lnot P\) the constructivist recovers \(P \implies \lnot\lnot Q\), and the double negation is not the same coat as \(Q\). None of this book's claims live in that territory, but a reader who later meets it should know the four-row table has a philosophy attached, and the philosophy is why.

Honest scope. This page redirects implications whose negated conclusion gives purchase, with the tautology verified four rows deep and every worked claim machine-checked. It does not prove the tautology's philosophy (the classical table is asserted as definition, §3 says exactly where the load rests); it does not cover claims without arrows; and it does not decide in advance whether a given redirect has purchase — the three-key diagnosis is a checklist run on a specific claim, not an oracle. The standing blind spots are the converse (a different claim, forever the impostor), the slipped negation (P11's audit, owed on every use), and the u-turn (no purchase either way, the redirect refused honestly).

6 · What to try next

When the redirect stalls — the negation is exact, the road is honest, and the wheels still spin — the mitigation is always the same in kind: escalate the flip. The contrapositive assumed \(\lnot Q\) and tried to land it on \(\lnot P\); P11's machine assumes \(\lnot Q\) and hunts for a crash against any wall whatsoever — a proven fact, a definition, the assumption's own tail. The crash machine is strictly more permissive: it can prove claims the redirect cannot, at the price of delivering nothing but the verdict. So the ladder of escalation reads: redirect first (it keeps the cargo); if \(\lnot P\) is unreachable but any contradiction is findable, run P11's full machine; if even the ignition is the problem — \(\lnot Q\) cannot be written precisely — the claim is not an implication or the notation is not ready, and P01's pin-down is the honest next stop. The price of the escalation is real: a crash proves and never explains, and a redirect that almost lands often contains the structure the direct proof was missing — reread the stalled redirect before abandoning it, because the stall point is usually where the mathematics actually lives.

7 · Lineage interlude

The contrapositive is the oldest move in this book by documented pedigree. Aristotle's Organon (fourth century BC) works out the conversion of syllogisms — which inferences survive the swap of terms — and the second book of the Prior Analytics treats the conversion patterns the medievals would later extend under the name contraposition; the move was named, catalogued, and taught before algebra had a symbol to its name. The medieval logicians (Boethius's commentaries, then the scholastic textbooks) carried the conversion rules forward as standard equipment. The modern form arrived with the algebra of logic: Augustus De Morgan's Formal Logic (1847) systematized the negation laws — his named laws are the quantifier-flipping machinery this page's §5 audit runs — and George Boole's An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854) made implication itself a computable object, so that "same truth table" became a theorem one could check rather than a feeling one had. Truth tables as a completed method belong to the 1920s — Emil Post's dissertation (written 1920, published 1921) and Ludwig Wittgenstein's Tractatus both deployed the full tabular bookkeeping — and the four-row table of §3 is exactly that machine, run at its smallest size. The constructive caveat of §5 is the youngest item: L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism (from 1908) and Arend Heyting's formalization (1930) drew the boundary where the completed table stops being a proof, and the boundary is now standard graduate material — the move this page teaches is universal in classical mathematics and provably stronger than its constructive cousin. The Mersenne exhibit carries its own line: Marin Mersenne (1588–1648) compiled his primes in Cogitata physico-mathematica (1644), and the factorization identity of §2 — the engine of Exemplar A — is the standard first theorem of that theory.

Then check one of your answers against the census: find a number ending in 0 not divisible by 5, or report that the converse of (i) is true and say why.

Hint

Contrapositive: flip the arrow, negate both ends. Converse: flip, don't negate. Inverse: negate, don't flip. For (i)'s census: ending in 0 is divisible by 10, and every multiple of 10 is a multiple of 5 — read the claim's direction carefully.

Worked answer

(i) Contrapositive: "if a number is not divisible by 5, it does not end in 0." Converse: "if divisible by 5, it ends in 0" — false (15, 25, 35: divisible by 5, ending in 5). Inverse: "if it does not end in 0, it is not divisible by 5" — false, same witnesses; note the inverse of (i) is the contrapositive of the converse, which is why they always share a truth value. (ii) Contrapositive: "if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^{2}\) is odd" — provable in one line (odd \(= 2k{+}1\), square \(= 4k^{2} + 4k + 1\)), and the standard proof that even squares have even roots. Converse: "if \(n\) is even, \(n^{2}\) is even" — true, but irrelevant to the original's proof. Inverse: "if \(n^{2}\) is odd, \(n\) is odd" — true; it is the contrapositive of the converse. (iii) Contrapositive: "if the match is not indoors, it did not rain." Converse: "if the match is indoors, it rained" — the umpire's error. In each triple, exactly the original and the contrapositive are logically identical — the other two stand or fall on their own budgets, in pairs (converse and inverse are contrapositives of each other).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The base changes everything. Claim: for \(n \geq 2\), if \(a^{n} - 1\) is prime (with \(a \geq 2\) a whole number), then \(a = 2\) and \(n\) is prime. (a) Prove the "\(a = 2\)" half by contrapositive: assume \(a \geq 3\) and find the factorization. (b) Prove the "\(n\) prime" half — and say which prior exemplar did the work. (c) Check the census: \(3^{2} - 1 = 8\), \(3^{3} - 1 = 26\), \(4^{2} - 1 = 15\) — all composite, each factorization an instance of your part (a). (d) The confession: \(2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \times 89\). Say precisely which direction this row kills, and why the theorem survives it untouched.

Hint

For (a): \(a^{n} - 1 = (a - 1)(a^{n-1} + \cdots + a + 1)\), and when \(n \geq 2\) both factors exceed 1 — the same telescope as §2, with \(a\) in place of \(2^{a}\).

Worked answer

(a) Redirect: assume \(a \geq 3\) (that is \(\lnot Q_{1}\), "\(a \neq 2\)"). Then \(a - 1 \geq 2\), and the identity \(a^{n} - 1 = (a-1)(a^{n-1} + \cdots + 1)\) — verified by multiplying back out, the middle terms telescoping — exhibits two factors both greater than 1. So \(a^{n} - 1\) is composite: \(\lnot P\). Collect: \(a^{n} - 1\) prime forces \(a = 2\). (b) With \(a = 2\) the claim collapses to Exemplar A's Mersenne statement, already proved by the same redirect: \(2^{n} - 1\) prime forces \(n\) prime. The two halves are two contrapositives stacked, and the second is the first with the base settled. (c) \(8 = 2 \times 4\) (factor \(3 - 1 = 2\)); \(26 = 2 \times 13\) (factor \(3 - 1 = 2\)); \(15 = 3 \times 5\) (factor \(4 - 1 = 3\)) — each composite factorization is part (a)'s identity with the numbers filled in. (d) The row kills the converse — "n prime forces \(2^{n} - 1\) prime" — which was never claimed. The theorem is the arrow \(2^{n} - 1\) prime \(\implies\) \(n\) prime (plus the base clause), and a composite Mersenne number at a prime index sits entirely outside it. Confusing that row with a counterexample to the theorem is §5's first failure mode, wearing a Mersenne mask.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The modulus decides. For each modulus below, state the claim, decide whether it is true, prove it by contrapositive where it is true, and refute it by witness where it is false. (a) \(m = 3\): "if \(a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}\), then \(3 \mid a\) or \(3 \mid b\)" — Exemplar C claims it; re-derive the residue census and the redirect. (b) \(m = 2\): "if \(a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}\), then \(a\) or \(b\) is even" — the census is squares mod \(4\), not mod \(2\), and the redirect is one line. (c) \(m = 5\): "if \(a^{2} + b^{2} = c^{2}\), then \(5 \mid a\) or \(5 \mid b\)" — census first; the answer differs from (a) and (b), and the witness is the most famous triple in the book. (d) Write the boundary sentence: what property of the residue census decides the claim for a modulus \(m\), and why does the census come before the redirect?

Hint

Squares mod 3 are \(0, 1\); squares mod 4 are \(0, 1\); squares mod 5 are \(0, 1, 4\). For (c), check the triple \(3, 4, 5\) itself against the claim — and check what \(1 + 4\) does mod 5.

Worked answer

(a) True. Redirect: if \(3 \nmid a\) and \(3 \nmid b\), each leg is \(\equiv \pm 1\), so \(a^{2} + b^{2} \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 3\); but \(c^{2} \equiv 0\) or \(1\), never \(2\). So \(a^{2} + b^{2} \neq c^{2}\) — \(\lnot P\) — and the tautology collects. (b) True. Census: an odd number squares to \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\). Redirect: if both legs are odd, \(a^{2} + b^{2} \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 4\); but \(c^{2} \equiv 0\) or \(1 \pmod 4\), never \(2\). So at least one leg is even. Note the census runs mod 4, not mod 2: mod 2 the odd squares are \(\equiv 1\), and \(1 + 1 \equiv 0\) is a legal square value, so the obstruction is invisible — the modulus has to be big enough for the failure to show. (c) False, and the witness is the first triple every reader meets: \(3^{2} + 4^{2} = 5^{2}\), with \(5 \nmid 3\) and \(5 \nmid 4\). The census explains the collapse: squares mod 5 are \(0, 1, 4\), and the two distinct nonzero residues combine as \(1 + 4 = 5 \equiv 0\) — a square value (\(c \equiv 0 \pmod 5\)) reached by two non-multiple legs. Mod 3 and mod 4 the nonzero square residue was single (\(\{1\}\)), so two non-multiple legs always summed to \(2\), off the square list; mod 5 the sum lands back on it, and the obstruction evaporates — no redirect can succeed, because the redirect would have to derive a falsehood from a true residue situation. (d) The boundary sentence: the claim holds for modulus \(m\) exactly when no two square residues of non-multiples of \(m\) sum to a square residue — a finite, censusable condition on the residue table. The census comes first because it decides the case before any machinery runs: where the census obstructs, the redirect proves the claim in one line; where the census permits, the honest work is finding the witness. Same method as Exemplar C, opposite verdicts — and the method, not the verdict, is the payload.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Three broken redirects, diagnosed cold. Each "proof" below claims to use this page's move; each is broken, and §5 named the failure classes. Diagnose each: which sentence was actually proved, which check catches it, and what the honest repair is. (i) "Claim: if \(n^{2}\) is even then \(n\) is even. Proof: contrapositive — if \(n\) is even, then \(n^{2} = 4k^{2}\) is even. Done." (ii) "Claim: if a series converges, its terms die. Proof: contrapositive — suppose the terms die; then... the series converges, unless the terms die too slowly, as the harmonic series shows; so the claim holds whenever the terms do not die." (iii) "Claim: if \(2^{n} - 1\) is prime, \(n\) is prime. Proof: contrapositive — suppose \(n\) is prime; \(2^{11} - 1 = 2047 = 23 \times 89\) is composite, which would contradict primality if \(n\) were not prime; the claim follows."

Hint

No hint — the diagnosis is the exercise. (For each: name the sentence proved — contrapositive, converse, inverse, or neither — and run §5's audit question: which end is negated?)

Worked answer

(i) The sentence proved is the converse ("\(n\) even \(\implies n^{2}\) even" — arrow flipped, ends clean). It happens to be true, which makes the corruption dangerous: a true sentence proved where a different true sentence was claimed. §5's audit question catches it — in the proved sentence, which end is negated? Neither. The repair: negate the conclusion (\(n^{2}\) odd), prove \(n\) odd \(\implies n^{2}\) odd, one line. (ii) The sentence proved is nothing: the argument assumes \(\lnot P\) ("the terms die" — the wrong end entirely; the redirect must assume \(\lnot Q\), the failure of the conclusion), wobbles through a hedge ("unless... as the harmonic series shows"), and lands on a restatement. The harmonic series is doing the work of an honest counterexample — to the converse — smuggled inside a "proof" of the original. The repair: assume the terms do not die (the exact quantifier-flipped \(\lnot Q\) of Exemplar B) and run the Cauchy computation. (iii) The sentence proved is the inverse wearing the claim's clothes: it assumes \(n\) prime (that is \(Q\), not \(\lnot Q\)) and gestures at a composite value of \(2^{n} - 1\) — which, even if it proved "\(\lnot P \implies \lnot Q\)"-shaped text, would be proving the inverse, a different claim (the inverse of the Mersenne claim is "n composite \(\implies 2^{n} - 1\) composite" — true, and provable by the §2 identity, but not what was asked). Worse, the displayed "proof" derives nothing: it cites one composite value at a prime index and calls it a contradiction. Three broken redirects, three different corruptions — converse, wrong-end ignition, inverse — and the audit question "which end is negated, and which direction does the arrow point?" catches all three in one reading.

Exercise 5 ◐ — Where the redirect beats the crash, and where it does not. This page claimed the contrapositive is P11's flip with a better landing: same ignition, but the proof keeps its cargo. Explore the boundary. (a) Take the Mersenne claim and run it both ways: the redirect of Exemplar A, and a full P11 machine (assume \(2^{n} - 1\) prime and \(n\) composite, and hunt a crash). Report where the crash machine ends up — and whether the factorization identity appears in both proofs or only one. (b) Take a negative claim — "\(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational" — and try to run it as a contrapositive: what is \(P\), what is \(Q\), and does the redirect have any road at all? Report honestly. (c) Explore outward: find (or invent) two more implications of each kind — one where the redirect lands cleanly, one where it is a u-turn — and state the feature of the conclusion's negation that decides which. (d) The boundary sentence: what does the crash machine prove that the redirect cannot, and what does the redirect deliver that the crash cannot? Say which you would reach for on a fresh claim, and why.

Hint

For (a): the crash machine gets to assume both "\(2^{n} - 1\) prime" and "n = ab" and derive any contradiction whatsoever — but look at what it reaches for first. For (b): an irrationality claim has no arrow — try writing \(P\) and \(Q\) and watch the redirect refuse.

Worked answer

(a) The redirect: assume \(n = ab\), and the identity hands over the factorization of \(2^{n} - 1\) — landing on \(\lnot P\) in one line, cargo intact (the explicit factors \(2^{a} - 1\) and the telescoping sum). The crash machine: assume \(2^{n} - 1\) prime and \(n = ab\); the very first consequence available is the same identity — \(2^{n} - 1\) has a nontrivial factor, contradicting primality. The crash proof is the redirect proof with the landing repackaged as an explosion: same identity, same one line, and the cargo (the explicit factorization) present in both. The lesson: for claims where \(\lnot P\) is itself a concrete computable fact, the two machines coincide, and the redirect merely says the quiet part — what the contradiction will be — out loud. (b) The claim "\(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational" has no arrow: it is a bare negative, \(P\) all by itself. To run a contrapositive one would have to manufacture \(Q\) — "if \(\sqrt{2} = p/q\) in lowest terms, then [something]" — and the natural manufacturing is P11's own ignition: the something is "two even integers share a reduced fraction", a contradiction. The redirect refuses: there is no conclusion to negate, and inventing one is just P11 wearing this page's hat. Honest report: the move does not apply, and knowing that is the diagnosis key working. (c) Clean landings, the shared feature: the negated conclusion is an existence with structure — a factorization (Mersenne), a residue class (Exemplar C), a surviving large term (Exemplar B) — something to plug into an identity or a machine. U-turns, the shared feature: the negated conclusion is another bare negative or a bare existence of the same shape as the hypothesis — "no solution exists" negated into "a solution exists" hands you an object you know nothing else about. (d) The crash machine proves claims the redirect cannot touch: any implication where the contradiction comes from an unexpected wall — P11's external-contradiction type, where \(\lnot P\) is not the crash site at all. The redirect delivers structure: the explicit reason (the factorization, the residue, the surviving term) that the implication holds, which a crash never has to supply. On a fresh claim: run the three-key diagnosis — if \(\lnot Q\) has handles, redirect; if \(\lnot Q\) is a stone but a crash is findable, P11; if the negation cannot be written precisely, stop and pin the claim down first. The habit is one audit question asked before any machinery runs: what does the failure of the conclusion put in my hand?
