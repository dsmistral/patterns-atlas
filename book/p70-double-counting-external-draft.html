Tier 2 · Canon Foundations

P70 · Double counting

Count the same set of incidences in two different ways — once by rows and once by columns — so two seemingly unrelated sums are forced to equal the same corner total.

Prerequisites: P02 · Experiment with small cases, P10 · Force a collision

On-ramp 1 — The row-sum / column-sum ledger identity. Imagine a rectangular spreadsheet with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns filled with numbers \(M_{i,j}\). Whether you add each row first and then sum the row totals, or add each column first and then sum the column totals, you are adding the exact same \(m \times n\) cells:

\[ \sum_{i=1}^m \left(\sum_{j=1}^n M_{i,j}\right) \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^n \left(\sum_{i=1}^m M_{i,j}\right). \]

When every cell \(M_{i,j} \in \{0, 1\}\) records whether row object \(a_i \in A\) and column object \(b_j \in B\) interact, this matrix is called an incidence matrix, and equating the row totals to the column totals is the method of double counting (also called Fubini's principle for finite sets).

On-ramp 2 — Incidence pairs \((a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\). Often a problem gives you only one set of objects (say, vertices of a graph or subsets of \(\{1, \dots, n\}\)) and a messy sum. To double-count, you ask: what is a single "checkmark" \((a, b)\) that this sum is counting? Once you name the pair \((a, b)\) — such as (vertex \(v\), edge \(e\) touching \(v\)) or (committee \(S\), chairperson \(x \in S\)) — grouping the pairs by their first coordinate gives one formula, and grouping them by their second coordinate gives a second formula!

1 · Before we begin

In P02 · Experiment with small cases, we explored small configurations to uncover hidden numerical patterns, and in P12 · Name the obstruction, we saw how Euler solved the Seven Bridges of Königsberg by focusing on vertex degrees (how many bridges touch each landmass).

Whenever a local rule relates two types of objects — vertices and edges, divisors and multiples, committees and members, or triangles and boundary segments — Double counting (P70) builds the incidence ledger between them. Summing by rows gives one perspective; summing by columns gives another; equating them turns local degree rules into global conservation laws.

2 · Meet the problem: the nine dinner guests and the vertex–edge ledger

Nine guests attend a dinner party. At the end of the evening, each guest reports that they shook hands with exactly \(3\) other guests. Could every guest's report be accurate?

If you try to test this by drawing \(9\) dots on a page and connecting them with lines so that every dot touches \(3\) lines, you will repeatedly get \(8\) dots to have \(3\) lines while the \(9\)-th dot is stuck with \(2\) or \(4\). Why is \(9\) guests with \(3\) handshakes each impossible, no matter how cleverly the handshakes are arranged?

Instead of searching through graphs, let us build a ledger whose rows are the guests (vertices \(v \in V\)) and whose columns are the handshakes (edges \(e \in E\)). Put a \(1\) in row \(v\), column \(e\) whenever guest \(v\) participated in handshake \(e\), and \(0\) otherwise. To see how the ledger works, inspect the complete table for the \(4\)-vertex, \(5\)-edge "diamond graph" with edges \(e_1 = \{v_1, v_2\}\), \(e_2 = \{v_1, v_3\}\), \(e_3 = \{v_2, v_3\}\), \(e_4 = \{v_2, v_4\}\), \(e_5 = \{v_3, v_4\}\):

Vertex \(\backslash\) Edge \(e_1 = \{v_1,v_2\}\) \(e_2 = \{v_1,v_3\}\) \(e_3 = \{v_2,v_3\}\) \(e_4 = \{v_2,v_4\}\) \(e_5 = \{v_3,v_4\}\) Row sum \(\deg(v_i)\)
\(v_1\) \(1\) \(1\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(2\)
\(v_2\) \(1\) \(0\) \(1\) \(1\) \(0\) \(3\)
\(v_3\) \(0\) \(1\) \(1\) \(0\) \(1\) \(3\)
\(v_4\) \(0\) \(0\) \(0\) \(1\) \(1\) \(2\)
Column sum \(2\) \(2\) \(2\) \(2\) \(2\) Corner Total \(= 10\)

Count the total number of \(1\)s in this table in two ways:

  • By rows (vertices): Row \(v\) contains \(\deg(v)\) ones (the number of handshakes guest \(v\) made). Summing across all rows gives \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)\) (here \(2 + 3 + 3 + 2 = 10\)).
  • By columns (edges): Every handshake \(e = \{u, w\}\) involves exactly \(2\) guests, so every column sum is \(2\)! Summing across all \(|E|\) columns gives \(2|E|\) (here \(5 \times 2 = 10\)).

Equating the row total and the column total proves Euler's Handshaking Lemma (1736):

\[ \sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) \;=\; 2|E|. \]

Now return to our \(9\) dinner guests: if each of the \(9\) guests had \(\deg(v) = 3\), the sum of the row totals would be \(9 \times 3 = 27\). But the sum of the column totals is \(2|E|\), which is always an even integer! Since \(27\) is odd, no such party can ever exist.

3 · Name the move

Box P70 · The Double Counting Move

To prove a combinatorial identity \(\sum_{a \in A} r(a) = \sum_{b \in B} c(b)\) or bound the size of a structure, define a finite incidence set of pairs

\[ \mathcal{I} \;\subseteq\; A \times B \]

such that for each \(a \in A\) there are \(r(a) = |\{b \in B : (a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\}|\) incident columns, and for each \(b \in B\) there are \(c(b) = |\{a \in A : (a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\}|\) incident rows. Counting \(|\mathcal{I}|\) first grouped by \(A\) and then grouped by \(B\) forces

\[ \sum_{a \in A} r(a) \;=\; |\mathcal{I}| \;=\; \sum_{b \in B} c(b). \]

Box P70.A · The Four Verification Obligations of Double Counting

  1. Specify the Row Set \(A\), Column Set \(B\), and Incidence Condition \(\mathcal{I}\): State clearly what constitutes an ordered pair \((a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\) (for example, "\(a\) is a \(k\)-element subset of \(\{1,\dots,n\}\) and \(b \in a\) is a chosen member").
  2. Evaluate the Row Counts \(r(a)\) and Column Counts \(c(b)\): For a fixed \(a \in A\), count how many \(b \in B\) satisfy \((a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\); for a fixed \(b \in B\), count how many \(a \in A\) satisfy \((a, b) \in \mathcal{I}\).
  3. Audit Boundary Cases & Multiplicities: Check whether boundary elements (such as exterior edges of a polygon triangulation) or degenerate elements (such as self-loops in a graph) have a different column count or multiplicity than interior elements.
  4. Equate Totals (or Chain Row/Column Inequalities): Set \(\sum_{a \in A} r(a) = \sum_{b \in B} c(b)\) to prove an exact identity, or use \(|A| \cdot \min_a r(a) \le |\mathcal{I}| \le |B| \cdot \max_b c(b)\) to bound \(|A| / |B|\).

Box P70.B · Three-Key Recognition Diagnosis

  • Claim type: An identity equating two sums (or a sum and a closed form), a parity obstruction ("the number of odd-degree vertices/doors is even"), or an extremal bound on edges, subsets, or geometric incidences.
  • Problem shape: Two distinct families of objects (vertices & edges, subsets & elements, integers & divisors, points & lines, triangles & edges) linked by a binary relation "belongs to / touches / divides".
  • Tools at hand: \(0\text{–}1\) incidence matrices, bipartite graphs, Cauchy–Schwarz on row sums (\(\sum r(a)^2 \ge |\mathcal{I}|^2 / |A|\)), and summation order interchange \(\sum_i \sum_j = \sum_j \sum_i\).

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 (Combinatorics) · The committee-with-chair identity and weighted subset sums

Problem. For any integers \(1 \le k \le n\), prove without expanding factorials or taking derivatives that

\[ k \binom{n}{k} \;=\; n \binom{n - 1}{k - 1}, \qquad\text{and deduce that}\qquad \sum_{k=1}^n k \binom{n}{k} \;=\; n\,2^{n-1}. \]

Solution.

Step 1 (Define the incidence set of pointed committees). Let \(A\) be the set of all \(k\)-element subsets (committees) \(S \subseteq \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\), let \(B = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) be the set of people, and define the incidence set

\[ \mathcal{I} \;=\; \bigl\{(S, x) \in A \times B \;:\; |S| = k \text{ and } x \in S\bigr\}, \]

where \((S, x) \in \mathcal{I}\) represents a \(k\)-person committee \(S\) together with a designated chairperson \(x \in S\) (illustrated for \(n = 4, k = 2\) in Fig P70.2).

Step 2 (Count \(|\mathcal{I}|\) by rows \(A\) and by columns \(B\)).

  • Grouped by committees \(S \in A\): There are \(|A| = \binom{n}{k}\) committees of size \(k\). For each committee \(S\), any of its \(k\) members can be chosen as chairperson, so \(r(S) = k\). Thus \(|\mathcal{I}| = k\binom{n}{k}\).
  • Grouped by chairpersons \(x \in B\): There are \(|B| = n\) choices for the chairperson \(x\). Once \(x\) is chosen, the remaining \(k - 1\) members of \(S \setminus \{x\}\) can be chosen from the remaining \(n - 1\) people in \(c(x) = \binom{n-1}{k-1}\) ways. Thus \(|\mathcal{I}| = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}\).

Equating the two counts proves \(k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}\). Summing over all \(k = 1, \dots, n\) (or counting pointed committees \((S, x)\) of any size directly: each of the \(n\) chairs \(x\) can be joined by any subset of the remaining \(n - 1\) people in \(2^{n-1}\) ways) yields

\[ \sum_{k=1}^n k \binom{n}{k} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^n n \binom{n - 1}{k - 1} \;=\; n \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \binom{n - 1}{j} \;=\; n\,2^{n-1}. \]

Exemplar 2 (Number Theory) · Turning the erratic divisor count \(\tau(m)\) into the smooth harmonic staircase \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\)

Problem. Let \(\tau(m)\) denote the number of positive divisors of \(m\). Prove that for every positive integer \(N\),

\[ \sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m) \;=\; \sum_{d=1}^N \left\lfloor \frac{N}{d} \right\rfloor, \]

and deduce that the average number of divisors of an integer in \(\{1, \dots, N\}\) satisfies \(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m) = \ln N + O(1)\).

Solution.

Step 1 (Build the divisibility incidence matrix). Individual values of \(\tau(m)\) jump erratically — primes have \(\tau(p) = 2\), while highly composite numbers have many divisors. To tame \(\sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m)\), form the \(N \times N\) incidence table \(\mathcal{I} = \{(d, m) \in \{1, \dots, N\}^2 : d \mid m\}\), placing a \(1\) in row \(d\), column \(m\) whenever \(d\) divides \(m\) (shown for \(N = 6\) in Fig P70.3, left).

Step 2 (Compare column sums against row sums).

  • By columns \(m \in \{1, \dots, N\}\): Column \(m\) has a \(1\) at every divisor \(d \mid m\), so its column sum is \(c(m) = \tau(m)\), and the sum of all columns is \(\sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m)\).
  • By rows \(d \in \{1, \dots, N\}\): Row \(d\) has a \(1\) at every multiple of \(d\) up to \(N\), namely \(m \in \{d, 2d, 3d, \dots, \lfloor N/d \rfloor d\}\). Thus row \(d\) contains exactly \(r(d) = \lfloor N / d \rfloor\) ones!

Equating the column total and the row total gives the exact identity \(\sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m) = \sum_{d=1}^N \lfloor N / d \rfloor\). Since \(\frac{N}{d} - 1 < \lfloor \frac{N}{d} \rfloor \le \frac{N}{d}\), each floor term differs from \(\frac{N}{d}\) by less than \(1\), so

\[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{m=1}^N \tau(m) \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{d=1}^N \left(\frac{N}{d} + O(1)\right) \;=\; \sum_{d=1}^N \frac{1}{d} + O(1) \;=\; \ln N + O(1). \]

Exemplar 3 (Extremal Graph Theory) · Cherry double-counting in \(C_4\)-free graphs (Kővári–Sós–Turán)

Problem. Suppose a simple graph \(G = (V, E)\) on \(n\) vertices contains no \(4\)-cycle \(C_4\). Prove that the number of edges \(|E|\) satisfies

\[ \sum_{v \in V} \binom{\deg(v)}{2} \;\le\; \binom{n}{2}, \qquad\text{and consequently}\qquad |E| \;\le\; \frac{n}{4}\bigl(1 + \sqrt{4n - 3}\bigr) \;=\; O\bigl(n^{3/2}\bigr). \]

Solution.

Step 1 (Double-count cherries). A cherry is a \(2\)-edge path \((v;\, \{u, w\})\) consisting of a center vertex \(v \in V\) and an unordered pair of distinct vertices \(\{u, w\} \in \binom{V}{2}\) that are both adjacent to \(v\). Let \(\mathcal{I}\) be the set of all cherries in \(G\):

  • Grouped by the center vertex \(v \in V\): Vertex \(v\) has \(\deg(v)\) neighbors, and any unordered pair of neighbors of \(v\) forms a cherry centered at \(v\). Thus \(r(v) = \binom{\deg(v)}{2}\), giving \(|\mathcal{I}| = \sum_{v \in V} \binom{\deg(v)}{2}\).
  • Grouped by the endpoint pair \(\{u, w\} \in \binom{V}{2}\): If a pair \(\{u, w\}\) had two distinct common neighbors \(v_1 \neq v_2\), then \(u - v_1 - w - v_2 - u\) would form a \(4\)-cycle \(C_4\)! Because \(G\) is \(C_4\)-free, every pair \(\{u, w\}\) has at most \(1\) common neighbor (\(c(\{u, w\}) \le 1\)).

Summing over all \(\binom{n}{2}\) pairs \(\{u, w\}\) yields the fundamental cherry inequality:

\[ \sum_{v \in V} \frac{\deg(v)\bigl(\deg(v) - 1\bigr)}{2} \;=\; |\mathcal{I}| \;=\; \sum_{\{u, w\} \in \binom{V}{2}} c(\{u, w\}) \;\le\; \binom{n}{2} \;=\; \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}. \]

Step 2 (Apply Cauchy–Schwarz to relate \(\sum \deg(v)^2\) to \(2|E|\)). By the Handshaking Lemma, \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|\). By Cauchy–Schwarz (P15), \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)^2 \ge \frac{(2|E|)^2}{n} = \frac{4|E|^2}{n}\). Substituting this into \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v)(\deg(v) - 1) \le n(n - 1)\) gives the quadratic inequality

\[ \frac{4|E|^2}{n} - 2|E| \;\le\; n(n - 1) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 4|E|^2 - 2n|E| - n^2(n - 1) \;\le\; 0, \]

whose positive root (P04) gives \(|E| \le \frac{n}{4}\bigl(1 + \sqrt{4n - 3}\bigr)\).

5 · When the trick breaks

Exhibit A — Treating boundary elements and interior elements as if they had the same column count. Suppose a convex \(k\)-gon is partitioned into \(T\) triangles by non-intersecting diagonals, creating \(E_{\text{int}}\) interior edges and \(E_{\text{bd}} = k\) boundary edges around the perimeter (Fig P70.3, right). If you double-count the pairs (triangle \(t\), edge \(e\) of \(t\)) and carelessly write \(3T = 2E\), your formula fails! Why? Because an interior edge belongs to \(2\) triangles, whereas a boundary edge belongs to only \(1\) triangle.

Mitigation: Split the column set \(B\) into interior columns \(B_{\text{int}}\) and boundary columns \(B_{\text{bd}}\): \(3T = 2E_{\text{int}} + 1 \cdot E_{\text{bd}}\).

Price: Tracking boundary terms explicitly (which often reveals the key topological invariant!).

Default: Always check Obligation 3 of Box P70.A on boundary edges/vertices before equating totals.

Exhibit B — Confusing ordered pairs \((u, w)\) with unordered pairs \(\{u, w\}\) creates a factor-of-\(2\) discrepancy. In the Handshaking Lemma, if you define \(\mathcal{I}\) as ordered pairs of adjacent vertices \((u, w)\) with \(u \sim w\), then each edge \(\{u, w\}\) contributes \(2\) ordered pairs \((u, w)\) and \((w, u)\). If you forget whether your second coordinate is an ordered pair or an unordered \(2\)-element subset, your equation will be off by a factor of \(2\).

Mitigation: Always test your incidence identity on a tiny graph or set first (P02), such as a single edge \(K_2\) (\(n = 2, |E| = 1\)) or a triangle \(K_3\) (\(n = 3, |E| = 3\)).

Price: Ten seconds to plug in \(n = 2\) or \(n = 3\).

Default: Write \(\{u, w\} \in \binom{V}{2}\) for unordered pairs and \((u, w) \in V \times V\) for ordered pairs.

Exhibit C — Swapping summation order in an infinite matrix without absolute convergence (Fubini failure). Consider the infinite matrix \(M_{i,j}\) (\(i, j \ge 1\)) with \(M_{i,i} = +1\) on the main diagonal, \(M_{i, i+1} = -1\) on the superdiagonal, and \(0\) everywhere else. Every row sum is \(+1 + (-1) = 0\), so \(\sum_{i=1}^\infty \bigl(\sum_{j=1}^\infty M_{i,j}\bigr) = 0\). Yet column \(1\) sums to \(+1\) and every column \(j \ge 2\) sums to \(-1 + 1 = 0\), so \(\sum_{j=1}^\infty \bigl(\sum_{i=1}^\infty M_{i,j}\bigr) = +1 \neq 0\)!

Mitigation: For infinite double sums or integrals, verify either that all entries are nonnegative (\(M_{i,j} \ge 0\), Tonelli's Theorem) or that the sum of absolute values converges (\(\sum_{i,j} |M_{i,j}| < \infty\), Fubini's Theorem).

Price: Checking absolute convergence before swapping infinite sums.

Default: Keep combinatorial double counting on finite sets \(A, B\) (or truncate up to \(N\) before taking limits, as in Exemplar 2).

Honest scope of this chapter. On finite sets, double counting is an unconditional algebraic identity. In measure theory and analysis, its continuous twin is the Fubini–Tonelli Theorem (\(\int_X \int_Y f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_Y \int_X f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\)), and in differential geometry and topology, boundary-vs.-interior double counting (Exhibit A) becomes Stokes' Theorem \(\int_{\Omega} d\omega = \int_{\partial \Omega} \omega\), where interior facet contributions cancel in opposite-orientation pairs and only the boundary survives.

7 · Lineage interlude

From Euler's degree sum and Cauchy's double series to Fubini, Sperner, and extremal incidence geometry (1736 – 1954).

  • Leonhard Euler (1736): In his paper on the Seven Bridges of Königsberg, Euler observed that because each bridge connects two landmasses, the sum of bridge incidences across all landmasses is twice the number of bridges — the birth of both graph theory and double counting.
  • Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1821) & Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet (1849): Cauchy formalized row-vs.-column summation for double arrays \(\sum_i \sum_j a_{i,j}\) (alongside his famous counterexample showing that conditionally convergent infinite arrays can change value when transposed). In 1849, Dirichlet introduced his hyperbola method, refining the divisor incidence matrix \(\sum_{m \le N} \tau(m) = \sum_{d \le N} \lfloor N/d \rfloor\) by splitting the region \(d k \le N\) along the diagonal \(d = \sqrt{N}\) to obtain \(\sum_{m \le N} \tau(m) = N \ln N + (2\gamma - 1)N + O(\sqrt{N})\).
  • Guido Fubini (1907) & Leonida Tonelli (1909): Extending double counting from finite grids to Lebesgue measure spaces, Fubini and Tonelli established the exact hypotheses (nonnegativity or absolute integrability) under which iterated integrals \(\int_X \int_Y f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\) and \(\int_Y \int_X f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\) coincide.
  • Emanuel Sperner (1928) & Tamás Kővári, Vera T. Sós, Pál Turán (1954): Sperner used parity double counting across simplex facets to give an elementary combinatorial proof of Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, and Kővári, Sós, and Turán used cherry double counting to bound the maximum number of edges in bipartite-subgraph-free graphs, launching modern extremal incidence geometry.

8 · Exercises

Exercise P70.1 (●○○ — Odd-degree parity and regular bipartite graphs).

  1. In any finite simple graph \(G = (V, E)\), partition the vertices into \(V_{\text{even}} = \{v \in V : \deg(v) \text{ is even}\}\) and \(V_{\text{odd}} = \{v \in V : \deg(v) \text{ is odd}\}\). Use the Handshaking Lemma \(\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2|E|\) to prove that \(|V_{\text{odd}}|\) is always an even integer.
  2. Suppose \(G = (L \sqcup R, E)\) is a bipartite graph in which every edge has one endpoint in \(L\) and one endpoint in \(R\). If every vertex in \(L\) and every vertex in \(R\) has the same degree \(d \ge 1\), prove by double counting \(|E|\) that \(|L| = |R|\).
Hint

For Part 1, look at \(\sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) = 2|E| - \sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v)\) modulo \(2\). For Part 2, count the edges \(E\) first from the \(L\)-endpoints and then from the \(R\)-endpoints.

Worked answer

1. Even number of odd-degree vertices. Splitting the Handshaking Lemma across \(V_{\text{even}}\) and \(V_{\text{odd}}\) gives

\[ \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) \;=\; 2|E| \,-\, \sum_{v \in V_{\text{even}}} \deg(v). \]

Every term on the right-hand side is even, so the sum on the left must be even. Since each summand \(\deg(v)\) for \(v \in V_{\text{odd}}\) is odd (\(\equiv 1 \pmod 2\)), we have \(|V_{\text{odd}}| \equiv \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} 1 \equiv \sum_{v \in V_{\text{odd}}} \deg(v) \equiv 0 \pmod 2\), so \(|V_{\text{odd}}|\) is even.

2. Equal parts in a \(d\)-regular bipartite graph. Because every edge \(e \in E\) has \(1\) endpoint in \(L\) and \(1\) endpoint in \(R\), counting incidences between \(L\) and \(E\) gives \(|E| = \sum_{u \in L} \deg(u) = d\,|L|\), while counting incidences between \(R\) and \(E\) gives \(|E| = \sum_{w \in R} \deg(w) = d\,|R|\). Equating the two gives \(d\,|L| = d\,|R|\), and dividing by \(d \ge 1\) yields \(|L| = |R|\).

Exercise P70.2 (●●○ — Invariant triangle and edge counts in polygon triangulations). Let \(P\) be a polygon with \(B\) boundary vertices (and \(B\) boundary edges), and let \(I\) interior points be chosen inside \(P\). Suppose \(P\) is partitioned into \(T\) triangles whose vertices are precisely the \(B + I\) points (with no point lying in the interior of any triangle or edge), creating \(E_{\text{int}}\) interior edges.

  1. Double-count the sum of all \(3T\) interior angles of the \(T\) triangles — first grouped by the \(T\) triangles, and second grouped around the \(I\) interior vertices and \(B\) boundary vertices — to prove that \(T = 2I + B - 2\).
  2. Double-count the pairs (triangle \(t\), edge \(e\) of \(t\)), distinguishing interior edges from boundary edges, to prove that \(E_{\text{int}} = 3I + B - 3\).
Hint

Each triangle has angle sum \(\pi\). Around each of the \(I\) interior vertices, the angles form a full circle \(2\pi\); at the \(B\) boundary vertices, the angles sum to the interior angle sum of a \(B\)-gon, which is \((B - 2)\pi\).

Worked answer

1. Angle double-counting for \(T\). Let \(\Sigma_\theta\) be the sum of all \(3T\) corner angles across all \(T\) triangles:

  • Grouped by triangles: Each of the \(T\) triangles has angle sum \(\pi\), so \(\Sigma_\theta = \pi T\).
  • Grouped by vertices: The angles meeting at each of the \(I\) interior vertices sweep out a full \(2\pi\) circle (contributing \(2\pi I\)), while the angles meeting at the \(B\) boundary vertices partition the interior angles of the \(B\)-gon (contributing \((B - 2)\pi\)).

Equating the two expressions and dividing by \(\pi\) gives

\[ \pi T \;=\; 2\pi I + (B - 2)\pi \quad\implies\quad T \;=\; 2I + B - 2. \]

2. Edge–triangle double-counting for \(E_{\text{int}}\). Let \(\mathcal{I}\) be the set of pairs \((t, e)\) where \(e\) is one of the \(3\) sides of triangle \(t\):

  • Grouped by triangles: Each of the \(T\) triangles has \(3\) sides, so \(|\mathcal{I}| = 3T\).
  • Grouped by edges: Each of the \(E_{\text{int}}\) interior edges borders \(2\) triangles, while each of the \(B\) boundary edges borders \(1\) triangle, so \(|\mathcal{I}| = 2E_{\text{int}} + B\).

Equating \(2E_{\text{int}} + B = 3T = 3(2I + B - 2) = 6I + 3B - 6\) gives \(2E_{\text{int}} = 6I + 2B - 6 \implies E_{\text{int}} = 3I + B - 3\).

Exercise P70.3 (●●● — Gauss's totient theorem and weighted divisor double counting). Recall Euler's totient function \(\varphi(d) = |\{a \in \{1, \dots, d\} : \gcd(a, d) = 1\}|\).

  1. For a fixed positive integer \(m\), consider the \(m\) fractions \(\frac{1}{m}, \frac{2}{m}, \dots, \frac{m}{m}\). When each fraction \(\frac{k}{m}\) is reduced to lowest terms \(\frac{a}{d}\) (by dividing numerator and denominator by \(\gcd(k, m)\)), its denominator \(d = \frac{m}{\gcd(k, m)}\) is a divisor of \(m\) and \(\gcd(a, d) = 1\). Group the \(m\) fractions by their reduced denominator \(d \mid m\) to prove Gauss's identity: \[ \sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) \;=\; m. \]
  2. Weight row \(d\) of the \(N \times N\) divisibility matrix \(M_{d,m} = \mathbf{1}_{d \mid m}\) from Exemplar 2 by \(\varphi(d)\) and double-count the matrix sum to prove that for every \(N \ge 1\), \[ \sum_{d=1}^N \varphi(d) \left\lfloor \frac{N}{d} \right\rfloor \;=\; \frac{N(N + 1)}{2}. \] Verify this identity numerically for \(N = 6\).
Hint

In Part 2, the \((d, m)\) entry of the weighted matrix is \(\varphi(d)\,\mathbf{1}_{d \mid m}\). Column \(m\) sums to \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\), while row \(d\) has \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\) entries each equal to \(\varphi(d)\).

Worked answer

1. Grouping the \(m\) fractions \(\frac{k}{m}\) by reduced denominator \(d \mid m\). Every fraction \(\frac{k}{m}\) (\(1 \le k \le m\)) reduces uniquely to \(\frac{a}{d}\) where \(d \mid m\), \(1 \le a \le d\), and \(\gcd(a, d) = 1\). Conversely, for every divisor \(d \mid m\) and every \(1 \le a \le d\) with \(\gcd(a, d) = 1\), multiplying top and bottom by \(m/d\) recovers a unique fraction \(\frac{a(m/d)}{m}\) in our list. Since each divisor \(d \mid m\) has \(\varphi(d)\) such numerators \(a\), partitioning the \(m\) fractions by \(d \mid m\) gives \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\).

2. Double-counting the \(\varphi(d)\)-weighted divisibility table. Define the \(N \times N\) matrix with entry \(W_{d,m} = \varphi(d)\) if \(d \mid m\) and \(0\) otherwise:

  • Sum by columns \(m = 1, \dots, N\): Column \(m\) sums to \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\) by Part 1. Summing over \(m = 1, \dots, N\) gives \(\sum_{m=1}^N m = \frac{N(N+1)}{2}\).
  • Sum by rows \(d = 1, \dots, N\): Row \(d\) has \(\lfloor N / d \rfloor\) nonzero entries, each equal to \(\varphi(d)\), so row \(d\) sums to \(\varphi(d)\lfloor N / d \rfloor\).

Equating the two sums proves \(\sum_{d=1}^N \varphi(d) \lfloor N / d \rfloor = \frac{N(N+1)}{2}\). For \(N = 6\), \(\varphi(1..6) = (1, 1, 2, 2, 4, 2)\) and \(\lfloor 6/d \rfloor = (6, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1)\), giving

\[ 1(6) + 1(3) + 2(2) + 2(1) + 4(1) + 2(1) \;=\; 6 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 \;=\; 21 \;=\; \frac{6 \times 7}{2}. \]

Exercise P70.4 (◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis: IMO 1998 Problem 2). In a competition, there are \(a\) contestants and \(b\) judges, where \(b \ge 3\) is an odd integer. Each judge rates each contestant as either "pass" or "fail". Suppose \(k\) is a number such that, for any two judges, their ratings agree on at most \(k\) contestants. Prove that

\[ \frac{k}{a} \;\ge\; \frac{b - 1}{2b}. \]
Worked answer

1. Pattern diagnosis (P70 · Double counting + P04 · Complete the square). The hypothesis bounds agreements per pair of judges (\(\le k\) contestants), while we want to relate \(k\) to the total number of contestants \(a\). This is a classic row-vs.-column inequality between the set \(A\) of \(a\) contestants and the set \(B = \binom{\text{Judges}}{2}\) of \(\binom{b}{2}\) unordered pairs of judges. Define the incidence set

\[ \mathcal{I} \;=\; \bigl\{\bigl(C, \{J_1, J_2\}\bigr) \in A \times B \;:\; \text{judges } J_1 \text{ and } J_2 \text{ give contestant } C \text{ the same rating}\bigr\}. \]

2. Upper bound by columns (judge pairs). There are \(|B| = \binom{b}{2} = \frac{b(b-1)}{2}\) pairs of judges, and each pair agrees on at most \(k\) contestants (\(c(\{J_1, J_2\}) \le k\)). Therefore,

\[ |\mathcal{I}| \;=\; \sum_{\{J_1, J_2\} \in B} c\bigl(\{J_1, J_2\}\bigr) \;\le\; \binom{b}{2} k \;=\; \frac{b(b - 1)}{2}\,k. \]

3. Lower bound by rows (contestants). Fix a contestant \(C \in A\), and suppose \(x\) judges rate \(C\) "pass" while \(b - x\) judges rate \(C\) "fail". Two judges agree on \(C\) if and only if both say "pass" or both say "fail", so the row sum is

\[ r(C) \;=\; \binom{x}{2} + \binom{b - x}{2} \;=\; \frac{x(x - 1) + (b - x)(b - x - 1)}{2} \;=\; \frac{2x^2 - 2bx + b(b - 1)}{2}. \]

Completing the square in \(x\) (P04) gives \(2x^2 - 2bx = \frac{1}{2}(2x - b)^2 - \frac{b^2}{2}\). Because \(b\) is an odd integer and \(x\) is an integer, \(2x - b\) is a nonzero odd integer, so \((2x - b)^2 \ge 1^2 = 1\)! (Equivalently, the closest split is \(x = \frac{b-1}{2}\) and \(b - x = \frac{b+1}{2}\).) Hence for every contestant \(C\),

\[ r(C) \;\ge\; \frac{\frac{1}{2}(1) - \frac{b^2}{2} + b(b - 1)}{2} \;=\; \frac{b^2 - 2b + 1}{4} \;=\; \frac{(b - 1)^2}{4}. \]

Summing over all \(a\) contestants and comparing with the column upper bound gives

\[ a \cdot \frac{(b - 1)^2}{4} \;\le\; |\mathcal{I}| \;\le\; \frac{b(b - 1)}{2}\,k \quad\implies\quad \frac{k}{a} \;\ge\; \frac{b - 1}{2b}. \]

Exercise P70.5 (◐ — Exploration: Sperner's Lemma via the "Door-in-the-Room" parity double count).

  1. 1D Sperner Lemma: Divide a line segment into \(m\) small subsegments \(I_1, \dots, I_m\) by vertices \(v_0, v_1, \dots, v_m\). Color the left endpoint \(v_0\) with color \(1\), the right endpoint \(v_m\) with color \(2\), and every interior vertex \(v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}\) arbitrarily with color \(1\) or \(2\). Call a subsegment \(I_j\) bichromatic if its two endpoints have different colors \(\{1, 2\}\). By double-counting incidences \((v, I_j)\) where vertex \(v\) has color \(1\) and is an endpoint of \(I_j\), prove that the number of bichromatic subsegments is odd (and hence at least \(1\)).
  2. 2D Sperner Lemma (Cohen's Door-Room Proof, 1967): Now triangulate a large triangle whose vertices are colored \(1, 2, 3\), and color every vertex of the triangulation with \(\{1, 2, 3\}\). Call a small triangle a "room" and any edge colored \(\{1, 2\}\) a "door". How many \(\{1, 2\}\)-doors can a single triangle room have depending on whether its three vertices are colored \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) (panchromatic) or not? Deduce by double-counting (room, door) incidences modulo \(2\) that if the outer boundary has an odd number of \(\{1, 2\}\)-doors, then the triangulation must contain an odd number of panchromatic \(\{1, 2, 3\}\)-triangles!
Hint

In 1D, a \(\{1, 1\}\)-segment has \(2\) endpoints of color \(1\), a \(\{1, 2\}\)-segment has \(1\), and a \(\{2, 2\}\)-segment has \(0\); meanwhile each interior color-\(1\) vertex touches \(2\) segments while the boundary endpoint \(v_0\) touches \(1\). In 2D, a \(\{1, 2, 3\}\)-triangle has \(1\) door (\(\{1,2\}\)), a \(\{1, 1, 2\}\)- or \(\{1, 2, 2\}\)-triangle has \(2\) doors, and all other triangles have \(0\) doors!

Worked answer

1. 1D Sperner parity double-count. Let \(\mathcal{I}\) be the set of pairs \((v, I_j)\) where \(v\) is an endpoint of subsegment \(I_j\) and \(\text{color}(v) = 1\):

  • By subsegments \(I_j\): A \(\{1, 1\}\)-segment contributes \(2\) to \(|\mathcal{I}|\), a bichromatic \(\{1, 2\}\)-segment contributes \(1\), and a \(\{2, 2\}\)-segment contributes \(0\). Thus \(|\mathcal{I}| \equiv \#\{\text{bichromatic } \{1,2\}\text{-segments}\} \pmod 2\).
