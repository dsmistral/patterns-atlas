The Patterns Atlas · P27 · the eighth move of tier 3

Complex analysis for integer problems

Some integer questions — how fast a count grows, what an infinite sum of reciprocals weighs — refuse every algebra we own, and the obstruction is never on the real line. The move: let the variable off the line, read the question where the object breaks, and let the break's location and shape carry the arithmetic. Factoring, it turns out, is how the plane tells the integers their secrets.

New word: radius of convergence — a distance, not a point. A shelf series \(A(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + a_2 z^2 + \cdots\) is more than a filing system: it is also an honest sum whenever it settles. Where does it settle? On a disk: if \(z\) has size \(r\), the shelves grow no faster than some rate \(\rho\), and the sum settles when \(r \rho < 1\). The honest statement — and the one this page works to death — is that the series converges inside the circle of radius \(1/\rho\) and not outside it, with \(\rho\) the growth rate of the shelves. P73's Exhibit A said this in one sentence: the nearest break sets the radius. This page makes the sentence precise, and the precision forces a change of scenery — a distance is measured in the plane, to every break the object has, on the line or off it.
New word: analytic, in the plane's sense. A function is analytic on an open region if, at every point of the region, it is its own shelf series — some disk around each point where the function equals a convergent power series. Sums, products, quotients (denominators nonzero), and compositions of analytic functions are analytic; a quotient is analytic exactly where its denominator does not vanish. The word for a point where a function is not analytic, though it is analytic at every nearby point — a place the object breaks — is a singularity. The two specimens this page meets are the pole (the function blows up like a fixed negative power of the distance, as \(\frac{1}{1-z}\) does at \(z=1\)) and the branch point (the function has no single honest value past the point, as \(\sqrt{1-z}\) has none past \(z=1\)) — the pole ends the bookkeeping gently, the branch point ends it in a tear.
New word: analytic continuation. A shelf series knows only its disk — outside the radius it says nothing, and inside it says everything, for the series is the function there. But the function defined by a formula often outlives its series: \(\frac{1}{1+x^2}\) is a perfectly honest function at \(x=2\), even though its shelf series at the origin stopped at radius \(1\). Writing down the function somewhere new — the same object, defined past the break of the series that introduced it — is analytic continuation, and its honesty is a theorem this page quotes but does not prove: when a continuation exists at all, it is unique, because two analytic functions that agree on any open patch agree everywhere they are both defined. The series is a local chapter; the function is the book.

1 · Before we begin

Four pages built this one. P06 taught us to put a stuck object inside a composition with one we control — its two-squares recipe will return, once removed, as the shadow of this page's exemplars. P16 taught us that a problem may be the same problem in different clothes, and that translating between wardrobes is a mathematical act. P24 split a signal into waves and found the sine at the bottom of its ladder — a function determined, as we will see, by nothing but its zeros. And P73 hung whole families of counts on a clothesline, extracted exact answers by pure shelf algebra, and then stopped itself, honestly, at the door this page opens. Its asymptotic door said, verbatim: "Pushed honestly, the read walks straight through the radius, because past a singularity a series can only continue by complex-analytic means — and counting questions turn analytic. The page that owns that crossing is complex analysis for integer problems (P27, planned) — named here as a door, with its machinery owed, not silently assumed."

Here is the tension this page resolves. P73's algebra is exact where it works and blind where it matters for growth: it can hand us \(c_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\) exactly, yet no shelf algebra anywhere will say that \(c_n\) grows like \(4^n\) divided by \(n^{3/2}\) and a constant involving \(\pi\). Growth claims, P73 kept insisting, are borrowed analytic statements that must be labeled. But borrowed from where? The honest answer runs deeper than a lending library: the growth of the shelves is governed by where the object breaks — and the breaks, this page's encounter will show, need not be anywhere on the real line. To read an integer question's asymptotics we must let the variable roam the whole plane, factor what we find there, and read the arithmetic off the wreckage. The algebra answered exact questions; the plane answers the ones algebra cannot even state.

2 · The encounter

Take the simplest object P73 never quite examined: \[ W(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1+z^2}. \] File it on shelves first, the way P02 taught us to begin. It is geometric in \(z^2\): \[ \frac{1}{1+z^2} \;=\; 1 \;-\; z^2 \;+\; z^4 \;-\; z^6 \;+\; \cdots, \] so the census of shelves reads \(1,\; 0,\; -1,\; 0,\; 1,\; 0,\; -1,\; 0,\; \ldots\) — perfectly periodic, period four, the alternation of the geometric series stamped onto every second shelf. So far, bookkeeping.

Now ask the question that breaks the bookkeeping. The shelf series reaches exactly to \(z = 1\) and refuses to settle: at \(z=1\) the shelves read \(1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots\) and never agree; at \(z = 2\) they blow up outright. Yet look at the object the shelves were filing. At \(z = 1\) the fraction is \(\frac{1}{2}\) — smooth, finite, ordinary. At \(z = 2\) it is \(\frac{1}{5}\). At \(z = 100\) it is \(\frac{1}{10001}\). On the entire real line the denominator \(1 + z^2\) never vanishes — there is no break anywhere on the line, of any kind, at any size. The series stopped at radius \(1\) for a reason that is invisible from the line: nothing on the line breaks.

Something broke at distance exactly \(1\) from the origin — and since it was not on the line, we must look off it. Factor the denominator, allowing the wider number system its say: \[ 1 + z^2 \;=\; (1 - iz)(1 + iz). \] The first factor vanishes at \(z = i\), one unit up the imaginary axis; the second at \(z = -i\), one unit down. Each sits at distance exactly \(1\) from the origin (Fig P27.1). The series knew — the radius of convergence is a distance to the nearest break in the plane, and the nearest breaks of this object are two points the real line never visits. Factoring the denominator revealed exactly where the arithmetic of the shelves is decided; on the line the function is innocent, and its guilt is stored off the line.

And the shelves' own shape — the census \(1, 0, -1, 0, 1, 0, -1, 0\) — is the shadow of those breaks' placement. The breaks sit at distance \(1\) (so the shelves neither grow nor decay) and at right angles to the line (so they oscillate, period four, rather than alternate period two the way a break at \(z = -1\) alone would force). Location sets the rate; angle sets the oscillation. Two numbers — a distance and a direction — already read the census. What a full theory of breaks could read from a whole family of objects, no algebra on the line could even ask: the constants the next sections extract, the growth laws, the exact prices of infinite sums. The machine that does this has a name, and the next section gives it one.

3 · The pattern

Pattern P27 — Complex analysis for integer problems. When a question about integers — the growth of a family of counts, the exact weight of an infinite sum, the law hiding in a table — is filed as a function \(A(z) = \sum a_n z^n\), stop treating \(z\) as a bookmark. Let \(z\) roam the plane and ask the three analytic questions: where does the object break (the singularities), which break is nearest the origin (its distance is the radius, its reciprocal the shelves' growth rate), and what shape does the object have near that break (a pole prices a pure geometric; a branch point prices a geometric times a power of \(n\); a wall of breaks prices nothing — the read stops). Then read the integer answer off the break: the growth law from the location, the correction from the shape, the mysterious constants — the \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\), the \(\pi^2/6\) — from the local form of the wreckage. Factoring is the act that reveals the structure: factoring a denominator to find its zeros, factoring an entire function over its zeros, factoring the integers into primes to build the function at all.

Or, in words: hang the question where P73 hung it, then ask what the whole plane says. Smoothness on the real line is a shadow; the shelves obey the nearest place the object cannot be continued; and the arithmetic the algebra could not see is stored, precisely and recoverably, in how the object breaks.

Box P27.A — the three obligations. A growth claim read off a break is a certificate, and three cheap checks police it, in deliberate echo of the encounter. First, name the nearest singularity before any claim: its distance is the radius, and a radius asserted without its break is a number quoted from nowhere — this is the encounter's whole lesson. Second, exhibit the local shape at the break: the constant term, the singular term, the type — pole or branch point — each stated, each machine-checkable against the object itself. Third, state the transfer honestly: the passage from "the function looks like this near its break" to "the shelves grow like that" is a theorem with hypotheses, and a read that uses it must say so and carry its error term — exact extraction labeled exact, asymptotic reads labeled asymptotic, borrowed estimates named as borrowed. Small-size checks confirm and detect; the shape at the break is the reason; neither is the other.

The three-key diagnosis. Claim type: an asymptotic law, a growth constant, or an exact price for a sum over integers — the questions algebra answers only when it can, and cannot even state otherwise. Problem shape: the question is already on shelves (P73's filing system), or is a sum over integers, or asks "how fast" of a family — shapes that define a function of one variable whose breaks mean something. Tools at hand: the location, type, and local shape of the singularities, plus continuation when it exists — with the transfer theorem, when it is needed, named and owed.

How you could have found this move: every time P73's algebra delivers the package but not the growth — every time the exact shelves exist and the law of the shelves does not — the obstruction is stored at a break. The discovery route is the encounter's: ask what the series' radius is protecting, factor until the breaks confess, and read the census back from the geometry. The next three exemplars run exactly that loop, on the three kinds of integer questions the plane answers best.

4 · Three exemplars

Exemplar 1 — the drift priced (Catalan growth, read from the tear). P73's exemplar 3 extracted the exact form \(c_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\) for the ballot paths, and its pattern box demanded honest growth claims. Here is the claim, and here is what it costs. Divide the exact count by its leading engine \(4^n\) and by \(n^{3/2}\): the drift table reads \[ \frac{c_n\, n^{3/2}}{4^n} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \approx 0.564190, \] and the machine watches it go: \(0.2500\) at \(n=1\), \(0.4375\) at \(n=4\), \(0.4937\) at \(n=8\), \(0.5269\) at \(n=16\), \(0.5340\) at \(n=20\) — approaching slowly, the gap shrinking like \(1/n\) (Fig P27.2). Two facts in that table are beyond algebra. First the constant: no manipulation of binomial coefficients produces \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\); \(\pi\) enters integer counting exactly here. Second the correction: the exponent \(-3/2\) on \(n\), the reason the drift crawls instead of landing. Both are read from the break. P73's package was \(C(z) = \frac{1-\sqrt{1-4z}}{2z}\), and the break is the tear under the square root: at \(z = \frac{1}{4}\) the function has a branch point — not a pole, a tear. Near it, one line of algebra reorganizes the object into its local shape: writing \(t = 1-4z\), \[ C \;=\; \frac{1-\sqrt{t}}{2z} \;=\; \frac{2(1-\sqrt{t})}{1-t} \;=\; \frac{2}{1+\sqrt{t}} \;=\; 2 \;-\; 2\sqrt{1-4z} \;+\; 2(1-4z) \;-\; \cdots \] The shelves of \(2\) and of \(2(1-4z)\) are constants and one shelf — they cannot price a family. The singular term is everything: the shelves of \((1-z)^{1/2}\) are exactly \(-\binom{2n}{n}\big/\big((2n-1)4^n\big)\) — an algebraic fact, machine-checkable at every \(n\) — and so the singular term files \[ [z^n]\,\big(-2\sqrt{1-4z}\big) \;=\; \frac{2\binom{2n}{n}}{2n-1} \;\sim\; \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi}\, n^{3/2}}, \] the last step being the one place algebra cannot follow: the central binomial's Stirling law \(\binom{2n}{n} \sim \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\), itself a fact of the same planar kind (the \(\Gamma\)-function's door, deeper than this page). The transfer — why the singular term alone prices the family — is the transfer theorem, named here, used here, owed properly in the lineage: what this page states is the shape at the break, the exact algebraic shelves of the singular term, and the machine's confirmation that the drift table obeys \(c_n \sim \frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi}\, n^{3/2}}\), with the gap shrinking like \(1/n\) — at \(n=200\) the raw drift still reads \(0.5610\), the correction plainly alive, while the one-term-corrected read of the second exercise lands all five decimals there — and no mismatch anywhere checked. Moves used: P73 filed the package and extracted the exact shelves; P02's census habit became the drift table itself; the obligations of Box P27.A police all three steps.

Exemplar 2 — the same machine, a gentler break (Fibonacci, and why algebra sufficed). P73's encounter filed the tilings to \(F(z) = \frac{1}{1-z-z^2}\) and priced them by partial fractions; here is the planar reread, and the contrast that teaches more than either half alone. The denominator factors over its roots: \(1-z-z^2 = (1-\varphi z)(1-\psi z)\), so the object has exactly two breaks — poles, both of them, no tears — at \(z = \frac{1}{\varphi} \approx 0.618\) on the line and \(z = -\varphi \approx -1.618\) off the useful neighborhood. P73's Fig P73.2 drew the circle through the near break; the obligations now name the rest. The nearest break is a pole, and a pole files pure geometric shelves: \([z^n] \frac{c}{1-\varphi z} = c\,\varphi^n\), no correction, no drift, no \(\pi\). The far pole files the tiny alternating correction \(\frac{-1}{\sqrt{5}}\psi^n\), and \(\lvert\psi\rvert = \frac{1}{\varphi} < 1\) means it decays — the machine watches it die: the exact shelf \(f_n\) sits within \(0.0403\) of \(\frac{\varphi^{n+1}}{\sqrt{5}}\) at \(n=4\), within \(0.00225\) at \(n=10\), within \(0.000203\) at \(n=15\), within \(0.000018\) at \(n=20\) — every \(f_n\) the nearest integer to \(\frac{\varphi^{n+1}}{\sqrt{5}}\), which is why partial fractions could finish the job alone. This is the diagnostic contrast of the whole page: a pole's shelves are a geometric — algebra reads them exactly, because partial fractions are the local shape at the break, executed; a branch point's shelves carry a correction \(n^{-3/2}\), and algebra has no shelf operation that produces it. Exemplar 1's drift crawls precisely because its break tears; this exemplar lands instantly because its break only spikes. Growth questions do not ask whether the package will yield, they ask what kind of break the package has. Moves used: P73 owned the filing and the exact extraction; P16's wardrobe habit reads the two exemplars as one machine in two outfits.

Exemplar 3 — an exact price by factoring (Basel, over the zeros of sine). The third question is not about growth at all — the plane answers exact integer-adjacent questions too, and this one is the book's oldest. Sum the reciprocals of the squares: \(\sum_{n \geq 1} \frac{1}{n^2} = 1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{9} + \cdots\). The partial sums creep: \(1.549768\) at ten terms, \(1.634984\) at a hundred, \(1.643935\) at a thousand — converging, but to what? No shelf algebra in this book can close it. The object that closes it is P24's sine — \(\sin(\pi z)\), a function that vanishes at exactly the integers, one simple zero per integer, and nowhere else. Factor the sine over its zeros the way a polynomial factors over its zeros — this is the labeled step, and the label is honest: an infinite product over zeros is a theorem (the Weierstrass factorization), not an analogy, and this page quotes it rather than proving it: \[ \frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z} \;=\; \prod_{n \geq 1} \left(1 - \frac{z^2}{n^2}\right). \] Both sides are entire — analytic at every point of the plane, which is what makes the factoring lawful — and both sides have the same zeros. Now the extraction is a shelf comparison, P73's own machine: the left side's shelves are elementary, \[ \frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{\pi^2}{6}z^2 \;+\; \frac{\pi^4}{120}z^4 \;-\; \cdots; \] the right side's \(z^2\)-shelf collects, from each factor, exactly \(-\frac{1}{n^2}\). Comparing: \[ \sum_{n \geq 1} \frac{1}{n^2} \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{6} \;\approx\; 1.644934. \] The creeping partial sums now have their limit, exact — and the price of an integer sum was stored, all along, in the zeros of a wave. The \(z^4\)-shelf pushes one honest step further (the last exercise runs it), and the machine confirms every number: partial sums, the limit, and the product's own shelves against the sine's at sample points. This is the blurb's sentence fulfilled: factoring reveals structure — over the integers for the plane, over the zeros for the sine, and the integer question falls out of the match. Moves used: P24 owned the sine and its zeros; P16 owned the wardrobe change from sum to shelf; the shelf comparison is P73's extraction, one floor up.

5 · Three exhibits

Exhibit A — the wall is real, and subtler than "no formula". It is tempting to say the branch point simply forbids extraction — tempting and wrong, and the honesty is worth the detour. The singular term's shelves were exact: \([z^n](-2\sqrt{1-4z}) = \frac{2\binom{2n}{n}}{2n-1}\), an algebraic identity machine-checked at every size this page runs. The wall is not at the extraction; it is at the law of the extracted shelves. What algebra cannot do is state that \(\binom{2n}{n}\) grows like \(\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\) — that fact is itself planar, the central binomial's own break-structure compressed into Stirling's law, and the \(\pi\) in the drift table arrives exactly there, from the geometry of the \(\Gamma\)-function's break, a door below this page. So the division of labor is precise, and worth saying in one sentence: algebra delivers exact shelves; the plane prices their growth. A page that forgets the first half asserts constants it cannot compute; a page that forgets the second half quotes growth it cannot justify — both defects are real, both are policed by Box P27.A, and this page commits neither.

Exhibit B — the wall of breaks (a natural boundary). Count partitions of \(n\) into powers of \(2\) — the same binary family P73's sixth exercise telescoped from the distinct side. Here the parts may repeat: the package is \[ B(z) \;=\; \prod_{k \geq 0} \frac{1}{1-z^{2^k}}, \] and the census begins \(1,\; 1,\; 2,\; 2,\; 4,\; 4,\; 6,\; 6,\; 10,\; 10,\; 14,\; 14,\; 20\) for \(n = 0\) through \(12\) — every odd shelf copying the even shelf before it, because a partition of an odd number must contain a \(1\). Now look at the breaks: each factor \(\frac{1}{1-z^{2^k}}\) breaks at every \(2^k\)-th root of unity, and the union over \(k\) — every root of unity of dyadic order — is dense on the unit circle: between any two points of \\(\lvert z\rvert = 1\) there is another break, and another, forever. There is no continuation through that wall anywhere; the disk is all the object there is; the read that priced Catalan — continue past the break, inspect the local shape — stops cold, because there is no "past". The family still has asymptotics (Mahler's, and they are strange — oscillating terms among the leading ones), but they are earned by entirely different machinery, quoted here and owed elsewhere. The moral is the boundary of the pattern honestly drawn: the move reads breaks it can reach, and the plane generously builds objects whose breaks seal every door.

Exhibit C — the shape, never the count. The drift law prices \(c_{100} \approx 0.5642 \cdot \frac{4^{100}}{100^{3/2}}\) — call it \(9 \times 10^{56}\) — and would be humiliated by the exact question: \(c_{100}\) is a specific integer, \(c_{10}\) is the specific \(16796\), and no growth law ever returns one of them. This is P73's price sentence, sharpened by its own subject: the analytic read prices the package but forgets the count. Mitigation: run both machines and know which one each sentence came from — the exact shelves from the algebra, the growth from the break, the obligations of Box P27.A separating them at every step. Price: the growth law is the answer to a different question, and pretending otherwise sells a rate as a count. Default: exact extraction for the number, the break's shape for the law, and the gap between them stated whenever both appear.

Honest scope. This page reads one-variable functions of one planar variable, with three break-types on its desk: poles, branch points, natural boundaries — and the trichotomy is the page's spine (pure geometric; geometric times a power of \(n\); no read at all). Beyond the desk, honestly named and owed: the transfer theorem that licenses every "the singular term prices the family" step (the lineage's Flajolet–Odlyzko); the Weierstrass factorization behind the sine product; the \(\Gamma\)-function behind Stirling's law and the \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\); the proofs of uniqueness of continuation, quoted in the third on-ramp. The circle method — where these reads, multiplied by many breaks at once, grow into the modern law of Waring-type problems — is a later page's whole subject, named as a door and not assumed. And the standing label of the whole book applies with special force here: machine checks at any finite run are detection, never proof; the shapes at the breaks are the reasons, and the two are policed apart on every page of this tier.

6 · The doors this opens

Three doors leave this page, and each is the same planar habit pointed at a different quarry.

The waves door. Exemplar 3 factored the sine over its zeros — and the sine is a wave, the standing wave of P24's organ pipe, whose every musical fact is a fact about the placement of its zeros and poles. The factorization \( \frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z} = \prod (1 - z^2/n^2) \) is Fourier analysis in embryo: what P24 did by adding waves on the line, the plane does by multiplying factors over the zeros, and the two bookkeep the same harmonic content. The shelf comparison that priced Basel is, in P24's dialect, the statement that the wave's energy ledger and its zero ledger agree. Mitigation: the product over zeros is lawful for entire functions of controlled growth — sines, cosines, and their kin — and unlawful for arbitrary lists of zeros; the growth control is the suppressed hypothesis and must be named. Price: the wave picture prices shelves by zeros but stays silent about convergence rates on the line; each bookkeeping pays for its own questions. Default: sums of reciprocal powers → factor the entire function; orthogonality and approximation questions → back to P24's waves.

The localization door. This page's blurb says factoring reveals structure, and Exemplar 3 factored a function; the integers themselves factor the other way, and the door on that side belongs to P30. Euler's identity is the hinge: \[ \sum_{n \geq 1} \frac{1}{n^s} \;=\; \prod_{p \text{ prime}} \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}, \] the sum over all integers equal, shelf by shelf, to a product over the primes — unique factorization, worn as a function. The left side is a shelf family this page can analyze; the right side is P30's habit of working one prime at a time; and the prime-counting questions that come through this door — how the zeros of a zeta-like object price the primes — are the deepest integer problems the plane has ever answered. Nothing past the hinge is assumed here; the door is named, its machinery is P30's and its descendants', and this page supplies only the hinge. Mitigation: the identity is exact for real \(s > 1\) and analytic continuation past that line is a theorem with a proof, not a mood; unlabeled continuation claims are the asymptotic door's original sin committed again. Price: the prime product converges beautifully and extracts terribly — the same wall as P73's partition product, one floor down. Default: factor the integers when the question is multiplicative; hang the shelves when it is additive; the zeta hinge when it is both.

The clothesline door. Every object this page read was first hung where P73 hung it: the Catalan package, the Fibonacci package, the binary product, even Basel's sum (a shelf of a factored wave). The two pages are one machine with two gears — P73's gear is algebra and answers exactly; this page's gear is geometry and answers asymptotically and, for entire functions, exactly. The next gear down the canon is already visible from here: reading many breaks at once, arcs of them, major and minor (P38, planned); mapping the plane's geometry to the problem's geometry, so shapes do the algebra (conformal mapping, P28, planned); and trading the smooth for the piecewise-linear, where the breaks are the whole structure (P40, tropicalize, planned). None of those pages is assumed here; each will pick up exactly the habit this page installed — locate the breaks, name their types, read the integers off them. Mitigation: the habits transfer, the theorems do not — each later page owes its own transfer proof, and citing this page is not one. Price: every gear past the first adds hypotheses; the disk is where the series lives, and the rest of the plane is leased, never owned. Default: file first (P73), locate the breaks (this page), and only then choose the later gear — a page that reaches for arcs before it can price one break is borrowing three doors at once.

7 · Lineage

The plane entered integer mathematics through the same two eighteenth-century hands that built P73's shelf algebra. Leonhard Euler resolved the Basel problem in 1735 — the exact price \(\pi^2/6\) for the reciprocals of the squares — by treating the sine as a polynomial over its zeros, and the factorization that this page's third exemplar runs is Euler's own argument, publication-finished in his 1748 Introductio, the same book whose partition chapter P73's lineage already credited; the function \(\Gamma\) that powers the constants of Exhibit A is Euler's too, from the same decade's work on interpolating the factorials. The nineteenth century made the plane lawful: the theorems that continuation is unique, and that a function's values in one patch govern it everywhere, stand to Cauchy and Riemann's credit, and Gaston Darboux showed in the 1870s how a function's behavior near its singularities on the circle of convergence prices its coefficients' growth — Darboux's method is this page's exemplars, run a century early. The twentieth century industrialized it: G. H. Hardy and S. Ramanujan's 1918 asymptotic for the partition numbers read P73's partition product through its nearest singularity to a formula accurate enough to stun, and Hans Rademacher sharpened the same read into an exact convergent series for \(p(n)\) in 1937 — the asymptotic door, walked all the way through. Philippe Flajolet and Andrew Odlyzko's transfer theorems (1990) made the passage from local shape to coefficient law a system, and Philippe Flajolet and Robert Sedgewick's Analytic Combinatorics (2009) — the same book P73's lineage cites — is the modern canon of the whole move. The dates in this interlude are load-free and were checked fresh for this page; the lineage is a story, and the mathematics carries itself.

8 · Exercises

●○○ ●●○ ●●● ◆ ◐ — five exercises, from the first break to the sealed wall.

Exercise 1 · Invisible breaks, found and priced ●○○
(a) Run the shelf census of \(\frac{1}{1+2z^2}\) through \(n=7\), by long division or by the geometric read, and state the pattern. (b) Factor the denominator and name the two breaks, with their distances from the origin. (c) Predict from the breaks alone — before dividing — what the shelves do: growth or decay per step, and the oscillation's period; then confirm against (a).

Hint

(b) The breaks of \(\frac{1}{1+z^2}\) were at \(\pm i\); what does the coefficient \(2\) move? (c) A distance prices the rate; an angle prices the oscillation — measure both for your breaks.

Worked answer

(a) The shelves read \(1,\, 0,\, -2,\, 0,\, 4,\, 0,\, -8,\, 0\): the geometric \(1 - 2z^2 + 4z^4 - 8z^6 + \cdots\) stamped on every second shelf. (b) \(1 + 2z^2 = 0\) at \(z^2 = -\frac{1}{2}\), so the breaks are \(z = \pm\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\), each at distance \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071\). (c) Distance \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\) means growth per step of \(\sqrt{2}\) — the even shelves double in amplitude every two steps, which is exactly \(1, -2, 4, -8\): factor \(\sqrt{2}\) per step on the alternating even subsequence, and the odd shelves are zero because the angle \(\pi/2\) forces period-four oscillation, vanishing on alternate steps. The prediction is the census, before a single division — and any first mismatch would have falsified the breaks' location, which is the whole point.

Exercise 2 · The drift, accelerated ●●○
Exemplar 1's drift table approaches \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\) with a gap shrinking like \(1/n\). (a) Confirm the \(1/n\) law by computing \(n \times\) the relative gap at \(n = 8,\, 12,\, 20\) from the table's own numbers. (b) A pure \(1/n\) correction means the drift should follow \(C_n\big(1 + \frac{a}{n}\big) \to \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\) for some constant \(a\); estimate \(a\) from your part-(a) products. (c) The known refinement — quoted, not derived here — is \(a = \frac{9}{8}\); verify that the accelerated value at \(n = 20\) lands within \(0.0002\) of \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\), and say precisely what this check does and does not prove.

Hint

(a) The relative gap is \(\frac{\text{limit} - \text{drift}}{\text{drift}}\). (b) If the gap is \(\frac{a'}{n}\) in relative terms, then \(a' = n \times\) relative gap — compute it at all three sizes and watch it settle.

Worked answer

(a) From the machine-checked table: drift \(0.4937\) at \(n=8\), \(0.5154\) at \(n=12\), \(0.5340\) at \(n=20\), against the limit \(0.564190\): the relative gaps are \(0.1427,\; 0.0947,\; 0.0566\), and the products \(n \times\) gap read \(1.1416,\; 1.1361,\; 1.1316\) — settling, which is the \(1/n\) law confirmed. (b) The products converge near \(1.13\), and the correction constant is \(a \approx \frac{9}{8} = 1.125\) (the residual drift toward \(1.125\) is the \(1/n^2\) term's work, visible but small). (c) Accelerated: \(0.5340 \times (1 + \frac{9}{8 \times 20}) = 0.5340 \times 1.05625 = 0.56401\), within \(0.00018\) of \(0.56419\) — a 170-fold improvement on the raw gap \(0.0302\), from one correction term. The check proves the refinement fits at the sizes run; it detects, it does not derive — the constant \(9/8\) belongs to the full singular expansion, and its derivation is the transfer theorem's business, owed and named, not run here.

Exercise 3 · Basel, pushed one shelf further ●●●
(a) Re-derive \(\sum \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}\) from the \(z^2\)-shelf comparison of the sine product — state both sides' \(z^2\)-shelves explicitly. (b) Push to the \(z^4\)-shelf: the product's \(z^4\)-shelf is \(\sum_{m < n} \frac{1}{m^2 n^2}\), which is \(\frac{1}{2}\big(\big(\sum \frac{1}{n^2}\big)^2 - \sum \frac{1}{n^4}\big)\); equate it with the sine's \(\frac{\pi^4}{120}\) and solve for \(\sum \frac{1}{n^4}\). (c) Machine-check both limits against partial sums to a thousand terms, and state exactly what the shelf comparison proved and what the factorization step silently used.

Hint

(b) You now know \(\sum \frac{1}{n^2}\) — substitute it, keep the unknown \(\sum \frac{1}{n^4}\) alone on one side. (c) \(\frac{\pi^4}{120} \approx 0.811742\); \(\frac{\pi^4}{90} \approx 1.082323\). What does "entire, with controlled growth" buy in the factorization?

Worked answer

(a) Left: \(\frac{\sin(\pi z)}{\pi z} = 1 - \frac{\pi^2}{6}z^2 + \frac{\pi^4}{120}z^4 - \cdots\), so its \(z^2\)-shelf is \(-\frac{\pi^2}{6}\). Right: each factor contributes \(-\frac{1}{n^2}\) to the \(z^2\)-shelf, so the product's \(z^2\)-shelf is \(-\sum \frac{1}{n^2}\). Equating: \(\sum \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} \approx 1.644934\). (b) Substituting the known \(\frac{\pi^2}{6}\): \(\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{\pi^4}{36} - \sum \frac{1}{n^4}\big) = \frac{\pi^4}{120}\), so \(\sum \frac{1}{n^4} = \frac{\pi^4}{36} - \frac{\pi^4}{60} = \pi^4\big(\frac{5}{180} - \frac{3}{180}\big) = \frac{\pi^4}{90} \approx 1.082323\). (c) The partial sums: \(\sum_{n \leq 1000} \frac{1}{n^2} \approx 1.643935\) against \(1.644934\); \(\sum_{n \leq 1000} \frac{1}{n^4} \approx 1.082323\) against \(1.082323\) — the fourth-power sum lands on its limit at six decimals, the square sum still \(0.001\) short, exactly the difference \(1/n^2\)-decay and \(1/n^4\)-decay should show. The comparison itself is algebra and proves what it says, given the factorization; what was silently used is that the sine is entire of controlled growth, so that factoring over its zeros is lawful and no exponential factors are missing — the Weierstrass step, quoted on this page, proved nowhere in it.

Exercise 4 · Diagnose the growth, cold ◆
A colleague hands you the shelf family of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-9z}}\) — no derivation, no table, just the object. Diagnose cold: where are the breaks, what type is the nearest, what growth law should the shelves follow, and what exact algebraic form do they have? State your answer's three obligations as you discharge them, and give one machine check that would falsify your diagnosis fastest.

Worked answer

Obligation one, name the break: the square root tears at \(1 - 9z = 0\), a single branch point at \(z = \frac{1}{9}\), distance \(\frac{1}{9}\) — so the shelves grow at rate \(9\) per step, and there are no other breaks anywhere (the other branch of the root is a fiction of the algebra, not a second singularity of the chosen branch's disk). Obligation two, the local shape: this is a negative half-power, \((1-9z)^{-1/2}\) — the tear that amplifies rather than dampens — and a half-power prices a correction \(n^{-1/2}\), not the \(n^{-3/2}\) of the positive-half tear: the shelves should follow \(\frac{9^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\). Obligation three, the transfer, labeled: the \(n^{-1/2}\) law is the transfer theorem's half-power case, quoted. The exact form: \((1-z)^{-1/2} = \sum \binom{2n}{n}\frac{z^n}{4^n}\), so the shelves are \(\binom{2n}{n}\frac{9^n}{4^n}\) — exact, algebraic, machine-checkable at every size. Fastest falsification: the rate — the shelf at \(n=10\) is \(\binom{20}{10}\frac{9^{10}}{4^{10}} \approx 6.144 \times 10^8\), and the law reads \(6.221 \times 10^8\), ratio \(1.013\), approaching \(1\) like \(1/n\); a shelf growing at rate \(8\) or \(10\), or decaying, would falsify the break's location instantly. The check detects the diagnosis at the sizes run; the break's location and type are the reason, and the two are different obligations — as the box demands.

Exercise 5 · The sealed wall ◐
Explore the binary partitions of Exhibit B. (a) Verify the census \(1,\, 1,\, 2,\, 2,\, 4,\, 4,\, 6,\, 6,\, 10,\, 10,\, 14,\, 14,\, 20\) at \(n = 0\) through \(12\) by direct enumeration of small cases. (b) Prove the odd-copy law \(a(2n+1) = a(2n)\) by a bijection, and derive from the same reasoning the recurrence \(a(2n) = a(2n-1) + a(n)\); confirm both on the census. (c) Explain, from the product \(\prod_k \frac{1}{1-z^{2^k}}\), why the breaks are dense on the unit circle, and why this page's machinery therefore stops at the wall. (d) Explore honestly: what would a growth law for \(a(n)\) even look like, given the odd-copy law, and what kind of machinery — named or unnamed on this page — could deliver it?

Hint

(b) An odd number's partition must contain a \(1\) — why? And a partition of an even number with no \(1\)-parts has all parts even — divide them. (c) Which points of the unit circle are \(2^k\)-th roots of unity for some \(k\), and how many of those are there as \(k\) grows?

Worked answer

(a) Direct enumeration, e.g. \(n=8\): \(8\); \(6+2\); \(4+4\); \(4+2+2\); \(4+2+1+1\); \(2+2+2+2\); \(2+2+2+1+1\); \(2+2+1+1+1+1\); \(2+1+\cdots+1\); \(1+\cdots+1\) — ten, and the rest of the census checks shelf by shelf against the enumeration. (b) Odd-copy law: all parts but the \(1\)-parts are even, so a partition of an odd number contains a \(1\); remove one \(1\) and you have a partition of the even number below — and adding a \(1\) inverts the map, a bijection, so \(a(2n+1) = a(2n)\). Even recurrence: a partition of \(2n\) either contains a \(1\)-part or does not; removing one \(1\) from the first kind bijects them with partitions of \(2n-1\), and halving every part of the second kind bijects them with partitions of \(n\) into powers of \(2\) — so \(a(2n) = a(2n-1) + a(n)\). The census confirms: \(a(8) = a(7) + a(4) = 6 + 4 = 10\), \(a(12) = a(11) + a(6) = 14 + 6 = 20\). (c) The factor \(\frac{1}{1-z^{2^k}}\) breaks at every \(2^k\)-th root of unity, and those points — angles \(\frac{2\pi m}{2^k}\), dyadic fractions of a turn — include, for large \(k\), points within any chosen distance of any point of the circle at all: between any two breaks there are more breaks, forever. Continuation requires an open patch free of singularities to walk through, and the circle has none — the wall seals every door, and the Catalan-style read (continue, inspect, transfer) cannot even begin. (d) The odd-copy law says the family grows by copying: \(a(n)\) and \(a(n+1)\) share their size, so no smooth \(C \cdot \rho^n \cdot n^{\alpha}\) can be exactly right — the honest law must itself carry the copying (Mahler's asymptotics do, with periodic oscillation built in — named in Exhibit B, proved nowhere on this page, and earning the "strange" honestly). The machinery that delivers it works on the functional equation the recurrence files, not on singularities through a wall — a genuinely different door, and the exercise's honest endpoint is its name, not its proof.
