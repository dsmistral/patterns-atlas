The Patterns Atlas · P25 · the eighth move of tier 2

Check every case by machine

A friend places eight queens on a chessboard so that no two attack — a minute of pencil work — and then asks the real question: in how many ways can it be done? The pencil that found one arrangement has no honest route to the count: the raw space holds \(8^8 = 16{,}777{,}216\) placements, and even the disciplined version — one queen per row, one per column — holds \(8! = 40{,}320\) candidates to check by hand, eleven hours at one per second. The machine sweeps the disciplined space with pruning that kills dead branches the moment they violate a rule, examines only \(15{,}720\) candidate placements, and prints 92 — every case visited, none missed, none counted twice. The census settles more than patience ever could: the smallest boards are a shock (two queens and three queens admit zero arrangements — non-existence, certified with the same authority as the count), the answer 92 is an integer fact, final forever, and the board's own symmetries split the 92 into twelve classes — eleven families of eight and one family of four, the family of the half-turn-symmetric solution the census finds unprompted. But the machine inherits the honesty rules of the pencil, and this page prices them in full: a census of a finite question — how many eight-queen arrangements? — is a proof; a census of an infinite one — is every even number a sum of two primes? — is a detector, and the two never wear each other's clothes. Three runs in public: the narcissistic census (which three-digit numbers equal the sum of the cubes of their digits? exactly 153, 370, 371 and 407 — a closed census, because \(\textit{three-digit}\) pins the question finite, and a size cap closes the whole family: no such number can have five or more digits), the queens census with its symmetry audit, and the domino-tiling census whose table — 1, 5, 11, 36, 95, 281, 781, 2245 — hands its pattern to the reader: a four-term recurrence the machine can check to any depth and only a proof can close. The prices, paid in full: the space must match the question or the census answers a different one in perfect honesty; finite does not mean feasible (Euler's 36 officers — \(36!\) raw arrangements — took Tarry years of hand enumeration, 1900–01, and some spaces still outrun every machine on earth); and \(\textit{the computer checked it}\) is itself a claim about code, which wants a certificate of its own. Prerequisites: P03 (the prosecutor's questions — this page hands the pencil to a machine, keeping every honesty rule). Next: the certificate the census cannot print — P66 · Prove by induction.

New word: census. A census is a walk through a question's cases that visits every case and counts each exactly once. It differs from a search: a search hopes to find something and may stop when it does; a census promises to miss nothing, and its answer — a count, an existence, a non-existence, a longest or shortest — is a fact about the whole space, not about the part that looked promising. The machine does not make a census honest; visiting everything, once, does. New word: pruning. Pruning is cutting a branch of the search the moment it violates a rule already broken: the placement with two queens in one column is dead, and so is every extension of it. Pruning is P03's instinct — don't ask the dead questions — executed at machine speed; done honestly, it changes how many cases the machine must try, but never which answers exist. New word: certificate. A certificate is a record that lets a skeptic re-check an answer without re-running the work or trusting the answerer. For a census the cheapest certificate is a second, independent enumerator — different code, a different route through the same space — that returns the same answer; when two engines that share nothing agree, the answer has a witness in both, and the reader who writes one has earned the right to quote it.

1 · Before we begin

The reader arrives owning two pencils and one standing promise. From P02: the conjecture factory — compute small cases honestly, read the table, conjecture out loud, and remember that Moser's circle table doubled five times and then produced 31, one step past the comfortable drawing zone. From P03: the prosecutor — before believing a claim, attack it with the boundary cases, the degenerate configurations, the symmetric ones, the dangerous values, the extremes, and the first case nobody has checked; and P03's scope note, kept word for word: stress-testing certifies nothing. Its verdict is not-yet-dead, never true. And the promise, made at the end of P03 and again at the end of P24: when the cases are too many for the pencil, this page — the machine — takes over, with the same honesty rules: a finite check is a detector, not a proof, unless the cases were all of them.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Both tools are honest but budget-limited in the same way: they run on attention. P02's tables reached the sixth row before the drawing gave out; P03's malice reaches the cases a person can check before giving out — and the questions mathematics actually asks die far past that line. How many ways can eight mutually peaceful queens be arranged? P02's method answers for two queens, maybe three, and then the hand stops. Is every even number a sum of two primes? P03's method — check the boundary, check the extremes — checks a handful and stops, honestly, at not yet dead. The cases of both questions are finite, listable, mechanical to check — and counted in the millions. The good news, and the whole of this page: the same questions, handed to a machine that never tires, become censuses — complete walks through the space — and the honesty rules survive the handoff untouched. What changes is the budget. What must not change is the boundary between a closed count (a proof) and a sampled one (a detector) — and this page polices that boundary harder than any page before it, because the machine makes overreaching easy.

The runner is the eight-queens puzzle of §2, met twice: once as the pencil's defeat, then as the census's easiest public run. Between the two meetings, §3 names the move and the machine that runs it.

2 · Eight pieces, one count the pencil cannot close

A friend poses the old puzzle: place eight queens on a chessboard — the ordinary \(8 \times 8\) one — so that no two attack each other. No two share a row, a column, or a diagonal. The pencil is good at this: a minute or two of honest trying finds, say, queens at \((1,1)\), \((2,5)\), \((3,8)\), \((4,6)\), \((5,3)\), \((6,7)\), \((7,2)\), \((8,4)\) — row first, column second, one queen per row. Check it: every row is used once by construction; the columns \(1, 5, 8, 6, 3, 7, 2, 4\) are all distinct; and the diagonals — the pairs to verify, and the reader should verify two or three by eye — never collide. One solution, in public, the way P02 likes: the artifact before any analysis.

Now the friend asks the real question, and the puzzle changes species: in how many ways can it be done? Counting is not finding. The pencil that located one arrangement has no honest route to the total, and the arithmetic of the space says why. Every queen has 64 squares available: the raw space of placements is \(64^8\), and even the lazy version — each queen gets her own row, \(8^8 = 16{,}777{,}216\) placements — is a number no pencil closes. The disciplined version pins one queen per row and one per column, which is exactly a choice of which column each row's queen takes, all distinct: \(8! = 40{,}320\) candidates. At one candidate per second — generous, for hand work that must check seven diagonal pairs each — that is eleven hours of unbroken perfect attention, at the end of which the counter has earned a number and no way to check it. P02 stopped at the sixth Moser row for exactly this reason; the counting question stops at row eight.

The machine does not have the attention problem. Sweep the disciplined space — all \(40{,}320\) row-to-column assignments — checking each for diagonal peace, and print the survivors: 92. Better: prune the sweep the way the hand would pray to. Build the placement row by row, and abandon a branch the instant two queens attack: a branch that is already broken cannot heal below. The pruned sweep examines only \(15{,}720\) candidate placements on its way to the same 92 — the \(40{,}320\) shrunk by the deaths — and the pruning changed the effort and not the answer, because every abandoned branch was dead already.

But the census's real lesson is not speed. It is that the same sweep answers questions the pencil could not even pose honestly. Run it for the small boards:

board \(n \times n\)12345678
peaceful arrangements10021044092

Read the zeros twice, because they are the table's spine. The two-queen and three-queen boards — the smallest boards, the ones a beginner would bet on — admit no arrangement at all. The census settles non-existence with exactly the same authority as the count: it visited every case, and every case failed. This is P03's counterexample hunt at industrial scale — the black swan hunt where the machine checks the whole lake — and the small case turns out to be the treacherous one, exactly as P03's boundary cases always warned. (The 4-queen board, with its two arrangements, is small enough to close by hand in a few minutes; the reader should try, once, to feel the difference between closing a case and trusting a pattern.)

And then the friend asks the question that sets up this whole page: does every board from some point on have solutions? The table answers for each board it visited — 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, each a closed census, each a proof — and answers nothing about the boards it did not visit. The number 92 is final; the pattern "every \(n \ge 4\) works" is a conjecture wearing a table, and no depth of table makes it more. Run the machine on: \(n = 9\) gives 352, \(n = 10\) gives 724, \(n = 11\) gives 2,680, \(n = 12\) gives \(14{,}200\) — four more closed counts, and still the infinite question is untouched, because the machine reads cases, never the pattern across them. The census answers each finite question totally, and the infinite question not at all; sorting which question a census has actually closed is the discipline this page exists to teach, and §3 names it.

3 · Name the move

P25 · Check every case by machine — when the question's cases are finite and listable, do not sample and do not argue: enumerate every case, check each one, and read the answer off the completed census

When a question is finite in its cases — the possibilities can be listed, completely, before anything is run — but beyond the pencil's budget — too many to check by hand, or too error-prone to trust a hand's completeness — do not imitate a proof with a sample. Instead: pin the space — say exactly which cases constitute the question, because the space is part of the claim; enumerate without missing and without counting twice — the census's two virtues, and both are obligations, not manners; prune with recorded assumptions — abandon dead branches early, and record that pruning changes effort, never answers; audit with a second independent engine — a different route through the same space that must return the same answer; and print the boundary sentence — a closed count is a proof, a sample of an infinite claim is a detector, and every answer carries its status. What the move buys: total authority over finite questions — counts, existences, non-existences, extremes — at a budget the pencil can never reach, with honesty rules that survive the speed.

In words, the move has three working parts, and the page keeps saying which part is doing the work. First, the space: "the three-digit numbers" is a finite space; "the even numbers" is not, and a census of the first thousand evens is a detector wearing a bigger detector's clothes. Pinning the space is P01's precision at its most literal — the pin decides the truth status. Second, the sweep: an enumerator is a small, boring piece of code that must be right in a boring way — every case, once. The boring way is checkable: prune only on constraints the question already imposes, and keep the prune honest by re-running the census a second, independent way. Third, the verdict: the census returns facts — 92 arrangements, zero on the 3-board — and facts about whole closed spaces are proofs; the census also returns patterns, and patterns are conjectures, whatever their table's depth. The move's discipline is keeping the two outputs apart, and the §5 exhibits are the three standard ways the keeping fails.

Box P25.A · The census machine — five obligations, in order

Obligation 1 — pin the space. The space is part of the claim. "Peaceful arrangements of eight queens, one per row and per column" pins \(8!\); "three-digit numbers equal to the sum of the cubes of their digits" pins 900; "every even number" pins nothing finite at all. Before any machine runs, the sentence that pins the space goes on paper — §5's second exhibit is the standard disaster of skipping it.

Obligation 2 — visit every case, count each once. Completeness and disjointness are the census's two virtues, and both take work. The disciplined queens sweep is complete because every peaceful arrangement has one queen per row and column — so it appears exactly once among the \(8!\) column-assignments — and disjoint because each assignment is listed once. Both virtues fail in instructive ways: an enumerator that skips the first row's low columns misses cases; an enumerator of ordered digit pairs \((a, b)\) in a sum-of-cubes question counts every two-cube sum twice.

Obligation 3 — prune, and record what pruning assumed. A branch that already violates a rule cannot contain a solution, because solutions violate no rules — so abandoning it changes the effort and never the answer. Measured, on the queens: the raw space \(8^8 = 16{,}777{,}216\), disciplined to \(8! = 40{,}320\) by obligations 1–2, and swept by the pruned machine examining \(15{,}720\) candidate placements — three budgets, one answer. The figure records the cascade at every size:

Obligation 4 — audit with a second engine. A census's output is one number, and one number has no internal structure to inspect — the honest check is a different enumerator sharing no code: the queens count returned 92 by branch-pruned sweep and by plain permutation of the \(8!\) assignments; the domino counts of Example 3 come from two different tilings enumerators; the narcissistic census runs by numbers and by digits. Agreement of independent engines is the working certificate of this page, and its limits are named in §5's third exhibit.

Obligation 5 — print the boundary sentence. Every census ends with one of two sentences, and the sentence is part of the mathematics: "the space was all of the question — the count is a proof", or "the question outruns the space — the count is a detector". No census is strong evidence, or weak evidence; it is one of the two, decided by the pin of Obligation 1 and nothing else.

Box P25.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Testing, invisibly: every unit test that runs a function over a small range of inputs and compares against known outputs is a micro-census — the space pinned by the test list, the answer a count of mismatches, the boundary sentence "the code is right everywhere the test looked," which is a detector and not a proof of correctness. Puzzles, industrially: the census with symmetry reduction settled the count of completed Sudoku grids, and the game-solver's world — solitaire, Minesweeper, chess endgames — runs on complete case analyses whose tables are exactly this page's tables at scale. Chemistry, structurally: counting the isomers of a formula — every distinct arrangement of the same atoms — is a census through a space of bond assignments, pruned by valence rules; the discipline is Obligation 2's, missing none and counting none twice. And the four-color theorem, this page's biggest public census, is §7's story. The move is the same in every clothes: pin the space, walk all of it, read the answer off the whole.

The three-key signature — the checklist that says the move applies, and the checklist that says it was honestly run. (1) The cases are finite and listable — by the question's own statement, or by a pin, or by a cap argument that shrinks an infinite family to a finite space, as Example 1's inequality did; an infinite question with no cap in sight is a detector forever and belongs to the proof pages. (2) The budget is known before the run — the space's size estimated (Obligation 1), the machine's ceiling checked against the counts that will accumulate (Exhibit C's wrap), the pruning's savings anticipated but not trusted: the budget is a forecast, and the run reports the real number. (3) The question wants a fact of the whole space — a count, an existence, a non-existence, a longest or shortest — not a method; a question about the pattern across cases is handed onward, as a conjecture, to P66. And one diagnostic read off the answer: a census's output is one number with no interior structure to inspect — an answer that cannot explain itself must be audited from the outside, by the second engine, and never by re-reading the number it gave.

4 · Three runs in public

The move runs three times on this page, in public, the way P02 ran its tables: every number printed is one the reader's own machine can re-derive, and §8's exercises hand over the keys. The three runs are chosen to fail differently if honesty lapses: the first is a census whose cap closes an infinite question, the second is a census whose audit uncovers structure the count alone would have missed, and the third is a census that finds a pattern it cannot close — and says so.

Example 1 · The narcissistic census — an infinite question, closed

Moves used: P01 (the pin made literal — the space is part of the claim), P03 (the boundary check, promoted to the cap inequality).

Ask it as an infinite question first: which numbers equal the sum of the cubes of their own digits? Every number is a candidate; the space, unpinned, is all of the integers; the question looks like Goldbach's kind — a detector forever. Now pin it, in two moves, and watch it collapse.

First move, the cap. A number with \(d\) digits is at least \(10^{d-1}\); the sum of the cubes of its digits is at most \(729d\) (every digit a 9, \(9^3 = 729\)). Once \(729d < 10^{d-1}\) the two can never meet — and the inequality starts at \(d = 5\) (\(3{,}645 < 10{,}000\)) and only widens: each step multiplies the left by less than 2 and the right by 10. So no candidate has five or more digits: the infinite question has collapsed, by an argument, into the finite one — census the numbers from 1 to 9,999 and you have answered the infinite question in full. This is the cap move's power: it is not a machine trick, it is P03's boundary examination — go where equality is possible — plus one inequality that seals the boundary. The space was not all of the question; the argument shrank the question to the space.

Second move, the census. Sweep 1 to 9,999, cubing digits, summing, comparing. The survivors:

numbercube sumcheck
1\(1^3\)1
153\(1 + 125 + 27\)153
370\(27 + 343 + 0\)370

Five numbers, and the list is final — not because the machine is impressive but because the two moves compose: the cap proved nothing outside 1–9,999 can qualify, the census visited everything inside, and the composition is a proof, with the boundary sentence "the space was all of the question" true for once. Note what the census did not need: luck, sampling, plausibility. It needed the pin (digits, cubes, positive integers), the cap (five digits seals it), the sweep (every case once — sweep by the numbers 1 to 9,999, or sweep by digit-tuples; two engines, both below), and the verdict. The reader who wants the audit Obligation 4 demands can enumerate digit-tuples instead of numbers — for each length \(\ell\), every \(\ell\)-tuple of digits with its cube-sum, asking which sums land back on a number with those very digits — an engine sharing no code with the number-sweep, returning the same five survivors. Two independent walks agree: the certificate this page promised in its second new word.

Example 2 · The queens census, audited — 92 answers, 12 puzzles

Moves used: P02 (the honest artifact, read twice), P08 (orbits under the board's own symmetry group).

Run 1 of §2's machine is the disciplined sweep of the \(8!\) assignments; run 2 is the pruned row-by-row build that examines \(15{,}720\) candidate placements. Both print 92, and the agreement is Obligation 4's certificate — two engines, no shared code, one answer. But the count, on its own, is the least interesting thing the census knows, and the audit shows why.

A chessboard carries its symmetries: quarter-turn, half-turn, three-quarter turn, and four reflections — eight moves that carry any peaceful arrangement to another peaceful arrangement, because "attacks" does not care how the board was rotated. So the census's own output raises a question the count cannot answer: are the 92 really 92 different arrangements, or fewer puzzles seen from several sides? Ask the machine to sort its own answers into families under the eight symmetries — a second pass over the first pass's output — and it reports: twelve families. Eleven families of eight (a generic arrangement has eight distinct views), and one family of four.

A family of four means some arrangement is sitting still under one of the eight moves — the family's eight views hit only four distinct arrangements, each view shared by two moves. Which move? Not a reflection (no arrangement of the 92 is mirror-symmetric, a fact the audit also certifies, for free). It is the half-turn: the arrangement with queens at columns \((3, 5, 2, 8, 1, 7, 4, 6)\) — rows one to eight, column per row — is carried to itself by the 180° rotation: position \((r, c)\) rotates to \((9 - r, 9 - c)\), and row 1's queen in column 3 lands on row 8, column 6 — which holds a queen; row 2's column 5 lands on row 7, column 4 — a queen; row 3's column 2 lands on row 6, column 7 — a queen; row 4's column 8 lands on row 5, column 1 — a queen. The board turned halfway around and the arrangement did not change. The census never looked for symmetry; it printed 92 answers, and the symmetry was sitting in the list, waiting for a question with the nerve to ask. This is the page's quiet counterpoint to §1: the machine, asked honestly, answers questions the asker did not know were there — the second output nobody budgets for.

The composition — census, then symmetry sort — is a second, subtler certificate. The count 92 and the family structure \(92 = 11 \times 8 + 1 \times 4\) are computed by different code paths; a broken enumerator that miscounted would have to break consistently through both. And the boundary sentence still closes the run: twelve families is a fact about the eight-by-eight board, final forever — while the pattern a table-watcher might whisper, "the number of families stays small as the board grows," is a conjecture with a machine's testimony and no proof at all.

Example 3 · The domino census — a table that hands you a conjecture

Moves used: P02 (the conjecture factory, fed at machine speed), P03 (what a confirmed table is worth — nothing, as proof).

Third run, and the census's third mood: this time it answers its own question completely and manufactures a different question it cannot touch. Tile a \(4 \times n\) rectangle with dominoes (the \(1 \times 2\) pieces of the ordinary set): in how many ways? The space pins easily — each tiling is a placement of \(2n\) dominoes — and the enumerator is the cap move's mirror: sweep the rectangle column by column, carrying a 4-bit mask of which cells the previous column's horizontal dominoes already filled, and fill the rest by every legal combination of vertical and horizontal placements. Every tiling is visited exactly once — the sweep's completeness is visible in its construction, which is what Obligation 2 looks like when it holds. The count:

width \(n\)12345678
tilings151136952817812,245

The census is closed for every width it ran, and the honest machine can run further: \(n = 9\) through 12 give 6,336, 18,061, 51,205, 145,601 — closed counts, each final forever, the boundary sentence printing "proof" every time. And now look at the table, because the table is doing P02's work at machine speed: differences of differences, a linear recurrence hiding in the counts. Conjecture, in public: \(a(n) = a(n-1) + 5a(n-2) + a(n-3) - a(n-4)\), seeded by 1, 5, 11, 36. Check: \(36 = 11 + 25 + 1 - 1\). Check: \(95 = 36 + 55 + 5 - 1\). Check it on the machine for a thousand widths, ten thousand — the recurrence predicts every census count, exactly, and the agreement is total.

And the agreement is evidence of nothing, which is this example's whole point. The census proved, width by width, that the count is the table's value; the recurrence, at any depth of machine confirmation, remains a conjecture — P02's Moser table doubling five times before producing 31 is the ancestor of exactly this trap, and the machine, far from rescuing the reader, industrializes it: confirmation now arrives at a million cases per second, and the million-and-first case is exactly as unknown as before. The recurrence is true — but the proof lives in the sweep's structure (the column masks form a 16-state machine whose transition matrix owns the recurrence as its characteristic polynomial), and "the matrix's characteristic polynomial equals the recurrence" is a theorem to be proved, not a table to be trusted. The census hands the pattern to the reader; only the proof closes it. This page counts; P66 is where the closing move lives.

5 · When it fails — the eternal detector, the mislabeled space, and the silent wrap

The machine never lies; the sentence around it does. The three exhibits below are the standard failure modes of a census — not one of them involves the machine misbehaving, and that is the point: every failure is an honesty failure of Obligations 1, 5, and 4, each number one a lab can recompute today.

Exhibit A · The eternal detector — the census read as a proof. Compute \(\pi(x)\), the number of primes up to \(x\), and \(\operatorname{Li}(x)\), the logarithmic integral, at every power of ten the machine can reach: at \(x = 10^8\) it reports \(\pi(10^8) = 5{,}761{,}455\) against \(\operatorname{Li}(x) \approx 5{,}762{,}209\) — \(\operatorname{Li}\) ahead by about 754, as it has been at every single point anyone could ever afford to check, and as every census anyone can run today still finds. The tempting sentence is "Li is always ahead." The honest sentence is "Li is ahead everywhere I looked" — and the two sentences differ, because Littlewood proved in 1914 that the difference changes sign infinitely often: somewhere past the largest table anyone has run, the census that never disagreed will turn. The space was not all of the question; the count was a detector, and the detector's "yes" was only ever a verdict of not-yet-dead. P03's scope note — passing is not proving — is this exhibit's one-line refutation, and Obligation 5's boundary sentence is the vaccine: "the question outruns the space — the count is a detector." Print it, and the temptation dies on the page.

Exhibit B · The mislabeled space — the right answer to the wrong question. "How many ways does 1729 decompose into two cubes?" Enumerate ordered pairs \((a, b)\) with \(a^3 + b^3 = 1729\) and the machine answers four: \((1, 12)\), \((12, 1)\), \((9, 10)\), \((10, 9)\). Enumerate unordered pairs and it answers two. Both counts are correct censuses — complete, disjoint, pruned by the book — and only one answers the question that was asked: the census reports on the space Obligation 1 pinned, and on nothing else. If the pin is sloppy, the number that comes back is the right answer to a question nobody asked, delivered with all the confidence of a completed sweep. The disaster is quiet because nothing crashed: 4 and 2 are both integers, both reproducible, both wrong-or-right only relative to the sentence around them. Pin the space before the machine runs — on paper, in words — because no run, and no second run, can repair a pin nobody wrote down.

Exhibit C · The silent wrap — the census that agrees with itself. Ask the machine to count domino tilings of the \(2 \times 47\) board and let it store counts in 32-bit arithmetic. The true count is \(4{,}807{,}526{,}976\); a 32-bit register holds \(2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296\) at most, wraps silently past it, and the census cheerfully reports \(512{,}559{,}680\). No warning, no crash — one number, confidently wrong by exactly \(2^{32}\). Now the bitter half: write the second engine Obligation 4 demands, a different route through the same space, and it may return the same wrong number — because the two engines share the arithmetic underneath, and the arithmetic is where the failure lives. This is the second engine's named limit: independence must extend below the route, to the arithmetic; when a count can approach a power of two, audit in a second number system (60-bit floats, digit strings, or a second machine entirely) — and check the ceiling before the run, not after. Three exhibits, three obligations: print the boundary sentence; pin the space; audit the arithmetic.

Scope and price of P25

When the move applies. The question's cases must be finite and listable — or made so by an argument: a pin that names the space, or a cap that shrinks an infinite question to a finite one, as Example 1's inequality did. When the question is genuinely infinite with no cap in sight, the census is a detector forever, and the honest asker says so out loud, then hands the question to a proof page.

Price 1 — finite does not mean feasible. Euler's 36 officers — arrange six regiments and six ranks in a \(6 \times 6\) square, one of each per row and column — is a finite question with \(36!\) raw arrangements, and it took Gaston Tarry years of hand enumeration to close it (1900–01); no discipline of pinning saved the pencil. Machines raise the ceiling; they do not remove it. The raw queens space at board 25 is \(25^{25} \approx 10^{34}\) placements — every census has a size it cannot pay, and the honest page names its budget before it runs.

Price 2 — the sentence around the machine is the mathematics. The machine only visits; the truth lives in the pin sentence that names the space and the boundary sentence that names the verdict. A census with no written pin is a number looking for a question, and Exhibit B shows how confidently it finds the wrong one.

Price 3 — the census proves nothing about the unvisited. Every case it checked is a proof; every pattern across the cases is a conjecture, at any depth of machine confirmation, as Exhibit A's prime-counting table has been saying "yes" for a century and Littlewood proved it will eventually say "no."

Price 4 — "the computer checked it" is itself a claim about code. The count is only as good as the enumerator: bugs, silent wraps (Exhibit C), and misread questions all return integers with a straight face. The working certificate is the second independent engine — different route, different arithmetic — plus a reader who can read the code; when two engines that share nothing agree, the answer has a witness in both. At the scale of the four-color theorem, this price became a decade-long argument about what a proof is; §7 tells that story. Nothing on this page escapes the obligation: the reader who quotes a census has adopted its code.

6 · What next

The doors this page opens

The nearest door: P66 · Prove by induction. The census returns two outputs, facts and patterns, and the patterns are this door's raw material. Example 3's recurrence \(a(n) = a(n-1) + 5a(n-2) + a(n-3) - a(n-4)\) has been machine-checked to any depth the reader can afford — and remains a conjecture at every depth, because the machine reads cases and never the law across them. Induction is the move that closes exactly this shape: a base case (the census's first rows, each already a proof), and one honest step that carries every case to the next. The census hands P66 a ladder it has tested a thousand rungs deep; P66 proves the ladder holds to every rung there is. The reader who leaves this page with a confirmed table and the discipline to call it a conjecture has already understood why the next page is next.

The working door: P36 · Count with characters. Example 3's column machine is a secret: the 16 masks and their transitions form a transition matrix, and the matrix's powers know the tiling counts without walking a single tiling — the count \(a(n)\) falls out of a \(16 \times 16\) matrix raised to the \(n\)-th power, and the four-term recurrence is the matrix's characteristic polynomial. This is counting without visiting: the census's question answered by algebra about the space's structure instead of a sweep through its cases. P36 grows the room from a bare 16-state machine to a group's full rack of characters, and counts — orbits, teams, colorings — that no census could ever afford to walk. When a space is too big to enumerate but too symmetric to resist, this is the door.

The far door: P45 · Proof at scale, together. Every census has a size it cannot pay alone (Price 1), and the frontier's answer to the four-color-scale question — a space of almost two thousand configurations, a four-year search and 1,200 hours of machine time — was to industrialize this page: split the space, agree on interfaces, run the parts in parallel, audit every part, and publish the whole as one proof. That project is P45's subject, and its trust problem is exactly Price 4 at planetary scale: "the computers checked it" is a claim about code, witnesses, and referees, and the frontier's repair is P44's formal verification — the enumerator itself refereed, line by line, by a kernel small enough to trust. P25's five obligations are the social contract those proofs run on; the reader who understood this page has already understood their honesty rules, one machine at a time.

The default habit

From this page on, the reader handed a finite-looking question too big for the pencil should run, without being told: write the pin sentence first — the exact space, the exact question, on paper, before any code — because the space is part of the claim and no run can repair an unwritten pin; write the enumerator boring — simple enough that a skeptic can read it in one sitting, complete because every case appears, disjoint because each appears once; prune only on rules the question itself owns, and note that pruning bought effort, not answers; run the second engine — different route, different arithmetic — and treat agreement as the working certificate it is; and print the boundary sentence with every answer: proof if the space was all of the question, detector if it was not, and nothing in between. Then, if a pattern fell out of the census on the way — a recurrence, a growth rate, a "still small" — label it a conjecture and hand it to P66. The habit takes five minutes at the question's edge and is the whole difference between a number and an answer.

7 · Where it came from

The census is older than the machine, and its honesty problems were found by hand. The eight queens puzzle was posed by the chess composer Max Bezzel in 1848, and Franz Nauck published the first solutions in 1850 — extending the puzzle to \(n\)-queens on the way, and settling the 92. Gauss, in his 1850 letters to the astronomer Heinrich Schumacher, called Nauck's count confirmable methodisches Tatonniren — methodical groping, from the French tâtonner — and described the very sweep this page runs: place row by row, abandon the broken branch, back up and try the next column. And the letters carry the census's hardest lesson: Gauss himself, the greatest calculator alive, at one point counted 72 of the 92. The obligation to miss nothing was priced — by the best there was — before any machine existed to help with it. Édouard Lucas printed the same recursive method in 1882, crediting Emmanuel Laquière: pruning is a nineteenth-century idea wearing silicon.

The same era closed a century-old question by pure enumeration. Euler, in 1782, had asked whether 36 officers — six regiments, six ranks — could stand in a square with every row and column holding each regiment and each rank exactly once, and suspected they could not. Gaston Tarry confirmed it around 1900–01, case by case, by hand — a finite question, a complete sweep, and a non-existence theorem whose proof was patience. The census, done by hand, was already a proof and already knew its own price: Tarry's sweep is the ancestor of Price 1, the demonstration that finite and feasible are different words.

The detector was born honest, too. In 1914 — before electronic computers — J. E. Littlewood proved that \(\pi(x)\) and \(\operatorname{Li}(x)\) trade the lead infinitely often, in a Comptes Rendus note: a theorem about a table nobody could yet run, announcing that the table would eventually lie. It has not yet lied: the census has been pushed to \(10^{14}\) (Kotnik, 2008) and \(\operatorname{Li}\) is still ahead at every single point, with the first lead-change now expected near \(1.4 \times 10^{316}\) — and Exhibit A's moral, that a table's testimony is a verdict of not-yet-dead, is exactly what Littlewood priced in advance. The same years gave the census its loveliest wrong-question exhibit: Hardy, visiting Ramanujan in the nursing home in 1917–18, remarked that his taxicab's number 1729 seemed dull, and Ramanujan answered that it is the smallest number that is a sum of two cubes in two different ways — one number, two decompositions, and Exhibit B's ordered-versus-unordered question hiding in the most famous anecdote in number theory.

The machine era industrialized all three lessons at once. Edsger Dijkstra took the queens as the exemplar of structured programming in 1972, publishing the pruned sweep in full detail; Niklaus Wirth's 1976 textbook printed the count this page's figure borrows — 15,720 candidate placements — and made the census a first-week program for a generation. Then the census grew to a theorem: Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken announced the four-color theorem on 21 June 1976, after a four-year search and some 1,200 hours on an IBM 360 — almost two thousand reducible configurations, each checked by machine, roughly ten billion logical decisions. The controversy that followed was Price 4 made loud: was "the computer checked it" a proof? The sequel is the audit's vindication — an error in the procedure was found (by Ulrich Schmidt, in the 1980s) and fixed by re-census in 1989; a second, independent engine (Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas, 1996) re-ran the whole argument with fewer configurations; and Georges Gonthier formalized the proof inside the Coq proof assistant in 2005, so that the enumerator itself — the code, every line — is now machine-refereed. The census's honesty rules, §3's five obligations, were never new: Gauss owed them, Tarry paid them, and the machine merely inherits them at speed.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 · Close the four-queen board by hand ●○○
§2's table says the \(4 \times 4\) board admits exactly two peaceful arrangements. Earn the number: (a) write the pin sentence — the disciplined space, in words. (b) Close the census by hand, and find both arrangements as column lists (one queen per row). (c) Verify each: distinct columns, distinct rising diagonals, distinct falling diagonals. (d) The census's own audit: show the two arrangements are one puzzle — that the board's rotations leave the first arrangement still, and its reflections give the second.

Hint

The pin is §2's, one size smaller: one queen per row, one per column — so the space is the 24 column-lists — with diagonal peace checked by hand. For (d), track where a queen at \((r, c)\) goes under the half-turn and the reflections.

Worked

(a) Pin: arrangements are lists \((c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4)\) of distinct columns — 24 of them, since the row pin is built in — peaceful when no two queens share a diagonal. (b) Sweeping: with \(c_1 = 1\), no \(c_2\) works on the main diagonal; \(c_1 = 2\) leads to \((2, 4, 1, 3)\); \(c_1 = 3\) to \((3, 1, 4, 2)\); \(c_1 = 4\), like \(c_1 = 1\), dies — the mirror of the first death. Two survivors. (c) For \((2, 4, 1, 3)\): queens at \((1,2), (2,4), (3,1), (4,3)\); columns \(2, 4, 1, 3\) distinct; differences \(r - c\) are \(-1, -2, 2, 1\) — distinct; sums \(r + c\) are \(3, 6, 4, 7\) — distinct. Peaceful. Its partner checks the same way. (d) The half-turn sends \((r, c)\) to \((5 - r, 5 - c)\): \((1,2) \to (4,3)\), \((2,4) \to (3,1)\) — both occupied — so the arrangement is fixed by the 180° rotation; the quarter-turn sends \((r, c)\) to \((c, 5 - r)\) and also returns the same list: the rotation group of order 4 stabilizes it. The orbit therefore has size \(8/4 = 2\): the four reflections all yield \((3, 1, 4, 2)\), the only other arrangement. The 4-board is §2's symmetry audit in miniature: two answers, one family, and the family structure explains why there is no third.

Exercise 2 · The fourth-power family, closed by cap and census ●●○
Example 1 closed the cube family. Close the fourth-power family the same way: which numbers equal the sum of the fourth powers of their digits? (a) Prove the cap: no candidate has six or more digits. (b) Show the five-digit range cannot be skipped — why must the census actually run it? (c) Run the census over the range the cap leaves, and report the family. (d) Audit with the digit-tuple engine of Example 1.

Hint

Imitate Example 1 with \(9^4 = 6561\) in place of \(9^3 = 729\): a \(d\)-digit number is at least \(10^{d-1}\), and the digit-power sum is at most \(6561d\). For (b), compute \(5 \times 6561\) against \(10^4\) before concluding anything.

Worked

(a) At \(d = 6\): \(6561 \times 6 = 39{,}366 < 10^5 = 100{,}000\), and each further digit multiplies the left by less than 2 and the right by 10 — so no number with six or more digits can meet its digit-power sum, ever. (b) At \(d = 5\): \(5 \times 6561 = 32{,}805 \ge 10^4\), so five-digit candidates genuinely exist (every five-digit number up to 32,805 is, in principle, reachable) — the cap does not close the five-digit range, and a census that skipped it would be Exhibit A wearing Example 1's clothes: honesty demands the sweep. (c) Census 1 to 32,805: the survivors are 1, 1634, 8208, 9474 — check one: \(1634 = 1 + 1296 + 81 + 256 = 1 + 6^4 + 3^4 + 4^4\); and \(8208 = 4096 + 16 + 0 + 4096\), \(9474 = 6561 + 256 + 2401 + 256\). The five-digit sweep finds nothing: the family is exactly \(\{1, 1634, 8208, 9474\}\), final forever. (d) The second engine enumerates digit-tuples: for each length \(\ell\), every \(\ell\)-tuple of digits with its fourth-power sum, asking which sums land on a number with those very digits — a sweep sharing no code with the number-sweep, returning the same four survivors and closing the certificate. And the composition is a proof: cap (argument) + census (machine) = the infinite question answered.

Exercise 3 · The symmetry audit, run cold ●●●
Example 2 claimed: twelve families, \(92 = 11 \times 8 + 1 \times 4\), one half-turn-symmetric arrangement, no mirror-symmetric ones. Earn all four claims. (a) Enumerate the 92 as column lists. (b) Implement the eight symmetries of the square as functions on column lists. (c) Sort the 92 into orbits and report the sizes. (d) Exhibit the half-turn-symmetric arrangement and verify the half-turn fixes it, square by square; count the arrangements fixed by any reflection; and explain the small family's size 4 from its stabilizer.

Hint

A column list \((c_1, \dots, c_8)\) is the function \(r \mapsto c_r\). The half-turn is \((r, c) \mapsto (9 - r, 9 - c)\); the quarter-turn is \((r, c) \mapsto (c, 9 - r)\); the reflections are these followed by \(c \mapsto 9 - c\). An orbit's size is the group's size divided by its stabilizer's size — so a family of four is an arrangement that sits still under exactly two of the eight moves.

Worked

(a)–(c) The pruned sweep of §2, collecting column lists, returns 92; sorting under the eight maps returns 12 orbits — eleven of size 8, one of size 4 — and \(11 \times 8 + 1 \times 4 = 92\) accounts for every solution, none twice. (d) The small family's canonical member: \((3, 5, 2, 8, 1, 7, 4, 6)\). The half-turn sends \((r, c)\) to \((9 - r, 9 - c)\): row 1's queen at \((1, 3)\) lands on \((8, 6)\) — occupied; row 2's \((2, 5)\) lands on \((7, 4)\) — occupied; row 3's \((3, 2)\) lands on \((6, 7)\) — occupied; row 4's \((4, 8)\) lands on \((5, 1)\) — occupied. All four rows mirror their partners; the arrangement is fixed. The stabilizer is exactly \(\{\text{identity}, \text{half-turn}\}\), order 2, so the orbit is \(8/2 = 4\) — and the other members are its two quarter-turns and its transpose — the vertical mirror lands on the 270° image, which is why eight maps reach only four arrangements. The reflection test: applying each of the four reflections to each of the 92 arrangements returns the arrangement itself zero times — no mirror-symmetric arrangement exists on the 8-board, a fact the audit certifies without any extra run. The census counted 92 puzzles; the audit found 12 — and §2's table never asked.

Exercise 4 · Wirth's number, earned — and the wrap it prevents ◆
(a) Implement the pruned row-by-row sweep of §2 for \(n = 1\) to 8, counting not only the solutions but the candidate placements examined — every column tried at every node, accepted or rejected. Verify the counts \(1, 0, 0, 2, 10, 4, 40, 92\) and the examination counts \(1, 6, 18, 60, 220, 894, 3584, 15{,}720\). (b) Implement the plain permutation-sweep second engine and verify the same eight counts; say precisely what the agreement does and does not certify. (c) Now the domino wrap of Exhibit C: count \(2 \times n\) domino tilings for \(n = 1\) to 48, and find the smallest \(n\) whose count exceeds \(2^{32}\), the value a 32-bit machine would print, and the audit that catches it.

Hint

For (a), instrument the loop: every column considered at every row counts as one examination, whether or not a queen lands there — Wirth's textbook count of 15,720 is this number, and the placements that land are 2,056. For (c), the \(2 \times n\) counts are Fibonacci's — and \(2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296\) is a ceiling, not a suggestion.

Worked

(a) The instrumented sweep returns, for \(n = 1\) to 8, the solutions 1, 0, 0, 2, 10, 4, 40, 92 and the examinations 1, 6, 18, 60, 220, 894, 3584, 15,720 — the last is the figure Niklaus Wirth published for his backtracking program in 1976, and the placements that land are 2,056. The cascade of §3's figure is now the reader's own: \(8^8 = 16{,}777{,}216\) raw, \(8! = 40{,}320\) disciplined, 15,720 examined, 2,056 placed, 92 found. (b) The permutation engine sweeps all \(n!\) column-lists and checks diagonals on complete arrangements only: same eight answers, zero shared code with the pruned engine. The agreement certifies the count — two independent routes, one answer — and certifies nothing about the examination counts, which are a property of one engine's route; those are audited by reading the code, which is the point of the exercise. (c) The \(2 \times n\) counts run \(1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \dots\) — Fibonacci — and the first to exceed \(2^{32}\) is \(n = 47\): the true count \(4{,}807{,}526{,}976\), against a ceiling of \(4{,}294{,}967{,}296\), so a 32-bit machine wraps and prints \(512{,}559{,}680\) — Exhibit C's number, reproduced. The audit that catches it: run the same census in double precision — which holds every integer up to \(2^{53}\) exactly — and compare; the engines agree to \(n = 46\) (count \(2{,}971{,}215{,}073\), fits) and disagree at 47, and the disagreement's address is the diagnosis: a mismatch at exactly \(2^{32}\) is an arithmetic failure, not a route failure. The second engine's independence, Exhibit C said, must extend below the route; here it is, built by hand.

Exercise 5 · The detector you cannot afford ◐
Exhibit A's table stops at \(10^8\). Extend it. (a) Sieve \(\pi(x)\) at \(x = 10^2, 10^3, \dots, 10^8\), and compute \(\operatorname{Li}(x)\) at the same points by the convergent series \(\operatorname{Li}(x) = \gamma + \ln \ln x + \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{(\ln x)^k}{k \cdot k!}\) (with \(\gamma\) Euler's constant). (b) Tabulate the gap \(\operatorname{Li}(x) - \pi(x)\) at each power, to four decimals. (c) Write the detector's honest report: what the table establishes, what Littlewood's 1914 theorem says it cannot establish, how far the published census runs (Kotnik, 2008), and where the first lead-change is now expected. (d) The research question the table begs: the gap grows at every point you ran — yet the relative gap \(\operatorname{Li}(x)/\pi(x)\) shrinks toward 1. Say why both can be true at once, and why neither sentence is evidence about the sign changes.

Hint

The series for \(\operatorname{Li}\) converges for every \(x > 1\) — sum it until the terms die. For (d), the prime number theorem's content is \(\pi(x) \sim \operatorname{Li}(x)\): relative error to zero, absolute error to anywhere. Littlewood's sign changes live in the gap between the two claims.

Worked

(a)–(b) The sieve and the series agree with Exhibit A and extend it: at \(x = 10^2\) to \(10^8\), \(\pi(x) = 25, 168, 1229, 9592, 78498, 664579, 5761455\), against \(\operatorname{Li}(x) \approx 30.1261, 177.6097, 1246.1372, 9629.8090, 78627.5492, 664918.4050, 5762209.3754\). The gaps: \(5.1261, 9.6097, 17.1372, 37.8090, 129.5492, 339.4050, 754.3754\) — \(\operatorname{Li}\) ahead at every point, by more and more. (c) The honest report: the table establishes "Li is ahead everywhere I looked," a verdict of not-yet-dead at seven points; Littlewood proved the difference changes sign infinitely often, so the table's pattern must fail somewhere; the published census runs to \(10^{14}\) with no sign change (Kotnik, 2008), and the first lead-change is now expected near \(1.4 \times 10^{316}\) — a point whose census would take a machine bigger than the universe. The boundary sentence writes itself: proof, never; detector, always. (d) Both sentences are true because they measure different things: \(\pi \sim \operatorname{Li}\) says the ratio goes to 1 — the relative error dies — while the difference can grow without bound on both sides of zero; indeed the difference is known to change sign by growing to enormous positive values, dipping negative, and returning. A table of gaps, however deep, samples the second behavior and certifies nothing about it; a table of ratios, however close to 1, certifies the first and not the sign. The detector's honesty is to print both limits plainly: the census reads cases, and the law across them — P66's law, and beyond it the research frontier's — is not the census's to give.
