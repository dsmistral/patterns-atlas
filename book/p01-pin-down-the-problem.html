The Patterns Atlas · P01 · the first move of the spine

Pin down the problem

Say precisely what you are asked — before you try to answer it.

P-ID. Every pattern in this book has a permanent ID: P01, P02, P03, … (the full ladder is on the index page). When a solution uses a move, we name it by its ID, so a page can cite a move the way a textbook cites an equation. This page builds P01 — the move every other page starts from.

1 · Before we begin

You have seen the book's premise on the index page: this book teaches moves, not topics, because moves transfer to problems you have never seen. Every move page starts the same way: a problem first, the move second, and never the other way round.

Here is the tension this first page resolves. When a solution goes wrong, the first place to look is not the algebra, not the diagram — it is the statement. Which problem did you actually solve? More failed attempts die of answering a subtly different question than was asked than die of bad mathematics. The good news: the repair is cheap, teachable, and it is the one move that improves every other move you own. This is P01.

2 · Meet the problem

A bat and a ball together cost ₹110. The bat costs ₹100 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

An answer arrives instantly, without effort, without your asking for it: ₹10. And it is wrong. Do not worry — you are in the company of most people who take a famous three-question psychology test (Shane Frederick, Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making, 2005), where this is the opening item.

Let us do what we should have done before answering anything: pin the problem down. Write down exactly what was said.

  • Let the ball cost b rupees.
  • “The bat costs ₹100 more than the ball” pins to: bat = b + 100. (Not: bat = 100. That is a different sentence.)
  • “Together ₹110” pins to: b + (b + 100) = 110.

Now the problem is finished with us: 2b = 10, so b = 5. The ball costs ₹5, the bat ₹105. Check: 105 − 5 = 100 ✓, and 5 + 105 = 110 ✓.

Notice where the intuitive answer came from. “₹10” is the correct answer to a nearby but different problem — “the bat costs ₹100, what does the ball cost?” Our intuition solved that one, and handed us its answer. The arithmetic was never the difficulty; the difficulty was which problem we were solving.

Verify it, don't trust it. We machine-checked the pinned statement and the intuitive one: with b = 5, bat − ball = 100 and the total is 110; with b = 10, the bat would have to be ₹110 to cost ₹100 more, making the total ₹120 — not ₹110. Recorded in this page's lab (block 9). Small checks like this are cheap; we will run them throughout the book.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P01 — Pin down the problem. Before attempting a solution, restate the problem in precise terms: define every word it uses, rewrite each given fact as a relationship you can compute with, and state exactly what form an answer must take — including what kind of claim the problem is.

Or, in words: never solve a sentence. Solve the precise statement you have written yourself, and check that your precise statement says the same thing as the sentence did.

The move has four parts — a checklist you can run on any problem, in about a minute:

Box P01.A — the pinning checklist.

  1. Define the terms. Every word the problem leans on (“odd”, “diagonal”, “at random”, “more than”) gets a definition you could read aloud without the problem in front of you.
  2. Restate the givens as relationships you can compute with: equations, memberships, inequalities — not prose.
  3. State the target. What would an answer look like? A number (with units)? an object? a proof?
  4. Name the claim type. Compute · Prove-for-all · Prove-exists · Disprove. The type decides what counts as success (Box P01.B below).

The fourth part deserves its own box, because it is the part most solvers skip:

Box P01.B — the four claim types.

Claim typeWhat success looks likeWhat destroys it
ComputeA number, with units and a stated form (exact? rounded?)A different quantity than the one asked
Prove-for-allAn argument that works for every case in range, parameter named (“for every integer n ≥ 1, …”)One counterexample, anywhere in range
Prove-existsOne honest construction or exhibitA “proof” that never exhibits the object
DisproveOne counterexample, in rangeAn argument about “most” cases (irrelevant — one suffices)

When does P01 apply? The three-key diagnosis, used throughout this book, asks: what is the claim type? what shape does the problem have? what tools are at hand? For P01 the answers are: every claim type; any problem not yet restated in your own precise words — especially verbal, novel, or deceptively familiar ones; and the only tools needed are definitions, notation, and a picture. That last point matters: P01 is the entry move, available before you know any mathematics beyond the statement itself.

Claim. In this book, “claim” means any precise mathematical statement that can be true or false — not “claim” as in shouting. Naming the claim type is the first act of a proof, and every later page (P11 contradiction, P66 induction, P03 stress-testing) presupposes you have done it.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. In each, watch the pinning do real work — not ceremony.

Example 1 (numbers) — “the sum of two odd numbers is even”

Pin it down (checklist, in order): odd means an integer of the form 2k + 1 for some whole k; even means 2m for some whole m. The givens: two such numbers, call them 2k + 1 and 2j + 1. The target: prove-for-all — the sum is even, for every choice.

Now the “proof” is one line of arithmetic: (2k + 1) + (2j + 1) = 2k + 2j + 2 = 2(k + j + 1). Even. Notice what happened: once the terms are pinned, the proof is the pinning — there is no further idea to find. This is the mark of a well-pinned problem: it becomes easy to see what remains to be done. Moves used: P01.

Example 2 (counting, geometry) — diagonals of a 12-sided polygon

“How many diagonals does a convex polygon with 12 sides have?”

Pinned: a diagonal is the segment joining two vertices that are not adjacent (adjacent pairs are the sides, and sides are not diagonals). So: count the pairs of vertices, subtract the pairs that are sides:

  • pairs of vertices: 12 × 11 ⁄ 2 = 66,
  • minus the 12 sides,
  • = 54 diagonals.

Notice what the pinning bought: the moment “diagonal” became “vertex pair, not adjacent”, the count turned into arithmetic — nothing else was needed. Getting the definition wrong (say, counting intersection points inside, or including sides) yields a different number for a different problem. The same pinning, with n in place of 12, gives the general count n(n − 3)/2.

Moves used: P01 — with P02 allowed a brief cameo as a checking device.

Example 3 (calculus) — a growing circle

“The radius of a circle increases at 2 cm per second. At the instant the radius is 5 cm, how fast is the area growing?”

Pinned, the target is a precise object: the rate of change of area with respect to time — that is, dA/dt, in cm²/s. (Not dA/dr: the problem's clock runs in seconds; units are the tell.) The given: dr/dt = 2 cm/s; A = πr²; at the instant of interest, r = 5 cm. Chain rule, once:

dA/dt = 2πr · (dr/dt) = 2π · 5 · 2 = 20π ≈ 62.83 cm²/s.

“How fast is the area growing” never becomes computable until it is pinned as dA/dt with units. In related-rates problems, the pinning is most of the work — which is why examiners say students lose these problems in the first line, not the last.

Moves used: P01. (The chain rule itself is assumed at the class-12 entry level; the page builds no calculus beyond it.)

Units as intuition. “cm² per second” — area units, flowing in time. If your pinned target does not carry sensible units, that is a mis-pinning you can see from outside, before any computation. We machine-checked this one by simulating the growing circle over a tiny time step: the time-stepped rate matches 20π to four decimal places (62.8319 vs 62.8319).

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

P01 is necessary, cheap, and honest. None of that makes it a solution.

  • Pinned ≠ solved. The checklist produces a precise statement, not a proof. P01 tells you what must be shown — never how. If a problem resists after a clean pinning, the pinning has still helped: it shows the resistance is real, not verbal.
  • Over-pinning. Wrapping a one-step insight in heavy notation can bury it. If the pinned statement is longer than the problem, you may be solving bureaucracy. Mitigation: pin as lightly as the claim needs. Price: occasionally re-pin when a lighter statement turns out too thin. Default: full checklist the first time you meet a problem type; a one-line pinning once the type is familiar.
  • Mis-pinning. The bat-and-ball failure had this shape: an intuition quietly substitutes a nearby, easier problem and answers that. The checklist's cure is blunt: write the givens as relationships (“bat = ball + 100”), because prose hides substitutions that equations cannot.
  • Underspecified statements. Sometimes the sentence genuinely does not determine one problem. Joseph Bertrand's classic (Calcul des probabilités, 1889) asks: a chord of a circle is chosen “at random”; what is the probability it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle? Three honest pinnings of “at random” give three different answers: 1/3 (random endpoints), 1/2 (random midpoint along a radius), 1/4 (random midpoint anywhere in the disc). We simulated all three by machine (see Exercise 6): the simulation lands on 0.3365, 0.5015, 0.2508 with 100,000 trials each — three honest pinnings, three different problems.
Scope note. P01 makes the choice of interpretation visible; it cannot make the choice for you. When a problem is underspecified, the mathematician's move is to choose a definition and say so — the source technique behind this page is called axiomatize from instances: when a concept is vague, pin it by writing the precise rule that your examples obey. That is not a confession of weakness; it is how “number”, “function”, and “continuity” themselves got their definitions.

6 · What to try next

Once a problem is pinned, route by claim type (Box P01.B):

  • Target is a computation → reach for P02 · Experiment with small cases: build a small table before trusting any formula. Mitigation for the impatient: a table of three cases costs a minute. Price: occasionally the pattern in the small table misleads (P03 exists to stress it). Default: table first, formula second.
  • Claim is prove-for-all, with a whole-number parameter → P66 · Prove by induction (planned). If instead the claim says something cannot happen → P11 · Prove by contradiction (planned). Default: induction when there is a natural “next case”, contradiction when the negation is concrete.
  • Claim is prove-exists → P07 · Build an auxiliary object (planned): construct the thing. Price: construction is craft; it can take a genuinely new idea.
  • A vague term you must choose → the deeper gear of this very page: axiomatize from instances — write your examples first, then the rule they obey, and say which you chose.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. Pinning down before proceeding is ancient habit: Euclid's Elements opens by defining its terms — “a point is that which has no part” — before stating a single theorem. The move became a discipline at the turn of the last century, when David Hilbert's Grundlagen der Geometrie (1899) pinned down the axioms of geometry itself. And for problem-solvers it became a named phase in George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945), whose first of four phases is, precisely, understand the problem. The registry behind this book tracks the same move in its modern, computational form, under the technique id axiomatizeFromInstances. None of this history is load-bearing — it is seasoning — but it says something reassuring: the first move in the book is the oldest one in mathematics.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Three consecutive integers sum to 48. Which integers?

Hint

Pin the unknowns first: name the middle integer m. What are the other two, in terms of m?

Worked answer

Pinned: the integers are m − 1, m, m + 1, and (m − 1) + m + (m + 1) = 48. So 3m = 48, m = 16: the integers are 15, 16, 17 (check: 15 + 16 + 17 = 48 ✓). Notice how pinning by the middle one makes the equation symmetric — a preview of P08.

Exercise 2 ●○○

A shopkeeper marks a jacket 20% above cost, then sells it at a 20% discount. Did the shopkeeper gain or lose, and by what percentage?

Hint

Pin “20% of what?” — the markup and the discount are percentages of different bases.

Worked answer

Pinned: cost c, marked price 1.2c, selling price 0.8 × 1.2c = 0.96c. Sold at 96% of cost: a 4% loss. The tempting answer “no change” pins the discount to the cost as well — a different problem. Exact-fraction check (machine-verified): 4⁄5 × 6⁄5 = 24⁄25 = 0.96.

Exercise 3 ●●○

A car travels from A to B at 60 km/h and returns along the same road at 40 km/h. What is its average speed for the whole trip?

Hint

Pin the phrase “average speed” (Box P01.A, item 3) — it is a rate of total distance over total time. The answer is not the average of two numbers.

Worked answer

Pinned: average speed = total distance ÷ total time. Take a concrete leg (the answer cannot depend on it): 120 km each way. Times: 120⁄60 = 2 h out, 120⁄40 = 3 h back; total 240 km in 5 h → 48 km/h. The 50 km/h answer averages two speeds over equal weights in time — but the slow leg took more time, so it must weigh more. Machine check with the concrete numbers: 240 ÷ (2 + 3) = 48.

Exercise 4 ●●●

How many zero digits does 100! (100 factorial) end with?

Hint

Pin “ends with k zeros”: 10k divides 100!. And pin 10 as a product: 2 × 5.

Worked answer

Pinned: the count is the largest k with 10k dividing 100!, i.e. min(v₂, v₅) — the exponents of 2 and 5 in its factorization. Counting fives in 1, 2, …, 100: multiples of 5 contribute 20, and each multiple of 25 carries a second five, contributing 4 more; 20 + 4 = 24. Twos are plentiful (97 of them). So the answer is 24. Two independent machine checks agree: counting factors gives v₅ = 24, v₂ = 97; and computing 100! exactly (158 digits, beginning 9.33262… × 10157) and reading off its trailing zeros also gives 24. Detectors, using the same pinning: 10! ends in 2 zeros, 25! in 6 — these small cases can expose a wrong formula, though only the argument proves this one.

Exercise 5 ◆ Diagnose the pinning

Each item below is a proposed pinning. No hint: decide which pinnings are honest and which fail, and say why. (All three are machine-verified in the lab.)

  1. To show “n² + n + 41 is prime for every whole number n”, it suffices to check n = 1, 2, 3, and observe the values are prime.
  2. To show “the sum of two odd numbers is even”, pin “odd” as: of the form 2k + 1 for some whole number k.
  3. To count the diagonals of a 12-sided polygon, pin “diagonal” as: a pair of vertices that are not adjacent.
Worked answer

(1) Fails — claim type mismatch (Box P01.B: prove-for-all is destroyed by one counterexample, and completed by nothing less than a universal argument). Any number of checked instances leaves the claim open. This polynomial is a famous trap: it is prime for every whole number n from 0 to 39 — forty consecutive primes (machine-verified) — and at n = 40 it reads 40² + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 × 41, composite. Instances are detectors, never proofs of for-all claims; P03 will build this into a full move.

(2) Honest. The definition is what the proof needs, and it is faithful to the word: instances (… −3, −1, 1, 3, …) all fit 2k + 1 with k whole.

(3) Honest — the same pinning as Example 2, and it makes the count mechanical: 66 pairs minus 12 sides = 54.

Exercise 6 ◐ Bertrand's three problems

“A chord of a circle is chosen at random. What is the probability it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?” We pinned three different meanings of “at random”: (i) both endpoints chosen uniformly on the circle; (ii) the chord's midpoint chosen uniformly along a radius, in a uniformly random direction; (iii) the midpoint chosen uniformly over the whole disc. Explore: (a) compute the three probabilities by geometry (no simulation needed — each is one line once pinned); (b) verify against the recorded lab simulation (100,000 trials each: 0.3365, 0.5015, 0.2508); (c) hardest and most interesting: if you physically threw broomsticks at a circle drawn on the floor, which pinning would model your experiment? There is no single right answer — that is the lesson. A vague phrase is not one problem; pinning it is a choice, and the choice is yours to make and to state.

Ghost roots — when a legal step opens a side door

Solve, by routine school algebra, the innocent-looking

√(x + 2) = x.

Autopilot squares both sides: x + 2 = x², so x² − x − 2 = 0, so (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0 — candidates x = 2 and x = −1. Now check both against the original equation, as the checklist demands: at x = 2, √4 = 2 ✓; at x = −1, √1 = 1 ≠ −1. One of the two “answers” is an impostor.

Where did it come from? The symbol √ means the non-negative root, so the original statement silently requires x ≥ 0 — a wall the squared equation does not carry. Squaring is a legal step, but a one-way door: it erases the sign that separated −1 from +1, and the impostor x = −1 walks in. In fact −1 is an honest solution of the mirror equation −√(x + 2) = x — the door we opened let that equation's answer in too (machine-checked in the lab).

Moves used: P01 — with a first taste of P78 · Reversible steps (planned).

Box P01.C — the reversible squaring law. For real quantities A and B:

√A = B  ⇔  (A = B² and B ≥ 0).

Or, in words: a square root equals a number exactly when the square matches and the root's sign wall is respected — and no separate “A ≥ 0” check is needed, because A = B² already forces A ≥ 0.

Scope. Squaring without the sign wall is a one-way door: it solves √A = B and its mirror −√A = B in one stroke, so impostors can arrive. Mitigation, price, default: when squaring hands you a finite list of candidates, square freely and check every candidate back — cheap, and always safe. When the answer is a range of parameters or an inequality, do not square-and-hope: carry the side condition B ≥ 0 through the work, at the price of one extra inequality in flight. The full one-way/two-way discipline is the subject of P78 · Reversible steps (planned); re-attacking the survivors with nasty concrete values is P03 · Stress-test with special cases (planned).

The quantifier spine — who picks, and when

New word: quantifier. A quantifier is the part of a statement that says who chooses a value, and in what order. “For every” (∀) hands the choice to an adversary; “there exists” (∃) leaves the choice to you, the builder — but only after everything chosen before it is on the table. Read “for every n, there exists M” as a two-player game and the order of the phrases becomes the whole content.

What does it mean for a sequence x₁, x₂, x₃, … to be bounded? Two candidate pinnings, identical except for word order:

  • Specification A. For every n, there exists M with |xₙ| ≤ M.
  • Specification B. There exists M such that, for every n, |xₙ| ≤ M.

In A, the ceiling M is chosen after n — it may depend on n. In B, M is locked in before any n arrives: one ceiling for all time. Test both pinnings on two sequences: aₙ = (−1)ⁿ, which flickers −1, +1, −1, +1, …, and bₙ = n, which climbs 1, 2, 3, ….

Under A, even bₙ = n passes: whatever n the adversary names, the builder answers M = n + 1 (the lab played 1,000 rounds; the builder won 1,000). But A then says only that each single term is a finite number — true of every sequence that exists, and not what anyone means by “bounded”. Under B, aₙ passes with the single fixed ceiling M = 1 (100,000 terms machine-checked, none escaping), while every integer ceiling for bₙ is broken by the concrete counter-move n = ⌊M⌋ + 1 (nine ceilings tested, nine broken). Specification B is the honest pinning of “bounded” — two words changed order, and the meaning changed completely. This is the pinning checklist's quiet fourth item in action: before solving, know who picks what, and when.

Moves used: P01.

Exercise 7 ●○○

Find all real solutions of √(2x + 3) = x, pinning the domain wall before you square.

Hint

Box P01.C: squaring is honest only together with the wall x ≥ 0. What candidates does the quadratic hand you, and what does the wall remove?

Worked answer

Pinned first: the left side is a principal root, so any solution obeys x ≥ 0. Squaring with the wall: 2x + 3 = x² and x ≥ 0, i.e. x² − 2x − 3 = 0, candidates 3 and −1. The wall keeps x = 3 (check: √9 = 3 ✓) and rejects −1, at which √1 = 1 ≠ −1 — the same impostor shape as the ghost root above. Machine-verified: the extension lab finds exactly {3}.

Exercise 8 ●●○

For each real parameter a, solve (a² − 1)x = a − 1. An autopilot divides by a² − 1 and reports x = 1⁄(a + 1). For which a is that honest — and what really happens at the other values?

Hint

Dividing by D is reversible exactly when D ≠ 0. Which two values of a make the divisor a disguised zero? Substitute each into the un-divided equation.

Worked answer

Pinned: division by a² − 1 is an equivalence only when a ≠ 1 and a ≠ −1; those two values demand their own cases.

  • a ≠ ±1: x = (a − 1)⁄(a² − 1) = 1⁄(a + 1) — the unique solution, and the autopilot's formula is honest here.
  • a = 1: the equation reads 0 · x = 0 — true for every real x. At this value the autopilot's formula did not merely err; it discarded infinitely many solutions for one.
  • a = −1: the equation reads 0 · x = −2 — true for no x. The solution set is empty.

Machine-verified: the lab sweeps a from −40 to 40 (159 generic checks, 0 failures), confirms the all-reals case at a = 1, and the empty case at a = −1.

Exercise 9 ◆ Diagnose the impossible minimum

No hint — the diagnosis is the exercise. A student is asked for the minimum value of f(θ) = (sin θ + cos θ)² + 4(sin θ + cos θ) + 7 over all real θ. They substitute u = sin θ + cos θ, complete the square to (u + 2)² + 3, set u = −2, and report a minimum of 3. Explain precisely why the value 3 is impossible, name the pinning that was skipped, and give the true minimum.

Worked answer

The skipped pinning is the arena of u. Squaring u gives u² = 1 + sin 2θ, so 0 ≤ u² ≤ 2 and u lives in [−√2, √2]. But −2 < −√2: the point u = −2 is outside the arena — no real θ produces it, so the reported minimum is attained nowhere. On the legal arena, u + 2 ≥ 2 − √2 > 0, so (u + 2)² + 3 increases with u, and the minimum sits at the left edge u = −√2 (reached at θ = −3π⁄4): f = 2 − 4√2 + 7 = 9 − 4√2 ≈ 3.3431. Since 3 < 9 − 4√2, the student's value lies strictly below the true minimum — impossible, and visibly so the moment the arena is written down. Machine-checked: a 100,000-point dense scan bottoms out at 3.343146 (matching 9 − 4√2 to six places, at θ = −2.356194 = −3π⁄4), and the arena scan gives max|sin θ + cos θ| = √2 — never 2.

Exercise 10 ◐ The ghost-root phase transition

Replace the 2 in the ghost-root equation by a parameter: √(x + c) = x. Map how many real solutions exist as c runs from −∞ to +∞, using Box P01.C as your only tool. What you should find: a boundary value of c below which there are no solutions at all; a two-solution window where the quadratic's both candidates survive — zero ghost roots, the opposite of the story that opened this section (check the witness c = −2⁄9, whose candidates 1⁄3 and 2⁄3 both pass); and the familiar one-real-plus-one-ghost regime once c is positive (check c = 2).
