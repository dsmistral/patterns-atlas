The Patterns Atlas · P61 · the eleventh move of tier 5

Straighten and unstraighten

A curved world is expensive: products on a circle, a crossing at a corner, a form that refuses to separate. The move is a change of clothes for the computation — straighten the world near the problem, compute where the problem is transparent, and unstraighten the answer back to where it lives. The discipline is the round trip: the change of clothes must be invertible or it is theft, and whatever survives the round trip was never a coordinate artifact at all — it is the invariant, and it is the answer’s certificate.

New word: the chart. A chart is a dictionary between a piece of a curved world and a piece of the straight one — a bijection, both ways honest, from the points you care about to numbers you can compute with. Transport is what you do with the dictionary: carry the question across, compute, carry the answer back. The two demands that make it a move and not a trick: the dictionary must be invertible on its patch (a projection is not a chart — it forgets, and what it forgets it cannot give back), and it is local (a patch is a piece, not the whole world — the rest of the world is still there, waiting at the patch’s edge). What survives the round trip — the value, the count, the sign pattern that comes out the same through every honest dictionary — is the invariant, and naming it is the point of the whole exercise.

1 · Before we begin

P48 taught that an object is known by its probes: to study a thing, feed it every map and watch what it says. This page is that lesson with the maps chosen kindly: a chart is a probe whose answers you can read, and straightening is the choice of probe that makes the answer legible. P16 is already in the room — the t-line and the circle below are one problem in two wardrobes, and the dictionary is the isomorphism that proves it. P47 supplies the grammar: transport is only useful when it respects the structure you are carrying — the naturality discipline, checking the squares commute. And P62 — whose footer sent you here — built one repair and read it two ways: matching families at every region, germs at every point, and the two readings were one sheaf. This page makes that duality a discipline: compute in the straightened world, interpret in the curved one, and the round trip that survives it is an invariant.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P31 and P60 taught that when a construction is forced — by a universal property, by exhaustion over every candidate — the forcing is the proof, and nothing smaller or freer would do. Straightening feels like the opposite temperament: a change of coordinates is a choice, freely made, and choices are exactly what P31 taught us to distrust. The resolution is that the choice is only a route. Different charts of the same patch must deliver the same transported answers — or the answers were never about the curved world, only about the dictionary. So the route is free and the arrival is forced, and the page’s whole apparatus exists to check the second half: the round trip, run both ways, machine-verified over whole censuses. When two straightenings of the same stubborn object disagree, one of them was not a straightening — and the lab below catches exactly that failure, by injection.

2 · Meet the problem

The circle that will not multiply. The curved world is the unit circle, \\(x^2 + y^2 = 1\\). The job: multiply two of its points — rotate the plane, add angles, whatever the operation turns out to be — and the class-12 toolbox already has an identity for it: the complex-multiplication route of the bridge track’s B12 page, \\((x_1, y_1) \\odot (x_2, y_2) = (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2,\\; x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1)\\), and it works. But an identity that works is not an identity that explains. Where does the formula come from? Compute it directly on the circle and the two coordinates stay tangled; the operation is real, the circle is curved, and the product has no straight face.

The straightening, by hand. Stand at the anchor \\(A = (-1, 0)\\) — the one point of the circle we decline to chart, for a reason the census will expose. Every line through \\(A\\) of slope \\(t\\) meets the circle at \\(A\\) and at exactly one other point: substitute \\(y = t(x+1)\\) into \\(x^2 + y^2 = 1\\) and the quadratic in \\(x\\) — \\((1+t^2)x^2 + 2t^2 x + (t^2 - 1) = 0\\) — has \\(-1\\) as one root. The product of the two roots is the constant term over the leading coefficient, \\((t^2-1)/(1+t^2)\\); dividing out the known root \\(-1\\), the second root is \\((1-t^2)/(1+t^2)\\), and substituting back into \\(y = t(x+1)\\) gives \\(y = 2t/(1+t^2)\\). The point is

\\[(x, y) \\;=\\; \\left( \\frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2},\\; \\frac{2t}{1 + t^2} \\right), \\qquad t \\;=\\; \\frac{y}{x + 1},\\]

where the second formula is the dictionary read backwards: given the point, the slope that produced it is \\(t = y/(x+1)\\), straight off the construction, so the round trip is built in. The curved world minus the anchor is now a straight line: the \\(t\\)-line, with every rational \\(t\\) naming one rational point of the circle and vice versa. \\[t_3 \\;=\\; \\frac{t_1 + t_2}{1 - t_1 t_2},\\]

and the circle’s multiplication is the t-line’s addition-in-disguise — the addition law of the slope coordinate. One receipt, digit for digit: the points at \\(t = 1/2\\) and \\(t = 1/3\\) are \\((3/5, 4/5)\\) and \\((4/5, 3/5)\\); the t-route says \\(t_3 = (1/2 + 1/3)/(1 - 1/6) = (5/6)/(5/6) = 1\\), the point \\((0, 1)\\); the complex route computes \\(x_3 = 12/25 - 12/25 = 0\\) and \\(y_3 = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1\\) — the same point, no arithmetic luck involved. The invariant. What survived the round trip? On-curve-ness itself: every transported point satisfies \\(x^2 + y^2 = 1\\) exactly — the machine checks all \\(47\\) window points, and the check is not a formality, it is the certificate that the dictionary never invented a point. 3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P61 — Straighten and unstraighten. Three steps, each with its own proof obligation. (1) Find the straight patch. Locate the part of the curved world that already behaves like the straight one — the point the whole dictionary will hang from: an anchor point, a non-singular spot, a nonzero leading coefficient. Build the chart by hand and read the inverse off the construction: \\(t = y/(x+1)\\) was not cleverness, it was the definition of the line pencil written backwards. (2) Transport the computation. Carry the question across and do the algebra in the straight world — with the legality gate in force: the dictionary must be invertible on its patch, and it must respect the structure being carried (the addition law carries the circle’s product because the chart respects it). (3) Unstraighten and name the survivor. Carry the answer back through the same dictionary, check that it lands where it must (on-circle, exactly), and name the invariant — what did not depend on the chart — because the invariant is the certificate. Then report the edges honestly: what the chart could not see is part of the answer.

Or, in words: the curved world is not wrong and the straight world is not truer — the straight world is cheaper, and the price of the discount is a round trip you must complete. Compute there, interpret here, and the invariant is the receipt that your computation was ever about here at all. The move’s honesty lives in the two directions: straightening without unstraightening is numerology, and unstraightening without checking the survivor is laundering — an answer dressed in curved-world clothes that no round trip actually delivered.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the chart must be invertible on its patch. A bijection, both ways, every point of the patch accounted for. Jam-mode: the projection — a map that collapses the world onto a convenient axis and looks like a straightening. \\(\\S4\\) has the receipt: the signature of a quadratic form is carried faithfully through all \\(189\\) invertible congruence round trips and destroyed by the projection on \\(20\\) of \\(27\\) forms — what a non-invertible map forgets, no computation can give back.

Key 2 — the patch must be honest about its edges. A chart covers a piece; the missing point is a fact, not a flaw, and the computation must report where the dictionary ran out. Jam-mode: reading the chart as the world — \\(\\S2\\) counted \\(46\\) product pairs landing on the anchor, and the honest census says so; a computation that silently drops them has reported a smaller world than the one it was asked about.

Key 3 — the invariant must be named before the computation is trusted. Different charts of the same patch must deliver the same survivor: that agreement is the whole proof that the answer was about the curved world. Jam-mode: computing first and “discovering” the invariant in your own answer — the circular route; or trusting a chart-relative quantity that a different chart would contradict. The lab’s injection rows exist for exactly this: a quantity that changes under honest straightening is not an invariant, and the machine must catch it.

4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — the crossing that will not straighten linearly. The curved world this time is the node \\(xy = 0\\): two honest branches — the coordinate axes — meeting at the origin. First question, the invariant question: is the crossing a fact about the world or an artifact of coordinates? The obstruction is now visible, and it deserves its name. 5 · Exhibits: when the chart lies

Every pattern earns its keep by failing. Three exhibits — a chart that quietly shrinks the world, an answer carried home through the wrong dictionary, and a hope the move never promised — with the mitigation, the price, and the default.

Exhibit A — the chart that forgets. The t-chart of §2 is honest about one point — the anchor — and its census says so: \\(46\\) product pairs land on the unchartable point. But the forgetfulness is bigger than one point, and the exhibit is the shape of it. The t-line is open at both ends — a world where “going to infinity” means leaving, with no return — while the circle is closed: travel far enough in one direction and you come home. No bijection can reconcile that, and the chart is where the impossibility hides: the anchor is not a missing row of the dictionary, it is the line’s failure to close. §4 names the obstruction honestly — compactness, with the winding number as the invariant that measures it — but the failure mode the exhibit warns against stops there: a computation that runs entirely inside one chart, never meets the anchor, and quietly reports an open world’s answers about a closed one. The circle’s own census is the counter-receipt: the \\(46\\) anchor landings were counted, not dropped, and that counting is the difference between a chart and a census.

Mitigation: run the boundary census before trusting any interior computation — where the dictionary runs out, and how many of the problem’s cases land there (§2: \\(46\\), exactly the non-zero slopes, each paired with its reciprocal). A chart without a counted edge is a map without a frame.

Price: the missing point is irreparable in one chart. The fix is a second chart and a transition — §4’s two-chart cover — and the price is the bookkeeping: a transition law that must itself be checked, on the whole overlap, before any mixed-dictionary answer is trusted.

Default: one chart, one counted edge. If the computation’s cases touch the edge, either count them as edge-landings (honest) or add the second chart before computing (thorough) — but never let the edge be silent.

Exhibit B — the mixed-chart bug. The failure is quiet because it looks industrious: transport the question through chart A, compute honestly, and carry the answer back — through chart B. The bug is not sloppiness; it is two dictionaries that both work, misread as interchangeable. The quadratic-form census of §4 has the receipt at desk size: the projection \\(E = [1,0;0,0]\\) flattens every form, so the “answer” comes back through a non-invertible map — and the collapsed signature disagrees with the true one on \\(20\\) of the \\(27\\) forms. The projection is not a chart (Key 1), but the exhibit is subtler than the projection: even honest charts mix. Chart A’s \\(t\\) and chart B’s \\(s\\) both straighten the circle, and an answer transported through one and read in the other is wrong by the transition \\(s = -1/t\\) — the \\(46\\)-slope census of §4 is the receipt that the transition, once checked on the whole overlap, makes the mixing lawful. The cure is exactly one: through the same dictionary out as in, or through a transition law verified case by case first.

Mitigation: name the chart in the answer’s paperwork. “The signature is \\((1,1)\\)” is a verdict, because every invertible congruence agrees — \\(189\\) of \\(189\\) round trips; “the form’s first coordinate is \\(2\\)” is not a verdict — it is a chart-relative fact, and a different chart contradicts it.

Price: checking transitions is real work — the two-chart cover costs \\(46\\) slope checks and both round trips — and on bigger worlds the overlap is where the labor lives.

Default: through the same dictionary, always; across dictionaries, only through a verified transition. The lab’s injection rows exist for exactly this: a quantity that changes under an honest re-straightening was never an invariant.

Exhibit C — what the move never does. Straightening makes computation cheap; it does not make existence true. The circle’s multiplication law is a fact about the circle — proved by transporting, computing, and checking all \\(2{,}209\\) pairs — and the chart was the route, not the reason. But the move can be misread as proving things it merely relocates: the t-line’s addition law was already true before any chart existed, and a straightening that “discovers” a product on a world where no product was ever constructed has smuggled the conclusion in with the coordinates. The discipline is the one §1 borrowed from P31 and P60: verdicts come from forcing — a universal property, an exhaustion — and the chart is only a route to the computation. The move also never proves the curved world exists: the circle is given, the node is given, and if the question is does a world with these properties exist at all?, straightening is the wrong tool — the honest answers are the universal constructions and completions, where existence is the theorem.

Mitigation: state what was transported and what was proved. “The product law holds: verified on \\(2{,}209\\) pairs in both worlds” is a receipt; “the chart shows the circle is a group” is a category error with good posture.

Price: the receipts are censuses, and censuses are finite — this page’s windows are \\(47\\) slopes, \\(27\\) forms, \\(63\\) triples, and the honest claim stops at the window’s edge.

Default: the chart is a route; the verdict comes from the round trip. No round trip, no claim.
