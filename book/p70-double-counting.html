The Patterns Atlas · P70 · the thirteenth move of tier 2

Double counting

Count the same set of things twice — once along one axis, once along another — and the two answers must agree, because they are two names for one number. Forced agreement sounds humble; used deliberately, it is one of combinatorics' sharpest tools: identities fall out, impossibilities surface, and estimates appear from a table you already owned. This page is P02's table-reading turned on its side: P02 read the rows; here we read the rows and the columns, and insist they tell the same story.

New word: incidence. An incidence is a membership pair — a point lying on a line, a guest taking part in a handshake, a chairperson serving on a committee. Every double count is about a set of incidences, seen from two sides. New word: ledger. A ledger (or incidence table) is a table whose rows and columns are two families of objects, with a \(1\) in cell \((r, c)\) exactly when row-object \(r\) and column-object \(c\) are incident. The row sums and the column sums are two readings of one number: the count of \(1\)s. New word: cherry. A cherry is a two-edge path — a middle object joined to two ends. Counting cherries by their middles and by their end-pairs is the workhorse of extremal graph theory (Exemplar C runs one). New word: parity argument. A parity argument keeps only the remainder of a count modulo \(2\). Contributions from the interior come in pairs and vanish; what survives is exactly the boundary — a double count with the fat trimmed away (Exercise 5 runs one).

1 · Before we begin

P02 handed you the discovery engine — compute the small cases honestly and read the table. Its box said it outright:

Pattern P02 — Experiment with small cases. When a problem carries a whole-number size parameter \(n\) (or an object that comes in sizes 1, 2, 3, …), compute the smallest instances by hand, completely and honestly; read the table — its differences first — before guessing; state every guess as a conjecture and test it against fresh cases before believing it.

Or, in words: don't stare at the tenth floor. Build the first three floors by hand and watch how each floor is added — the building's rule is in the adding.

And you have already used the move this page names, once, without a license. On P02, Moser's circle problem killed the doubling conjecture with the count 31 — and the repair formula counted chord crossings by noticing that every four points determine exactly one crossing. That "every four" is a double count in disguise: crossings were tallied by their four-point causes, and the tally had to agree with the crossings drawn. Here is the tension this page resolves. A table looks like a list — one row after another, read left to right. But a table is a rectangle: the same grand total is visible along the rows and along the columns, and the two readings cannot disagree, because both are the count of the \(1\)s. One reading describes; two readings that must agree prove. The move from reading to proving is this page's whole content.

2 · Meet the problem: the impossible dinner party

Nine guests attend a dinner party. At the end of the evening, each guest reports: I shook exactly three hands. Could every report be accurate?

Try to draw it and you will keep failing — eight guests manage three handshakes each, and the ninth is stuck at two or four. But drawing is searching, and searching has no verdict. The ledger has. Make a table: rows are the nine guests, columns are the handshakes that took place, and cell \((v, e)\) holds a \(1\) when guest \(v\) took part in handshake \(e\). Now read the rectangle two ways.

By columns: every handshake involves exactly two guests, so every column holds exactly two \(1\)s. Whatever the number of handshakes, the total number of \(1\)s is twice it — an even number, no matter what.

By rows: each of the nine guests took part in three handshakes, so each row holds three \(1\)s, and the total is \(9 \times 3 = 27\) — an odd number.

The same \(1\)s, counted twice, gave 27 and an even number. Both readings are airtight; only the setup can be wrong. No arrangement of handshakes exists — not unlikely, not hard to find: impossible, and the proof is two sentences. A toy ledger shows the mechanics working when the numbers cooperate — four guests \(A, B, C, D\), handshakes \(AB, BC, CD\):

Notice what the ledger bought. No case split over "how the handshakes might be arranged", no cleverness — one rectangle, two readings, and the forced agreement (here, forced disagreement) settled everything. The same ledger, read honestly, produces identities among binomial coefficients (Exemplar A), estimates in number theory (Exemplar B), and bounds in graph theory (Exemplar C). The rest of this page names the move and runs it in three neighborhoods.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P70 — Double counting

Pattern P70 — Double counting. When a problem's objects can be organized as incidences between two families \(A\) and \(B\): (1) name the incidence set \(I \subseteq A \times B\) out loud — say exactly what one member of \(I\) is, before counting anything; (2) count \(|I|\) by rows (for each \(a \in A\), how many \(b\) are incident to it — total \(\sum_{a} r(a)\)) and by columns (for each \(b \in B\), how many \(a\) are incident to it — total \(\sum_{b} c(b)\)); (3) equate: \(\sum_{a} r(a) = \sum_{b} c(b) = |I|\). The identity is exact — no approximation entered anywhere — and its power is asymmetry: one side of the ledger is often smooth, known, or bounded while the other is jagged, wanted, or free, and the equation hands you the hard side at the price of the easy one.

Or, in words: count the crowd by the doors and by the seats — the answers must match, because both are the crowd. If they do not match, you miscounted, or the party was never possible; either way, the ledger has told you something no single reading could.

The three-key diagnosis. The claim type: an identity between two sums, a parity statement (the count mod 2 is all you need), or an upper bound dressed as a count. The problem shape: the objects come in pairs across two families, one side of the ledger is regular and the other ragged — each handshake touches exactly two guests, but guests touch wildly different numbers of handshakes. The tools at hand: P02's small cases (run the ledger on a toy instance before trusting it), P10's pigeonhole (a parity double count is pigeonhole modulo 2 — the pairs vanish and the boundary survives), and P66's proof habits (the equating step is a proof skeleton: define \(I\), count both ways, conclude).

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar A — the committee with a chair (combinatorics). The claim: for integers \(1 \le k \le n\), \(k\binom{n}{k} = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}\), and summing over \(k\), \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} k\binom{n}{k} = n\,2^{n-1}\). Name the incidence set: \(I\) is the set of pairs \((S, x)\) where \(S\) is a \(k\)-person committee from \(n\) people and \(x \in S\) is its chair — a "pointed committee". Count by committees: \(\binom{n}{k}\) committees, each with \(k\) choices of chair, so \(|I| = k\binom{n}{k}\). Count by chairs: \(n\) choices for \(x\), then \(\binom{n-1}{k-1}\) ways to fill the remaining \(k-1\) seats from the other \(n-1\) people, so \(|I| = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}\). Equate: the identity, no factorials expanded. The sum identity comes free — count pointed committees of any size: by chairs, \(n\) people each joined by any subset of the other \(n-1\), giving \(n\,2^{n-1}\); by committees, \(\sum_k k\binom{n}{k}\). The ledger's asymmetry did the work: "committee-side" was the question, "chair-side" was the answer. Check the toy: \(n = 4, k = 2\) gives \(2 \times 6 = 12 = 4 \times 3\) — both readings agree by hand. Moves used: P70 (the pointed-committee ledger), P02 (the \(n = 4\) toy check).

Exemplar B — the divisor ledger (number theory). Let \(\tau(m)\) be the number of divisors of \(m\) — an erratic function: \(\tau(p) = 2\) at primes, \(\tau(12) = 6\). The claim: for every \(N \ge 1\), \(\sum_{m=1}^{N} \tau(m) = \sum_{d=1}^{N} \left\lfloor N/d \right\rfloor\), and the average of \(\tau\) over \(1, \ldots, N\) is \(\ln N + O(1)\). Name the incidence set: \(I = \{(d, m) : 1 \le d, m \le N,\ d \text{ divides } m\}\). By columns (fixed \(m\)): the \(1\)s sit at the divisors of \(m\), so column \(m\) sums to \(\tau(m)\). By rows (fixed \(d\)): the \(1\)s sit at the multiples \(d, 2d, 3d, \ldots\) up to \(N\), and there are exactly \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\) of them. Equate: the identity. The jagged side became smooth: \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\) is within \(1\) of \(N/d\), so \(\sum_{d} \lfloor N/d \rfloor = N \sum_{d} \tfrac{1}{d} + O(N) = N \ln N + O(N)\), and dividing by \(N\): the average number of divisors up to \(N\) grows like \(\ln N\). Nothing about individual \(\tau(m)\) was ever understood — the ledger went around it. Moves used: P70 (the divisibility ledger), P02 (the \(N = 6\) toy), P15 (the squeeze that turns \(\lfloor N/d\rfloor\) into \(N/d + O(1)\)).

Exemplar C — cherries and the \(C_4\) cap (extremal graph theory). A bipartite graph has its vertices split into a left part (say \(m\) vertices) and a right part (\(n\) vertices), every edge joining left to right. Call it \(C_4\)-free if it contains no four-cycle — equivalently, no two left vertices share two common right neighbors. The claim: a \(C_4\)-free bipartite graph with \(m = n\) vertices on each side has at most \(\tfrac{n}{2}(1 + \sqrt{4n - 3})\) edges — about \(n^{3/2}\), far less than the \(n^2\) a dense bipartite graph could carry. Name the incidence set: \(I\) is the set of cherries — triples \((u, r, v)\) with \(u, v\) distinct left vertices, \(r\) a right vertex joined to both. Count by middles: right vertex \(r\) of degree \(d_r\) carries \(\binom{d_r}{2}\) cherries, so \(|I| = \sum_{r} \binom{d_r}{2}\). Count by ends: each pair of left vertices carries at most one cherry — two cherries on the same pair would be two common neighbors, a four-cycle. So \(|I| \le \binom{m}{2}\): the \(C_4\)-free hypothesis is a fence (P71 in disguise — the forbidden configuration caps the ledger). Now the squeeze: since \(x(x-1)\) is convex, \(\sum_r \binom{d_r}{2} \ge n \binom{e/n}{2}\), where \(e\) is the number of edges and \(e/n\) the average right-side degree. Chain it: \(n \cdot \tfrac{(e/n)(e/n - 1)}{2} \le \tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}\); with \(m = n\), solve the quadratic for \(e\): \(e \le \tfrac{n}{2}(1 + \sqrt{4n-3})\). The extremal-graph theorem was proved by a counting identity plus one convexity line — no structure of the graph was ever inspected. Moves used: P70 (the cherry ledger), P71 (the C4-free fence: one cherry per pair), P15 (convexity squeezes the sum below \(n\) times its average).

5 · When the trick breaks

The two routes must count the same set — boundary is not interior. Triangulate a convex \(k\)-gon: \(T\) triangles, some edges interior (each bordering two triangles), some on the boundary (each bordering one). Count pairs (triangle, side-of-triangle): by triangles, \(3T\); by edges, \(2E_{\text{int}} + k\) — and the careless \(3T = 2E\) is simply false. The ledger never lies, but a column that secretly comes in two kinds must be split before equating: \(3T = 2E_{\text{int}} + k\). The repair is the move itself, applied more carefully — and the split often hands you the invariant you were after (this identity, with \(T = 2I + k - 2\) from Exercise 2, is the door to Euler's formula).

Exact means exact: the floor is not decoration. The divisor identity is \(\sum \tau(m) = \sum \lfloor N/d \rfloor\), not \(\sum \tau(m) = \sum N/d\): at \(N = 6\) the true total is \(14\), while \(\sum_{d=1}^{6} 6/d = 6 + 3 + 2 + 1.5 + 1.2 + 1 = 14.7\). Replacing the floor by its round neighbor trades an identity for an estimate — sometimes the right trade (P15's squeeze made Exemplar B's average work), but it forfeits everything the exactness was buying, parity uses above all. Before rounding a ledger, ask which downstream step consumes the exact equality.

Infinite ledgers can disagree with themselves. Double counting, as stated, is a finite-family theorem: finitely many \(1\)s have one total. Push to infinite arrays and the two readings can diverge. Let \(a_{ij} = 1\) if \(j = i\), \(a_{ij} = -1\) if \(j = i - 1\), and \(0\) otherwise. Each row has finitely many nonzero entries and sums to \(1\) (row \(1\)) or \(0\) (every other row): total by rows, \(1\). Each column also has finitely many nonzero entries and sums to \(0\): total by columns, \(0\). Both iterated sums converge; they disagree. (No finite truncation of the array shows it — truncate to \(N \times N\) and both readings equal the sum of all entries, \(1\); the disagreement lives at infinity, where no "last column" repairs the balance.) The license for swapping the order of summation — nonnegativity, or absolute convergence, the content of the Fubini–Tonelli theorems — is the infinite-ledger repair, and it is a hypothesis, not a courtesy.

An identity is not an evaluation. \(\sum_{m \le N} \tau(m) = \sum_{d \le N} \lfloor N/d \rfloor\) names one number twice; it does not say what the number is. Extracting the asymptotics took a squeeze; extracting the sharp constant \((2\gamma - 1)N\) in the error term took Dirichlet's hyperbola method, which splits the ledger along the diagonal \(d = \sqrt{N}\) and treats the two halves differently. The ledger converts structure into an equation; solving the equation is another move, and the ledger will not do it for you.

Honest scope: double counting converts a two-family incidence structure into one exact equation, \(\sum_a r(a) = \sum_b c(b)\). It requires both routes to count the same set, it is a finite-family theorem unless a convergence license is re-earned, and it equates without evaluating — the hard side is bought at the price of understanding the easy side. When the ledger stalls, the audit order is: is the incidence set named? are the columns honest (no hidden boundary)? and is exactness actually needed?

6 · What to try next

The ledger's nearest sibling is P76 · Inclusion–exclusion, which counts unions and intersections of sets — the same two-families bookkeeping with subtraction added, and the natural next tool for "how many in at least one" questions. The parity variant braids with P10 · Pigeonhole: Exercise 5's door argument is the pigeonhole principle run modulo 2, and any existence claim that survives only as "an odd number of them" is that braid. The extremal braiding appeared in Exemplar C: when a forbidden configuration caps how many incidences a column may carry, the ledger plus the fence (P71) bounds whole families of graphs — modern extremal combinatorics is largely this duet. And the difference-side of the divisor staircase is P69 · Telescoping territory: summing \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\) by hyperbola is a differencing argument in ledger clothing. The default: whenever two families meet, spend one minute writing down the ledger's two readings — the minute pays for itself in identities, and occasionally in impossibilities.

7 · Lineage interlude

The move is older than its name. Leonhard Euler's 1736 paper on the Königsberg bridges — the document that laid the foundations of graph theory — counted crossings the ledger's way: each bridge contributes to the crossing-count of two landmasses at once, so the landmass totals must sum to twice the number of bridges, and Euler even stated the parity corollary this page's encounter runs: the number of landmasses touching an odd number of bridges is necessarily even. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet introduced the hyperbola method in 1849, splitting the divisor ledger along the diagonal \(d = \sqrt{N}\) to turn Exemplar B's identity into the sharp estimate \(D(x) = x\ln x + (2\gamma - 1)x + O(\sqrt{x})\) — and the question of shrinking that error term, the Dirichlet divisor problem, is open to this day. Guido Fubini (1907) and Leonida Tonelli (1909) proved the theorems that license swapping the order of integration — absolute integrability for Fubini, nonnegativity for Tonelli — the infinite ledger's repair. Emanuel Sperner's 1928 lemma used parity double counting across simplex faces to prove a result equivalent to Brouwer's fixed-point theorem (Exercise 5 runs its two-dimensional case), and in 1954 Kővári, Sós, and Turán turned cherry counting into the founding bound of extremal graph theory — Exemplar C's argument, at full power. The name "double counting" is vernacular; the technique itself is one of the few moves that is simultaneously elementary, ubiquitous, and still producing new mathematics.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○

(a) In any finite graph, prove that the number of vertices of odd degree is even. (b) A bipartite graph has left part \(L\) and right part \(R\), and every vertex — left or right — has degree exactly \(d \ge 1\). Prove \(|L| = |R|\). (c) Nine guests each shake exactly three hands: use (a) to say in one sentence why the party is impossible.

Hint

Count incidences (vertex, edge) two ways. For (a): the even-degree vertices contribute an even amount, so the odd-degree vertices must too. For (b): both readings of the edge count are \(d\) times a part size.

Worked answer

(a) \(\sum_{v} \deg(v) = 2|E|\) — the incidence ledger of the dinner party, in general form. Split: \(\sum_{\text{odd}} \deg(v) = 2|E| - \sum_{\text{even}} \deg(v)\), and the right side is even; a sum of odd terms is odd or even according as their number is, so the number of odd-degree vertices is even. (b) Count edges by left endpoints: \(|E| = \sum_{u \in L} \deg(u) = d\,|L|\); by right endpoints: \(|E| = d\,|R|\). So \(d\,|L| = d\,|R|\), and \(d \ge 1\) lets you cancel: \(|L| = |R|\). (c) The party would have nine odd-degree vertices — odd in number, violating (a). (Equivalently: \(9 \times 3 = 27\) incidences cannot equal \(2|E|\).)

Exercise 2 ●●○

A polygon with \(B\) boundary vertices (and \(B\) boundary edges) contains \(I\) interior points. It is partitioned into \(T\) triangles whose vertices are exactly the \(B + I\) points, with no point lying in the interior of a triangle or an edge. (a) Count the triangles' angles two ways — by triangles, and around the vertices — to prove \(T = 2I + B - 2\). (b) Count (triangle, side) pairs, splitting interior from boundary edges, to prove the number of interior edges is \(E_{\text{int}} = 3I + B - 3\). (c) Check both on the simplest case: no interior points, \(B = 3\).

Hint

For (a): each triangle carries \(\pi\); the angles around an interior vertex fill \(2\pi\), and the angles at boundary vertices fill the polygon's interior angles, \((B-2)\pi\) in total. For (b): \(3T = 2E_{\text{int}} + B\) — §5's boundary split.

Worked answer

(a) By triangles: the angle total is \(\pi T\). By vertices: each of the \(I\) interior points is surrounded by a full \(2\pi\); the boundary vertices' angles partition the polygon's interior angles, summing to \((B - 2)\pi\). So \(\pi T = 2\pi I + (B-2)\pi\), giving \(T = 2I + B - 2\). (b) By triangles: \(3T\) pairs. By edges: each interior edge borders \(2\) triangles, each boundary edge \(1\), so \(3T = 2E_{\text{int}} + B\). Substitute (a): \(2E_{\text{int}} + B = 3(2I + B - 2) = 6I + 3B - 6\), so \(E_{\text{int}} = 3I + B - 3\). (c) \(I = 0, B = 3\): a single triangle, \(T = 1\) ✓ and \(E_{\text{int}} = 0\) ✓ — the formulas' boundary case, and the ledger's toy check.

Exercise 3 ●●●

(a) Prove Gauss's identity: for every \(m \ge 1\), \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\), where \(\varphi(d)\) counts the integers in \(1, \ldots, d\) coprime to \(d\). (Route: reduce each fraction \(\tfrac{1}{m}, \tfrac{2}{m}, \ldots, \tfrac{m}{m}\) to lowest terms and sort the results by denominator.) (b) Weight the divisor ledger of Exemplar B: put \(\varphi(d)\) in cell \((d, m)\) when \(d \mid m\), and \(0\) otherwise. Double count the table's total to prove \(\sum_{d=1}^{N} \varphi(d) \lfloor N/d \rfloor = \tfrac{N(N+1)}{2}\). (c) Verify (b) at \(N = 6\).

Hint

For (a): \(\tfrac{k}{m}\) reduces to \(\tfrac{a}{d}\) with \(d = m / \gcd(k, m)\); for each divisor \(d\) of \(m\), exactly \(\varphi(d)\) numerators \(a\) appear. For (b): columns sum to \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d)\), which (a) evaluates; rows sum to \(\varphi(d) \lfloor N/d \rfloor\).

Worked answer

(a) Each \(\tfrac{k}{m}\) reduces uniquely to \(\tfrac{a}{d}\) in lowest terms, and \(d \mid m\) (write \(k = a \cdot \gcd(k,m)\), \(m = d \cdot \gcd(k,m)\)). Conversely each coprime pair \(a \le d\), \(d \mid m\), comes from exactly one \(\tfrac{k}{m}\), namely \(k = a(m/d)\). Sorting the \(m\) fractions by reduced denominator: denominator \(d\) receives \(\varphi(d)\) of them, and all \(m\) are sorted, so \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\). (b) The weighted table's total: by columns, column \(m\) sums to \(\sum_{d \mid m} \varphi(d) = m\) by (a), so the total is \(\sum_{m=1}^{N} m = \tfrac{N(N+1)}{2}\). By rows, row \(d\) has \(\varphi(d)\) sitting at each of the \(\lfloor N/d \rfloor\) multiples of \(d\), contributing \(\varphi(d)\lfloor N/d \rfloor\). Equate. (c) \(N = 6\): \(\varphi = 1, 1, 2, 2, 4, 2\) against \(\lfloor 6/d \rfloor = 6, 3, 2, 1, 1, 1\): \(6 + 3 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 = 21 = \tfrac{6 \cdot 7}{2}\) ✓.

Exercise 4 ◆

A student proposes a bipartite messaging network: 50 servers on the left, 50 clients on the right, 500 connections, engineered so that no two servers share two common clients (the network's protocol forbids it — a four-cycle would create a routing ambiguity). Without inspecting the protocol's implementation, decide whether such a network can exist, and if not, give the largest number of connections the protocol permits.

Worked answer

The network is a \(C_4\)-free bipartite graph with \(m = n = 50\), and Exemplar C's cherry ledger applies: \(|I| \le \binom{50}{2} = 1225\) by the end-pair reading, while the middle reading gives \(|I| = \sum_{r} \binom{d_r}{2} \ge 50\,\binom{e/50}{2}\) by convexity. So \(50 \cdot \tfrac{(e/50)(e/50 - 1)}{2} \le 1225\), i.e. \(e(e - 50) \le 50 \cdot 2450 = 122500\). Solving: \(e \le \tfrac{50 + \sqrt{2500 + 490000}}{2} = 25(1 + \sqrt{197}) \approx 25 \times 15.036 \approx 375.9\), so \(e \le 375\). The proposed \(500\) connections are impossible — the protocol's own rule caps the network at \(375\), about \(25\%\) under the proposal, and no implementation audit is needed: the ledger and the forbidden four-cycle suffice.

Exercise 5 ◐

(a) One-dimensional Sperner: the points \(v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_m\) are arranged on a segment in order, each colored \(1\) or \(2\), with \(v_0\) colored \(1\) and \(v_m\) colored \(2\). Call subsegment \(I_j = [v_{j-1}, v_j]\) bichromatic if its endpoints differ. Prove the number of bichromatic subsegments is odd — in particular, at least one exists. (b) Two-dimensional doors: a large triangle has its corners colored \(1, 2, 3\); a triangulation's vertices are each colored \(1, 2, 3\) arbitrarily. Call a small triangle panchromatic if its three vertices carry all three colors, and an edge a \(12\)-door if its endpoints are colored \(1\) and \(2\). Prove: if the big triangle's boundary contains an odd number of \(12\)-doors, the triangulation contains an odd number of panchromatic triangles.

Hint

For (a): count pairs (vertex colored \(1\), subsegment it touches) two ways — by subsegments, a \(11\)-segment carries \(2\), a \(12\)-segment carries \(1\), a \(22\)-segment carries \(0\); by vertices, interior color-1 vertices touch \(2\) segments and \(v_0\) touches \(1\). For (b): a panchromatic triangle has exactly \(1\) door; a triangle colored \(\{1,1,2\}\) or \(\{1,2,2\}\) has \(2\); all others have \(0\) — count (triangle, door) incidences mod 2, and note interior doors belong to two triangles.

Worked answer

(a) Let \(N\) be the number of pairs \((v, I_j)\) with \(v\) an endpoint of \(I_j\) colored \(1\). By subsegments: a \(11\)-segment contributes \(2\), a \(12\)-segment \(1\), a \(22\)-segment \(0\) — so \(N \equiv (\text{number of } 12\text{-segments}) \pmod 2\). By vertices: an interior color-1 vertex ends two subsegments (\(2 \equiv 0\)), \(v_m\) is colored \(2\), and \(v_0\) is colored \(1\) and ends one segment — so \(N \equiv 1 \pmod 2\). The number of bichromatic subsegments is odd. (b) Let \(N\) be the number of (triangle, \(12\)-door on its boundary) incidences. By triangles: panchromatic contributes \(1\), \(\{1,1,2\}\) or \(\{1,2,2\}\) contributes \(2 \equiv 0\), others \(0\) — so \(N \equiv \#\text{panchromatic} \pmod 2\). By doors: an interior \(12\)-door borders \(2\) triangles (\(\equiv 0\)), a boundary door borders \(1\). So \(\#\text{panchromatic} \equiv \#\text{boundary } 12\text{-doors} \pmod 2\), which is odd by hypothesis (the bottom side, between corners \(1\) and \(2\), is itself such a boundary by part (a) applied to that side's vertices). Odd means at least one: every such triangulation contains a panchromatic triangle — Sperner's lemma in two dimensions, proved entirely by parity bookkeeping.
