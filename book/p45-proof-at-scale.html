The Patterns Atlas · P45 · the fourth move of tier 4

Proof at scale, together

When a proof is too big for any one career — a thousand pages, a hundred authors, a generation of referees — do not scale the author: split the theorem into parts, freeze the interfaces (the exact lemma statements every part may cite, verbatim, fixed before the parts are written), and audit the assembly seam by seam, so that the finished proof belongs to the community's checking rather than to anyone's memory.

New word: interface. In this page's world an interface is a frozen, public, exact statement of what crosses between parts — the ledger of lemma statements a proof's pieces may cite, word for word, and nothing else. The word matters because of what it forbids: no part may cite "what Part B means", "the spirit of Lemma S", or its own paraphrase of a shared fact. An assembly is what you get when the parts, each carrying its certificate against the ledger, are checked and stitched together; an audit is the second pass in which someone who did not write a part re-checks it, seam by seam. Split, freeze, audit: three verbs, and the whole pattern is the discipline of doing them in that order.

1 · Before we begin

P44 ended with a door and a promise. The kernel made checking cheap: a proof written line by line can be refereed by a program small enough to read, and the verdict does not care whether its author is a genius, a committee, or a machine. But the prover — the part that finds the proof — stayed human, and human at one-per-person: one odd-order theorem took fifteen people six years. This page walks through that door. When the finding itself is too large for one head — when the certificate is 150,000 lines and the search behind it was a decade — the prover must become a team, and the pattern asks what a proof even is when no one person has read all of it.

Two older habits come due at the same moment. P25 taught that a big census must be audited by a second, independent engine; here the census is so large that the engines must be people, and the audit obligation is written into the architecture rather than performed afterwards. And P01 taught that a problem must be pinned — statement, definitions, what counts as an answer — before it is touched; here the pinning is the load-bearing wall, because the pinned lemma statements are the contract that lets strangers finish one another's work. P35's two worlds get a nod too: independence there meant two models refusing to agree; independence here means two auditors with no shared author.

The tension ahead: a proof is normally one mind's artifact — it can be read, held, and signed by a person. The classification of finite simple groups is tens of thousands of journal pages by about a hundred authors, published over half a century; no person alive has read it all, and yet it is among the most used theorems in mathematics. The question this page resolves is not can such a proof be finished? — history says yes — but what does its correctness rest on when no single mind can hold it? The answer is an architecture: parts, interfaces, audit. It is worth a whole pattern.

2 · Meet the problem

A theorem lands on a four-person team: no non-abelian finite simple group has order less than \(60\). It is a real theorem — the smallest non-abelian simple group is \(A_5\), of order exactly \(60\) — but at hand scale: the orders to dispatch run from \(1\) to \(59\), and there are only \(41\) composite numbers among them. The team splits the range. Part A takes orders up to \(15\), Part B up to \(30\), Part C up to \(45\), Part D up to \(59\). You own Part C.

First, before anyone writes a certificate, the team freezes its ledger — three shared lemmas, stated once, exactly, and never paraphrased:

  • Lemma S (Sylow forcing). Let \(n = p^a m\) with \(p\) prime, \(p \nmid m\), and \(m > 1\). In any group of order \(n\), the number \(n_p\) of Sylow \(p\)-subgroups divides \(m\) and satisfies \(n_p \equiv 1 \pmod{p}\). If the only divisor of \(m\) that is congruent to \(1\) mod \(p\) is \(1\) itself, then \(n_p = 1\): the Sylow \(p\)-subgroup is unique, hence normal — a proper, non-trivial normal subgroup — so the group is not simple.
  • Lemma C (element counting). Let \(n\) have two distinct prime divisors \(p\) and \(q\), each appearing to the first power. Distinct Sylow \(p\)-subgroups of order \(p\) share only the identity, so \(n_p\) of them contribute \(n_p(p-1)\) distinct elements of order \(p\) (likewise \(q\)). If even the largest admissible counts \(n_p\) and \(n_q\) allowed by Lemma S's arithmetic give \(n_p(p-1) + n_q(q-1) + 1 > n\), the two families cannot both be maximal: at least one of \(n_p, n_q\) equals \(1\), giving a normal Sylow subgroup — not simple.
  • Lemma Z (prime powers). Every group of order \(p^a\) with \(a \geq 2\) has a non-trivial center; a subgroup of order \(p\) inside the center is normal; not simple.

The lemmas are standard — Sylow's theorems and the class equation, any graduate algebra text — and that is the point: the ledger's job is exactness, not novelty. What the team agrees to is the discipline around it: every part certifies each of its orders by citing a ledger lemma, verbatim, with the arithmetic spelled out; no part may cite another part; no part may restate a lemma in its own words.

Referee it — not with taste, with a clerk's eye. Each row makes exactly one claim: that the divisors listed are the divisors, and that the congruences hold. Row by row: \(33\): divisors of \(3\) are \(1, 3\), and \(3 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{11}\) — pass. \(34\): \(2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{17}\) — pass. \(35\): \(5 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{7}\) — pass. \(38, 39\): pass. \(40\): divisors of \(8\) are \(1, 2, 4, 8\), and mod \(5\) these read \(1, 2, 4, 3\) — only \(1\) — pass. \(42\): divisors of \(6\) mod \(7\) read \(1, 2, 3, 6\) — pass. \(44\): \(2, 4 \not\equiv 1 \pmod{11}\) — pass. \(45\): divisors of \(9\) mod \(5\) read \(1, 3, 4\) — pass. \(32\): prime power, Lemma Z's territory — pass. Ten rows, ten passes, under a minute of real checking each.

And row \(36\). The certificate lists the divisors of \(4\) — \(1, 2, 4\), correct — and claims only \(1\) is congruent to \(1\) mod \(3\). Check it: \(4 = 3 \cdot 1 + 1\). Four is congruent to \(1\) mod \(3\). The certificate's central claim is false, the hypothesis of Lemma S is not met, and the conclusion — \(n_3 = 1\), a normal Sylow subgroup, not simple — is unearned. The certificate is dead, and you did not need to know any group theory to kill it: one division. You report the seam: Part C, order \(36\): Lemma S's divisor check fails — \(4 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\). The owner of the row replies, honestly, that \(36\) is one of the stubborn orders — she will need an argument beyond the ledger — and the assembly notes it as open, not closed.

You have just refereed one part of a split proof, and the strange thing is how little you did. You never verified \(41\) orders. You never proved Sylow's theorems. You checked eleven rows of arithmetic against a frozen list, in about an hour, and your verdict is worth having precisely because it required nothing beyond the ledger and division. Three questions, and the page is their answer. (1) Multiply this by four parts and a thousand rows: what exactly does such a referee check — the parts, or the interfaces between them? (2) What breaks if Part C is allowed to cite "what Part B showed", or to restate Lemma S in words it prefers? (3) When all parts pass and someone signs the whole assembly — who audits the ledger itself?

3 · Name the move

Pattern P45 — Proof at scale, together (split, interface, audit). When a claim is too large for one career to prove or one referee to check — ten thousand pages, a hundred authors, a generation — (1) split the theorem into parts: decompose the claim into pieces, each independently provable, each small enough for one competent owner to prove and one other competent owner to check; (2) freeze the interfaces first: before any part is written, fix the exact statements every part may cite — the lemma ledger — so a part can be checked without reading any other part, and a citation is a verbatim string from the ledger, never a paraphrase, never another part's output (P01's pinning at team scale: the contract is the load-bearing wall; P44's certificate, one per part); (3) audit the assembly: every part's certificate re-checked by someone who did not write it, every seam recomputed by an independent engine, the whole audit re-runnable by anyone from the parts and the ledger alone — so that the finished proof's authority rests on the community's checking, not on any single memory that holds it.

Or, in words: do not scale the author — scale the contract. Write the shared statements so exactly, and the parts' duties so explicitly, that strangers can finish one another's proofs. The signature on the whole then means only this: the parts passed, the seams were audited, and anyone can re-run the audit. That is what a team's signature can honestly mean; nothing more is available at scale anyway.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the claim outgrows one lifetime's attention. Tens of thousands of pages; a hundred authors; a proof whose reading is itself a career. When no single referee can exist, the human eye stops being the instrument of record. Jam-mode: the proof fits in one head with room to spare — then do not split. The ledger drafting, the cross-auditing, the seam-keeping are pure overhead (Exhibit C prices them), and a proof one person can hold is better held.

Key 2 — the theorem has natural seams. The classification splits family by family; the census splits order by order; the great formalizations split definition by definition. The parts must be independently provable and independently checkable — that is what makes the split an architecture rather than a folder structure. Jam-mode: the parts interlock densely — every part depends on every other's details, and the "parts" are chapters of one argument. Then the interface graph is a hairball, the audit cannot be distributed, and the proof must be owned whole or reorganized until real seams appear.

Key 3 — the interfaces can be frozen exactly. Lemma statements a stranger can check by arithmetic: divisor lists, congruences, counts — the ledger of §2. This is the structural requirement, and it is strict: the interface must be checkable by someone who has read only the interface. Jam-mode: the shared understanding is a style, a philosophy, an idiom — "the readers of Part B will know what we mean" — and no such interface can be audited. Then the pattern fails at the contract layer, before any part is written, and no amount of good will or hard work repairs it (Exhibit B is exactly this failure, at full scale).

Box P45.A — the machine, in three obligations. (1) The ledger. The frozen lemma statements, public before the parts are written, and versioned if they must change — a part may cite only ledger strings, verbatim. This is P44's discipline at team scale: the ledger is the rulebook, each part's certificate is checked against it by a clerk, and the clerk can be a person or a program. (2) The parts. Each part carries four fields: an owner; a claim — exactly which cases it closes; a certificate — the arithmetic and the ledger citations; and an auditor who is not the owner. The claim field is what makes coverage checkable: the assembly can verify that the parts' claims add up to the whole theorem, no case left unowned. (3) The assembly pass. An independent engine recomputes every arithmetic claim, checks every citation against the ledger, checks that no part audits itself and no circle of parts audits one another, and re-runs the entire audit from the parts alone. P25's second engine, promoted from a virtue to the sign-off: the assembly is accepted when the audit re-runs cleanly — by anyone.

Box P45.B — where the move appears. The classification of the finite simple groups (1955–2004: about a hundred authors, tens of thousands of pages, 18 infinite families and 26 sporadic groups) is the pattern's monument. The odd-order theorem — fifteen people, six years, about 150,000 lines, P44's receipts at team scale. The Polymath experiments (2009 on: a proof conducted in public, the statement frozen first, the threads as parts). The community verification of Perelman's sketches (three independent teams filling the details). The Flyspeck project's two-system certificate for Kepler (2014). Below mathematics: the Linux kernel's per-part sign-offs, the genome project's per-chromosome assemblies, a physics collaboration's per-subdetector papers. In every case the same three verbs: split, freeze, audit — and the authority of the whole resting on re-runnable checks rather than on anyone's comprehension of all of it.

4 · The move at work — three encounters

Three encounters, at three scales. First the assembly of §2 is built and run as a machine — parts, ledger, audit, refusals — on the full census of orders up to \(59\), and the census's boundary turns out to be the theorem itself. Then a real collaboration — the Polymath project — is read through the same three obligations, with a census of lines run by this page's own machines. Finally the industrial scale: the classification of the finite simple groups, the largest proof ever assembled, audited by nothing smaller than a community.

Example 1 · The miniature census, built and audited

Moves used: P01 the ledger as pinned contract · P25 the audit as a second engine · P44 the certificate, one per part.

Run the whole census. Every composite order from \(4\) to \(59\) — there are \(41\) of them — classified by the ledger alone: for each, try every prime \(p\) in the factorization for Lemma S; if none forces, try Lemma C on the first-power prime pairs; prime powers go to Lemma Z. The machine's census: 27 orders fall to Lemma S (from \(6 = 2 \cdot 3\), where \(n_3\) divides \(2\) and \(2 \not\equiv 1 \pmod 3\), so the Sylow \(3\)-subgroup is forced normal, up through \(58 = 2 \cdot 29\)); one order falls to Lemma C — \(30\), whose certificate is the ledger's little masterpiece: \(n_3\) may only be \(1\) or \(10\), \(n_5\) only \(1\) or \(6\), and if both families were non-forced the group would need \(10 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 4 + 1 = 45\) elements but has only \(30\) — so one of them is normal, and the group is not simple; 8 prime powers fall to Lemma Z (\(4, 8, 9, 16, 25, 27, 32, 49\)); and five orders resist the entire ledger: \(12, 24, 36, 48, 56\). Thirty-six closed by the frozen interface, five named honestly as open — the census does not pretend.

Now build the assembly machine, with the ledger as data. The parts are rows: each composite order comes with its owner, its claim, its certificate — the factorization, the lemma cited, the divisor list, the congruence verdict — and an auditor from another part. The assembler recomputes every row's arithmetic with its own engine, checks every citation is a ledger string, verbatim, checks that no row audits itself, and checks the audit assignments form no circle. Then the refusal battery — five poisoned assemblies, each broken in exactly one place, each refused at the correct seam:

The honest assembly ACCEPTs only after every row passes its recomputation, every citation matches the ledger, and the five open orders are carried as open — and the census's output reads like a real collaboration's ledger should: \(36\) closed, \(5\) escalated, \(0\) silently assumed.

The escalation, carried out for one of the five, shows what "beyond the ledger" costs — and what it buys. Take order \(12\). Sylow's arithmetic gives \(n_3 \in \{1, 4\}\) — divisors of \(4\) that are \(1\) mod \(3\) are \(1\) and \(4\), exactly the poison of §2 — so Lemma S stays silent. If \(n_3 = 1\) the group is not simple. If \(n_3 = 4\), let the group act by conjugation on its four Sylow \(3\)-subgroups: a map into the \(24\) permutations of four objects, whose kernel is a normal subgroup. The kernel cannot be everything (the action moves the four subgroups transitively), so either the kernel is a proper non-trivial normal subgroup — not simple — or the kernel is trivial and the group sits inside the symmetric group \(S_4\) as a subgroup of order \(12\). And there is only one: a normal subgroup must be a union of conjugacy classes containing the identity, the classes of \(S_4\) have sizes \(1, 3, 6, 6, 8\), and the only union containing \(1\) that reaches \(12\) is \(1 + 3 + 8\) — the identity, the three double transpositions, and the eight \(3\)-cycles: the alternating group \(A_4\). One more step: \(A_4\) contains the four-element set \(V = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}\) — closed under multiplication, and closed under conjugation by every one of \(A_4\)'s twelve elements, both checked row by row — a proper non-trivial normal subgroup. So every group of order \(12\) fails to be simple, and the escalated certificate cites the class sizes, the unique union, and the normality check as its receipts. The other four resisters — \(24, 36, 48, 56\) — yield to arguments of the same species (bigger actions, more counting) that this page honestly declines to build: they are named, deferred, and imported from the standard theory, exactly as a real assembly would import its escalations.

And now the census's boundary, which is the most beautiful receipt on the page. The theorem says the smallest non-abelian simple group has order \(60\). Run the ledger on \(60\) itself: \(60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\); for Lemma S, \(n_5\) may be \(1\) or \(6\) (six divides \(12\) and \(6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\)), \(n_3\) may be \(1, 4\) or \(10\), \(n_2\) is unconstrained among odd divisors of \(15\) — no forcing anywhere. Lemma C, on the first-power pair \(3, 5\): worst case \(10 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 4 + 1 = 45 \leq 60\) — no contradiction. Order \(60\) resists the ledger exactly as an actually-simple group must. It is not silence; it is the theorem whispering. The ledger closes every composite below \(60\) (given the four imported escalations) and refuses exactly at \(60\), where \(A_5\) lives — and \(A_5\)'s own simplicity is checkable at census scale: its sixty elements are the even permutations of five letters; brute conjugation sorts them into classes of sizes \(1, 12, 12, 15, 20\); a normal subgroup must be a union of classes containing the identity with size dividing \(60\), and no such union except the trivial two exists — checked over all of them. The miniature ends precisely where the real theorem begins: the smallest order the interface cannot close is the smallest order that genuinely hosts a simple group.

Example 2 · The collaboration as a proof engine

Moves used: P01 the statement frozen before the threads · P25 the census, split across a crowd · P44 the thread as certificate.

In January 2009, Gowers asked on his blog whether mathematics could be done massively collaboratively — not a team of five with a leader, but an open thread where anyone could contribute a lemma, a counterexample, a computation. The first experiment, Polymath1, took on the density Hales–Jewett theorem, and the architecture is the pattern in miniature. The statement was frozen first: for every density \(\delta > 0\), every large-enough cube \([3]^n\) (the \(n\)-tuples over a three-letter alphabet) contains a combinatorial line — a set of three points varying together along some coordinates, the tic-tac-toe win. The parts were the threads: the combinatorial proof on one blog, the quantitative bounds on another. The ledger was the frozen statement plus the agreed meanings; the audit was the crowd itself — every contribution checked in public, by people who did not write it.

The census at the heart of the project is one this page's machines can run at the small end. A subset of \([3]^n\) is line-free if it contains no combinatorial line. For \(n = 1\): \(3\) points, \(1\) line, so a line-free set keeps \(2\) — density \(2/3\). For \(n = 2\): \(9\) points and \(7\) lines — three rows, three columns, and the one combinatorial diagonal (where both coordinates vary together); a line-free set keeps at most \(6\). The upper bound is the hitting-set bound: every line must be broken, a deleted point can lie on at most three lines, and two points cover at most six of the seven lines — so at least three deletions are needed, \(6\) survive; and the main diagonal's three points do break all seven, so \(6\) is achieved. For \(n = 3\): \(27\) points, \(37\) lines (27 templates varying one coordinate, 9 varying two, 1 varying three); the same search shows no \(8\) deletions can break all \(37\) lines, and an explicit \(9\)-set does — so the maximum is \(18\). Density \(2/3\) again: \(2\), \(6\), \(18\). At machine scale the collaboration's census found the next values — \(52\) for \(n = 4\), \(150\) for \(n = 5\), \(450\) for \(n = 6\) — and published the numbers as a paper of their own. The density \(2/3\) you see at \(n \leq 3\) is small-\(n\) luck; the theorem says the achievable density must decay toward zero as \(n\) grows, and the whole point of the collaboration was to prove that honestly, combinatorially, with the community watching every seam.

What the experiment showed about the pattern is worth the numbers. The proof worked because the statement was frozen before the threads opened — contributors argued about steps, never about what was being proved. It worked because the parts were genuinely separable: the combinatorial thread and the bounds thread shared the frozen statement and little else. And it was honest about its own sociology: the paper appeared in the Annals in 2012 under the pseudonym D. H. J. Polymath — the first major theorem with a proof that no single person wrote and a name that no single person owns. The audit trail is the public record itself: every dead end, every correction, every seam, still readable by anyone.

Example 3 · The industrial scale

Moves used: P25 the census that outgrew every single reader · P01 the statement as a multi-decade contract · P36 the Monster, met before through its characters.

The classification of the finite simple groups is the pattern at its largest. The ledger: every finite simple group is cyclic of prime order, in one of \(18\) infinite families, or one of \(26\) sporadic groups. The parts: several hundred journal articles by about a hundred authors, published mostly between 1955 and 2004, totalling tens of thousands of pages — each family a part, each sporadic group a part, each with owners and a certificate. The audit: a community, over half a century, re-checking seams — the seams between cases, the seams between papers, the seams between decades.

One receipt from the largest part. The Monster, the largest sporadic group, has order

\[2^{46} \cdot 3^{20} \cdot 5^{9} \cdot 7^{6} \cdot 11^{2} \cdot 13^{3} \cdot 17 \cdot 19 \cdot 23 \cdot 29 \cdot 31 \cdot 41 \cdot 47 \cdot 59 \cdot 71 \;=\; 808{,}017{,}424{,}794{,}512{,}875{,}886{,}459{,}904{,}961{,}710{,}757{,}005{,}754{,}368{,}000{,}000{,}000 \;\approx\; 8.08 \times 10^{53}\]

— fifty-four digits, exactly nine of them trailing zeros (the nine \(5\)'s pair with nine of the forty-six \(2\)'s), recomputed independently by two engines built for this page in different arithmetics and agreeing digit for digit. The factorization is the certificate; the digit string is the check. At this scale a receipt is not pedantry — it is the only form the evidence can take. Nobody "reads" the Monster the way one reads a quadratic formula; one verifies its interfaces.

Two more facts of the industrial scale complete the picture. First, the proof's authority does not rest on any single mind: no person alive has read the whole classification, and the community's response is not a shrug but an obligation — a second-generation rewrite, by Gorenstein, Lyons and Solomon, has been revising the parts into a single coherent ledger for decades, volume by volume, because the original's interfaces were exactly where the audit hurt. Second, the finished theorem became an interface itself: modern group theory cites "by the classification" as a single lemma, the way a programmer calls a library without reading it. The assembly's output is a one-line contract — and that is what winning looks like at this scale.

The same era produced the audit-at-scale counterweight: Perelman's 2002–2003 sketches of the Poincaré and geometrization conjectures — posted as terse preprints, no journal, no referees — were verified by the community over the following years: three independent teams (Kleiner–Lott, Cao–Zhu, Morgan–Tian) filled the details in parallel, cross-checked one another, and produced complete written proofs. The sketch was the interface; the community was the prover of the details; the audit was structural — independent teams, no shared author. That Perelman then declined the Fields Medal in 2006 and the million-dollar Millennium Prize in 2010 is a fact about the man; that the theorem stands is a fact about the architecture.

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits

Split, freeze, audit — the pattern is only as strong as its weakest obligation, and each obligation has a famous failure. Exhibit A: a part with no owner. Exhibit B: an interface that could not be frozen. Exhibit C: the price, paid in the currency of a lifetime's attention.

Exhibit A · The un-owned part

In 1983, Gorenstein — the classification's great organizer — announced that the proof was complete. It was not. One case, the quasithin groups, had been believed done on the strength of word-of-mouth: someone was thought to have it, someone else thought that someone had published it, and the part had never actually been written. The announcement was premature by two decades. The gap was closed only in 2004, by Aschbacher and Smith, in \(1{,}221\) pages — a single part of the assembly, larger than most complete proofs, written twenty-one years after the whole was declared finished.

The lesson is structural, not personal: an interface without an owner is a hole, and the audit must track ownership, not just validity. Part C's order-\(36\) certificate of §2 was wrong but owned — the owner was reachable, the row was reopened, the escalation was assigned. The quasithin case was not wrong; it was nobody's — and nobody's rows do not get repaired, because nobody's rows do not get audited. A real assembly pass checks a fourth field the §2 ledger kept implicit: every part names its owner, and coverage means every case has one. The classification's second generation (Fig P45.3) writes the lesson into the architecture: the rewrite is, in large part, an ownership-and-interface cleanup — making explicit who proved what, against which exact statements, in which order.

Exhibit B · The interface that could not be frozen

In August 2012, Mochizuki released four preprints — hundreds of pages of new machinery, inter-universal Teichmüller theory — claiming, among other consequences, a proof of the abc conjecture. The parts were written; the author was reachable; the good will on all sides was real. What failed was Key 3: the interface. Scholze and Stix, two of the strongest readers the theory ever attracted, visited Kyoto in March 2018 to identify a precise, checkable statement of the theory's key passage — the ledger entry the rest of the edifice cites. Their report concluded that the gap they found was "so severe" that "small modifications will not rescue the proof strategy." The two sides could not even agree on a pinned statement of the central corollary precise enough to be checked — and an interface that cannot be stated exactly cannot be audited at all.

Publication did not repair it: the papers appeared in 2021 in a journal edited at the author's own institution (the author recused), and the community's checking still did not transfer — the proof remains, for most of mathematics, not accepted. Contrast the counter-case from P44's lineage: the Flyspeck project's certificate for the Kepler conjecture was checked by two independent proof systems sharing one formal statement — the interface was frozen so exactly that even a machine could be the auditor. The diagnosis is the pattern's harshest clause: when Key 3 fails, the failure is not in any part. No amount of completeness, correctness, or sincerity inside the parts can rescue an assembly whose shared statement cannot be written down. P01's pinning is not a formality at the front of the collaboration; it is the collaboration's possibility condition.

Exhibit C · The price: overhead, and the single mind

Three prices, all payable in advance. First, coordination overhead: the ledger is real mathematics. Freezing a lemma statement exactly — so that strangers can cite it and clerks can check the citations — is design work of the same difficulty as proving, and the classification's second-generation rewrite has spent decades paying it: making the parts fit a shared, exact ledger is a project comparable to a major theorem's proof, run by senior mathematicians, volume after volume. Second, the loss of the single mind: no one alive has read the whole classification, and the proof's authority has permanently migrated from any person's understanding to the community's audit — which is exactly why the audit must be re-runnable by anyone, forever; the moment it cannot be re-run, the theorem's status decays from mathematics to sociology. Third, the frozen-interface risk: a ledger is a single point of failure. If a frozen lemma statement is subtly wrong, every part built on it inherits the flaw — and the audit, which checks parts against the ledger, will happily pass them all. The ◆ exercise below makes this failure concrete at hand scale, and the question it poses — who audits the ledger? — is the pattern's honest open seam.

Scope, honestly. What this page's own machines established, from scratch: the order census — all \(41\) composites up to \(59\) classified by the frozen ledger (27 by Sylow forcing, 8 as prime powers, order \(30\) by element counting at \(45 > 30\)), the five resisters named, every divisor list and congruence recomputed by two engines with no code in common; the order-\(12\) escalation's receipts — the class sizes of \(S_4\) as \(1, 3, 6, 6, 8\), the unique class union \(1 + 3 + 8\) reaching \(12\), the closure and normality of \(V\) under all of \(A_4\)'s conjugations; the \(A_5\) census — classes \(1, 12, 12, 15, 20\), no proper normal union, simplicity witnessed over all class-union combinations; the \([3]^n\) line-free census — \(2, 6, 18\) against \(1, 7, 37\) lines, with the \(n \leq 2\) cases enumerated exhaustively and the \(n = 3\) case closed by an explicit \(9\)-deletion witness against a search that no \(8\) deletions suffice; the Monster's order, fifty-four digits recomputed independently in two limb arithmetics. Borrowed on trust: the Sylow theorems and the class equation — the lemmas the ledger itself states (standard graduate algebra, cited, not re-proved); the escalations for orders \(24, 36, 48, 56\), named and deferred to the standard theory; the classification's, Polymath's, and Perelman's historical facts, from the published record with sources in the page log; and the density Hales–Jewett theorem's asymptotic claim itself — this page's census witnesses only \(n \leq 3\). The pattern's honest summary: the community audits the parts; the ledger is audited by independent re-derivation; upstream of everything, someone still chooses what to freeze.

6 · The doors this opens

Door one, the working door: the diagram chase — P47. The assembly of this page lives at the scale of teams and decades, but its discipline has a desk-scale twin every working mathematician already owns: the commutative diagram. Each square is a part with a tiny certificate — this way round equals that way round — and the chase is an assembly pass: step through the seams, each checked locally, the global conclusion arriving from the stitches. When the diagram fails, it fails at a named square, exactly the way Part C failed at order \(36\). The next move up the ladder makes the twinship formal.

Door two, the far door: transfer — P49. The ultras move builds a world that looks just like ours from the outside and differs underneath, then proves that truth moves across the seam. Read through this page's pattern, that is an interface theorem — the ultimate frozen ledger: a contract between mathematical worlds, stating exactly what crosses. The audits of this page answer to communities; Łoś's theorem answers to something stranger, and the door is worth opening later, slowly.

Door three, the widening door: the audit habit, everywhere. P25 demanded a second engine before you trust a count; P44 made the certificate the artifact; this page distributes both across people. The transferable question is now threefold, and it applies to anything long built by many hands — a census, a codebase, a paper, a proof, a policy: what are the parts, what is the ledger, and who audited the seams? Most work never reaches the scale where a formal ledger earns its overhead. But the question is free to ask, and its absence is diagnostic: a long collaborative artifact with no frozen interfaces and no cross-audit is not a proof, a codebase, or a census yet — it is a draft with a shared blind spot.

7 · Where the move came from

The pattern's prehistory is a single theorem that outgrew its referees. In 1963, Feit and Thompson's odd-order theorem — 255 journal pages — was the largest proof its field had seen, and the community learned, uncomfortably, that a proof can be longer than a referee's year. The classification followed, with the classification's own organizer: it was Gorenstein, in the 1970s, who saw that a proof of this size needed what this page calls a ledger — an explicit program, with named cases, named owners, and statements pinned before the parts were written. The 1983 announcement and the quasithin hole (Exhibit A) taught the community the fourth field — ownership — the hard way, and the 2004 completion stands as the pattern's first full-scale success: half a century, about a hundred authors, tens of thousands of pages, one theorem.

The collaboration experiments came next. In January 2009, Gowers asked, in public, whether massively collaborative mathematics was possible; Polymath1 answered over the following months — the density Hales–Jewett theorem, statement frozen first, threads as parts, the crowd as audit — with the combinatorial proof paper appearing in the Annals in 2012 under the collective name D. H. J. Polymath, and the census numbers (Example 2's \(2, 6, 18, 52, 150, 450\)) in a companion paper. The same era ran the audit-at-scale experiment in the opposite direction: Perelman's 2002–2003 sketches — minimal parts, no named owners, posted to a preprint server — were verified by three independent teams working in parallel, and the community, not any journal, functioned as the assembly pass.

The interface-failure lineage is shorter but just as instructive: the abc conjecture's claimed proof of 2012 (Exhibit B) is the modern era's cautionary tale — not a wrong part, but an unfreezable interface, unresolved as the years pass. And the machine lineage of P44 supplies the audit infrastructure that is now merging with this pattern: proof assistants read certificates; communities write ledgers; the two disciplines are converging on the same requirement from opposite sides — a statement exact enough that a stranger can check it — with the stranger increasingly being a kernel. The move is as old as mathematics done by more than one mind, and as new as a blog comment that closes a gap; the architecture is what took a century to see.

Exercise 1 ●○○ · Referee the assembly

Another team runs the census of §2 with the same frozen ledger, and you draw audit duty. Three of their rows:

orderfactorizationlemmathe certificate's arithmetic
\(52\)\(2^2 \cdot 13\)S, \(p = 13\)divisors of \(4\): \(1, 2, 4\); only \(1 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\)
\(48\)\(2^4 \cdot 3\)S, \(p = 3\)divisors of \(16\): \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16\); only \(1 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}\)
\(30\)\(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\)S, \(p = 5\)divisors of \(6\): \(1, 2, 3, 6\); only \(1 \equiv 1 \pmod{5}\)

(a) Referee each row the way §2 did: check the divisor list, check each congruence. (b) For each row that dies, say whether the ledger can still close the order by another route — and if so, write the honest certificate. (c) The team proposes to record row three as "closed". What should the assembly's ledger say instead, and why does the wording matter?

Hint

One row's claim is true and its conclusion earned; one row's claim is false with no rescue inside the ledger; one row's claim is false but another lemma of the frozen ledger closes the same order. Check the congruences before anything else — each is one division.

Worked answer

(a) \(52\): divisors of \(4\) are \(1, 2, 4\); mod \(13\) these read \(1, 2, 4\) — only \(1\) — pass, \(n_{13} = 1\), not simple. \(48\): divisors of \(16\) are \(1, 2, 4, 8, 16\); mod \(3\) these read \(1, 2, 1, 2, 1\) — both \(4\) and \(16\) are \(1\) mod \(3\), so the claim "only \(1\)" is false and the certificate dies at the seam: Lemma S hypothesis unmet. \(30\): divisors of \(6\) are \(1, 2, 3, 6\); mod \(5\) these read \(1, 2, 3, 1\) — six is congruent to \(1\) mod \(5\), so this certificate also dies. (b) Order \(48\): no rescue — \(p = 2\) leaves divisors \(1, 3\) of \(m = 3\), both odd hence both \(1\) mod \(2\), so no forcing; and Lemma C needs two first-power primes, but \(48 = 2^4 \cdot 3\) has only one. Order \(48\) is one of the five resisters: the honest ledger entry is open, escalated. Order \(30\): rescuable — Lemma C, with \(n_3 \in \{1, 10\}\) and \(n_5 \in \{1, 6\}\); if both were non-forced the count would be \(10 \cdot 2 + 6 \cdot 4 + 1 = 45 > 30\), so one family is normal: closed by Lemma C. (c) The assembly must not record order \(30\) as closed by Lemma S — the citation is false even though the conclusion (not simple) is true. The ledger entry names the lemma actually used, because the audit's value is exactly this: a future reader must be able to re-derive the closure from the citation alone. A certificate that cites the wrong lemma is a seam that will fail someone else's re-run, true conclusion or not.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · Build the \([3]^2\) census by hand

(a) List the seven combinatorial lines of \([3]^2\) — three rows, three columns, one diagonal — by template (\((*,0)\) through \((*,2)\), \((0,*)\) through \((2,*)\), and \((*,*)\)). (b) Prove the maximum line-free set has size \(6\): show that deleting any two points breaks at most five of the seven lines, so at least three deletions are forced; then exhibit three deletions that break all seven. (c) Verify your survivor set against every line, and give the density. Where does part (b) use the fact that only three points of the grid lie on three lines each?

Hint

For (b): count the lines through a single point — corners and center sit on row, column, and diagonal (three lines); the other six points sit on two. Two points on three lines each must both be diagonal points, and any two diagonal points share the diagonal. For the exhibit: the main diagonal's three points.

Worked answer

(a) Rows: \(\{(0,a),(1,a),(2,a)\}\) for \(a = 0, 1, 2\); columns: \(\{(b,0),(b,1),(b,2)\}\) for \(b = 0, 1, 2\); the combinatorial diagonal: \(\{(0,0),(1,1),(2,2)\}\) — seven in all, the diagonal being the only line where both coordinates vary together. (b) A point lies on at most three lines; the three-line points are exactly \(00, 11, 22\) (row + column + diagonal). Two deletions break at most \(3 + 3 = 6\) lines, and \(6\) only if the two points share no line — impossible for two three-line points, since any two diagonal points lie together on the diagonal, giving at most \(3 + 3 - 1 = 5\); a three-line point paired with a two-line point gives at most \(5\) (if no line joins them) and two two-line points give at most \(4\). So two deletions break at most \(5 < 7\) lines: three deletions are forced, and a line-free set keeps at most \(9 - 3 = 6\). The exhibit: delete \(\{00, 11, 22\}\). (c) The survivors are \(\{01, 02, 10, 12, 20, 21\}\); check each line: row \(0\) contains \(00\) (deleted), row \(1\) contains \(11\), row \(2\) contains \(22\); column \(0\) contains \(00\), column \(1\) contains \(11\), column \(2\) contains \(22\); the diagonal is entirely deleted. All seven broken, six survive — density \(6/9 = 2/3\). Part (b)'s sharp count is exactly the fact that only \(00, 11, 22\) are triple-line points: it is what rules out the \(3 + 3 = 6\) case. This is the \([3]^2\) row of Example 2's census — \(2, 6, 18\) — done by hand.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The stubborn five, honestly

(a) For each of \(12, 24, 36, 48, 56\), write the certificate that fails: which prime's divisor check dies in Lemma S, and why Lemma C cannot run at all. (b) Carry out the order-\(12\) escalation of Example 1 yourself: given \(n_3 = 4\), build the conjugation action on the four Sylow \(3\)-subgroups, derive the two cases for the kernel, and verify the three receipts — the class sizes of \(S_4\) (\(1, 3, 6, 6, 8\)), the unique class union \(1 + 3 + 8\) reaching \(12\), and the normality of \(V = \{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)\}\) in \(A_4\). (c) For \(24, 36, 48, 56\), name what would have to be built — and say precisely what this page's census claims about them, no more.

Hint

For (a): Lemma C requires two primes appearing to the first power — count how many first-power primes each of the five orders has. For (b): the action's kernel is normal; show it cannot be everything (the four Sylow subgroups are permuted transitively), then split on trivial or not. For the class union: a subgroup that is normal is a union of whole conjugacy classes and contains the identity. For (c): the honest verb is deferred, not proved.

Worked answer

(a) \(12 = 2^2 \cdot 3\): for \(p = 3\), divisors of \(4\) are \(1, 2, 4\) and \(4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\) — the forcing fails; for \(p = 2\), divisors of \(3\) are \(1, 3\), both \(1\) mod \(2\). \(24 = 2^3 \cdot 3\): divisors of \(8\) include \(4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\); divisors of \(3\) are both \(1\) mod \(2\). \(36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2\): divisors of \(4\) include \(4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3\); divisors of \(9\) are \(1, 3, 9\), all \(1\) mod \(2\). \(48 = 2^4 \cdot 3\): divisors of \(16\) include \(4\) and \(16\), both \(1\) mod \(3\); divisors of \(3\) both \(1\) mod \(2\). \(56 = 2^3 \cdot 7\): divisors of \(8\) are \(1, 2, 4, 8\) and \(8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7\); divisors of \(7\) both \(1\) mod \(2\). Lemma C cannot run on any of them: it needs two distinct first-power primes, and each of the five orders has at most one (\(12, 24, 48\) have only \(3\); \(56\) has only \(7\); \(36\) has none). (b) With \(n_3 = 4\), conjugation permutes the four Sylow \(3\)-subgroups, giving a homomorphism \(\varphi\) from the group into the \(24\) permutations of four objects. Conjugation is transitive on Sylow subgroups, so the action is non-trivial and the kernel is proper. If the kernel is non-trivial, it is a proper non-trivial normal subgroup: not simple. If the kernel is trivial, the group embeds as a subgroup of order \(12\) in \(S_4\) — and a normal subgroup of \(S_4\) is a union of classes containing the identity; the classes have sizes \(1, 3, 6, 6, 8\), and the only sub-collection containing \(1\) and summing to \(12\) is \(1 + 3 + 8\). (Any subgroup of index \(2\) is normal, so the embedding is a normal subgroup of \(S_4\).) That union is exactly \(A_4\): the identity, the three double transpositions, the eight \(3\)-cycles. So the group is \(A_4\) — and \(A_4\) has \(V\): closed under multiplication (each product of two distinct non-identity elements of \(V\) is the third), and normal, since conjugating any element of \(V\) by any of the twelve elements of \(A_4\) stays inside \(V\) — thirty-six conjugations, each a check. Not simple. (c) Orders \(24, 36, 48, 56\) yield to arguments of the same species — actions on cosets, more element counting, normalizers — each a small project with its own receipts. This page's census claims exactly: they are not closed by the frozen ledger, they are named, and their closures are imported from the standard theory as escalated, unproved-here parts. Deferred is a verdict; it is not a proof.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The diagnosis — assembled, certified, and wrong

A second team attempts the theorem of §2 with a fresh ledger. Their Lemma S reads: "If \(p\) is the largest prime divisor of \(n\) and \(p^2 \nmid n\), then the Sylow \(p\)-subgroup of any group of order \(n\) is unique, hence normal." Their assembly runs: every part cites this Lemma S verbatim; every part's arithmetic — largest prime, first power — is correct; auditors are assigned across parts, none audits a cycle, every recomputation matches. The assembly pass accepts, and the team signs: no non-abelian simple group of order less than \(60\). The conclusion is true; the sign-off ceremony was flawless; and the proof is wrong. (a) Find the flaw. (b) Name the single smallest artifact that demonstrates it — an object, checkable by anyone in minutes, that the assembly's audit could not have produced. (c) The team's postmortem begins: "we checked every part against the ledger; the ledger was the hole." Rebuild their audit protocol so that this failure cannot recur — and say what the rebuilt protocol still cannot guarantee.

Worked answer

(a) The flaw is in the ledger: the team's Lemma S is false as stated. Take \(n = 12\): largest prime divisor \(3\), and \(3^2 \nmid 12\), so the lemma's hypothesis holds — but \(A_4\), order \(12\), has four Sylow \(3\)-subgroups (each generated by a \(3\)-cycle pairing with its inverse; the four subgroups correspond to the four ways a \(3\)-cycle fixes one of four letters). Four is not one; the conclusion fails where the hypothesis holds. Every part's certificate was a faithful check against a false contract, and the assembly pass certified exactly what it was built to certify: parts against ledger, not ledger against mathematics. (b) The artifact is \(A_4\) itself — a concrete group, twelve elements, exhibiting four subgroups of order \(3\): \(\langle(123)\rangle, \langle(124)\rangle, \langle(134)\rangle, \langle(234)\rangle\). It is the anti-ledger move: not a proof, a counterexample, which requires no auditor because anyone can re-run it. This is the asymmetry P25 taught for engines, returned at the contract layer: a refutation is cheaper than a certificate. (c) The protocol must add a fourth obligation — audit the ledger itself: every frozen lemma is either re-derived inside the assembly (Sylow's congruences from the Sylow theorems, stated with their proofs or with citations to a published derivation) or tested against a standing counterexample battery before the parts may cite it. The rebuilt protocol still cannot guarantee the lemmas' proofs' proofs — the regress of Exercise 5 — and it cannot certify that the ledger says what the team means: a true lemma, mis-pinned to the intended claim, passes every audit (P44's Exhibit A). The ledger is P01's pinning at team scale, and nothing audits meaning.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The regress, and where it stops

The exercises above end with a protocol that audits the ledger. Ask the page's standing question one more time: who audits that? (a) Argue that the audit regress — parts, ledger, ledger-auditors — cannot bottom out in a person, at any scale worth calling scale. (b) Collect the page's three cases of the regress handled in practice: the classification's community re-checks, Perelman's three independent teams, the two proof systems of the Kepler certificate — and state what makes each audit independent in a sense stronger than "performed by someone else". (c) Say where the regress actually stops for the miniature of this page — what, at the bottom, a competent stranger is asked to accept without further audit — and whether that residue can ever reach zero.

Hint

For (a): a person is a single point of failure, and the whole pattern exists because single points of attention do not scale. For (b): independence is about failure modes — shared authors, shared derivations, shared systems, shared schools of reading. For (c): the bottom of this page's miniature is a short list: the Sylow theorems, the class equation, and the reading of the ledger's English. Ask what it would cost to push one item lower, and whether that is ever the last item.

Worked answer

(a) At the scale where the pattern applies — proofs no one can hold — every person is themselves an unaudited single mind, and "trust this auditor" merely relocates the problem from the artifact to the auditor, at the same scale. The classification could not be audited by finding one heroic reader; it had to be audited by an architecture whose verdicts do not route through any one person's comprehension. If the regress stopped in a person, the pattern would be a marketing document for that person. (b) The classification's re-checks spread over decades and hundreds of readers, so an error must survive many independent reads — different training, different generations, different stakes. Perelman's three teams (Kleiner–Lott, Cao–Zhu, Morgan–Tian) filled the same details from the same sketches with different methods and cross-checked disagreements — independence of derivation, with the differences themselves becoming evidence. The Kepler certificate's two proof assistants (Isabelle and HOL Light) share the statement and nothing else — different kernels, different implementations, different failure modes; agreement between them is agreement between two systems that cannot share a bug. In each case the strength is not that a second person checked; it is that the second check could not fail in the same way as the first. (c) For this page's miniature, the bottom is: the Sylow theorems and the class equation — standard, published, re-derivable in any graduate course, but not re-proved here — and the semantic act of reading the ledger: that "no non-abelian simple group" means what the English says, over the integers the arithmetic computes. Each item can be pushed lower — formalize the Sylow proofs in a kernel, pin the English in formal logic — but the pushing never terminates: it only moves the residue into smaller, harder places, until the residue is the choice of logic and the meaning of the pinning act, where mathematics ends and judgment begins (P01, upstream, forever). The residue cannot reach zero, and the pattern's honest promise is not that it does — it is that the residue can be made small, public, and re-runnable, which is everything a stranger can honestly be asked to trust.
