The Patterns Atlas · P39 · the fifth move of tier 4

The optimized sieve

When exact inclusion–exclusion explodes into \(2^k\) rounding errors, replace the rigid Möbius signs with the square of a truncated divisor sum and minimize the resulting quadratic form.

Sieve primorial \(P(z)\), the Möbius function \(\mu(d)\), and quadratic majorants. To count elements in a finite integer set \(\mathcal{A} \subset [1, X]\) that are divisible by no prime \(p < z\), form the primorial \(P(z) = \prod_{p < z} p\); an integer \(n \in \mathcal{A}\) survives the sieve if and only if \(\gcd(n, P(z)) = 1\). The Möbius function \(\mu(d)\) equals \((-1)^r\) if \(d = p_1\cdots p_r\) is a product of \(r\) distinct primes, and \(0\) if \(d\) is divisible by a square \(p^2\). While exact inclusion–exclusion uses \(\sum_{d \mid \gcd(n, P(z))} \mu(d) = \mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n,P(z))=1}\), a quadratic majorant replaces that exact sum with \(\bigl(\sum_{d \mid n,\, d \le R} \lambda_d\bigr)^2 \ge \mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n,P(z))=1}\) for real weights \(\lambda_d\) normalized by \(\lambda_1 = 1\).

1 · Before we begin

P10 taught us how to force collisions when objects outnumber boxes, and P04 showed us how completing the square reveals the exact minimum of a quadratic expression. P30 analyzed divisibility one prime \(p\) at a time, and P38 showed us that binary prime problems (\(N = p_1 + p_2\) or twin primes \(p' - p = 2\)) hit a brick wall on the frequency circle because the Parseval \(L^2\) energy swamps the main term.

Here is why naive sieving hits an equally brutal wall. Suppose we want to count primes in \([1, X]\) by crossing out multiples of all primes \(p < z = \sqrt{X}\) using Legendre's inclusion–exclusion formula (P76): \(\sum_{d \mid P(z)} \mu(d)\,\lfloor X/d \rfloor\). Each floor \(\lfloor X/d \rfloor = \frac{X}{d} - \{X/d\}\) carries a fractional rounding error of size up to \(1\). And how many squarefree divisors \(d \mid P(z)\) are there? Exactly \(2^{\pi(z)}\)! Already at \(X = 2500\) (\(z = 50\), where there are \(\pi(50) = 15\) primes), there are \(2^{15} = 32{,}768\) rounding errors — more than thirteen times larger than the entire interval \(X = 2500\)! Worse still, if you simply chop the sum off at \(d \le R\), the truncated sum \(\sum_{d \mid n,\, d \le R} \mu(d)\) oscillates wildly in sign, so you lose both upper and lower bounds. How do you truncate a sieve to \(d \le R\) while keeping a valid inequality and choosing the best possible weights?

2 · Meet the problem: sieving by \(\{2, 3, 5\}\) with only five divisors instead of eight

Test the truncation crisis on a concrete toy sieve: we want to bound the fraction of integers \(n \in [1, X]\) that are coprime to \(P(6) = 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 30\), using only divisors \(d \le R = 6\). Out of the \(2^3 = 8\) divisors of \(30\), only five belong to our truncated budget \(D = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\) (omitting \(10, 15, 30\)).

Look at what happens if we naively truncate the Möbius sum \(\sum_{d \in D, d \mid n} \mu(d)\) at \(n = 30\): the five divisors in \(D\) all divide \(30\), giving \(\mu(1) + \mu(2) + \mu(3) + \mu(5) + \mu(6) = 1 - 1 - 1 - 1 + 1 = -1 < 0\) — a negative number that is less than the true indicator \(0\) at \(n=30\), while at \(n=10\) it gives \(1 - 1 - 1 = -1\) and at \(n=6\) it gives \(1 - 1 - 1 + 1 = 0\)! Naive truncation doesn't know whether it is an upper bound or a lower bound.

In 1947 Atle Selberg solved both problems in a single stroke. Let \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_5, \lambda_6 \in \mathbb{R}\) be any real numbers subject to the single normalization \(\lambda_1 = 1\), and form the squared divisor sum

\[w(n) = \left(\sum_{\substack{d \in D \\ d \mid n}} \lambda_d\right)^2.\]

Check \(w(n)\) on every integer \(n\):

  • If \(\gcd(n, 30) = 1\) (an unsieved survivor!), only \(d = 1\) divides \(n\), so \(w(n) = \lambda_1^2 = 1^2 = 1\).
  • If \(\gcd(n, 30) > 1\) (a sieved composite!), \(w(n)\) is the square of a real number, so \(w(n) \ge 0\) always holds!
Therefore \(\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n,30)=1} \le w(n)\) holds pointwise for every \(n\) and for every choice of real numbers \(\lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_5, \lambda_6\)! Summing \(w(n)\) over \(1 \le n \le X\), a pair \((d_1, d_2) \in D \times D\) divides \(n\) if and only if \(\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2) \mid n\), which happens \(\frac{X}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2)} + O(1)\) times:

\[\sum_{n \le X} \mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n,30)=1} \;\le\; X \underbrace{\sum_{d_1, d_2 \in D} \frac{\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2)}}_{Q(\lambda)} \;+\; O\bigl(|D|^2\bigr).\]

Now complete the square (P04) to minimize the quadratic form \(Q(\lambda)\)! Because \(\frac{1}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2)} = \frac{\gcd(d_1,d_2)}{d_1 d_2}\) and Gauss's identity says \(\gcd(d_1, d_2) = \sum_{m \mid \gcd(d_1,d_2)} \phi(m)\), substituting \(u_m = \sum_{d \in D,\, m \mid d} \frac{\lambda_d}{d}\) diagonalizes \(Q(\lambda)\) instantly into a sum of vijf squares:

\[Q(\lambda) = \sum_{m \in D} \phi(m)\,u_m^2, \qquad \text{subject to}\quad \lambda_1 = \sum_{m \in D} \mu(m)\,u_m = 1.\]

By Cauchy–Schwarz (P15), \(1 = \bigl(\sum \mu(m)u_m\bigr)^2 \le \bigl(\sum_{m \in D} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{\phi(m)}\bigr)\bigl(\sum_{m \in D} \phi(m)u_m^2\bigr)\), so the exact minimum of \(Q(\lambda)\) is the reciprocal of \(V(D) = \sum_{m \in D} \frac{1}{\phi(m)}\):

Divisor \(d \in D\) Totient \(\phi(d)\) Reciprocal \(1/\phi(d)\) Naive Möbius \(\mu(d)\) Optimal Selberg Weight \(\lambda_d^*\)
\(d = 1\) \(1\) \(1\) \(+1\) \(\mathbf{1} = +1.0000\)
\(d = 2\) \(1\) \(1\) \(-1\) \(\mathbf{-12/13} \approx -0.9231\)
\(d = 3\) \(2\) \(1/2\) \(-1\) \(\mathbf{-12/13} \approx -0.9231\)

Look at the last column: instead of abrupt \(\pm 1\) cutoff weights, Selberg's optimization tapers \(\lambda_5^* = -5/13\) smoothly near the boundary \(R = 6\) and achieves a certified upper-bound density of \(\frac{4}{13} \approx 0.3077\) (within \(4\%\) of the true \(8\)-divisor density \(\frac{4}{15} \approx 0.2667\)) while using only \(5\) divisors!

3 · Name the move

The Selberg–Maynard–Tao sieve framework generalizes our five-divisor calculation to any finite set \(\mathcal{A}\) of integers (or \(k\)-tuples of linear forms) where multiples of squarefree \(d \mid P(z)\) occur with local density \(g(d)/d\) (meaning \(|\mathcal{A}_d| = \frac{g(d)}{d}|\mathcal{A}| + r_d\), where \(g(p)\,p^{-1}\) is the fraction of elements divisible by \(p\), such as \(\frac{1}{p}\) for single integers or \(\frac{2}{p}\) for twin-prime pairs \((n, n+2)\)).

Multiplicative density \(g(d)\), the regenerative totient \(h(m)\), and the sieve sum \(V(R)\). Suppose each prime \(p \mid P(z)\) removes a fraction \(\omega(p)/p\) of the set \(\mathcal{A}\), with \(0 \le \omega(p) < p\). By the Chinese Remainder Theorem (B04), a squarefree divisor \(d = p_1\cdots p_r\) removes a fraction \(\prod_{p \mid d} \frac{\omega(p)}{p}\). Define the multiplicative function \(h(m) = \prod_{p \mid m} \frac{p - \omega(p)}{\omega(p)}\) so that \(\frac{d}{\omega(d)} = \sum_{m \mid d} h(m)\) (when \(\omega(p) = 1\), \(h(m) = \phi(m)\) is Euler's totient!). The sieve denominator is \(V(R) = \sum_{m \le R,\, m \mid P(z)} \frac{1}{h(m)}\).

Pattern P39 — The optimized sieve. To upper-bound (or detect) elements of a finite set \(\mathcal{A}\) having no prime factors \(p < z\), replace the exact indicator \(\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n, P(z)) = 1}\) with the non-negative quadratic form \(\bigl(\sum_{d \mid \gcd(n, P(z)),\, d \le R} \lambda_d\bigr)^2\) anchored at \(\lambda_1 = 1\). Diagonalize the resulting main-term quadratic form via the change of basis \(u_m = \sum_{m \mid d \le R} \lambda_d \frac{\omega(d)}{d}\) (P04 + P05) and minimize over \(u_m\) subject to \(\sum_{m \le R} \mu(m)u_m = 1\):

\[S(\mathcal{A}, P(z)) \;\le\; \frac{|\mathcal{A}|}{\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{m \le R \\ m \mid P(z)}} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{h(m)}} \;+\; \sum_{d_1, d_2 \le R} |\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}|\,|r_{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2)}|, \qquad \lambda_d^* = \frac{d}{\omega(d)\,V(R)}\sum_{\substack{k \le R/d \\ \gcd(k,d)=1}} \frac{\mu(d)\mu(k)}{h(dk)}.\]

Or, in words: Never sum \(2^{\pi(z)}\) raw inclusion–exclusion signs. Majorize the indicator by the square of a short divisor sum (\(d \le R\)), diagonalize the \(\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2)\) matrix by Möbius inversion into independent squares, and let Cauchy–Schwarz pick the exact weights that minimize the main term while keeping the remainder under \(R^2\).

Box P39.A — The three obligations of an optimized quadratic sieve.

  1. Anchor and Pointwise Positivity (\(\lambda_1 = 1\)): Fix \(\lambda_1 = 1\) (or for lower-bound Maynard–Tao sieves, normalize the baseline quadratic form \(\sum w_n\)) so that unsieved elements carry positive weight while composite elements carry non-negative squared weight \(\bigl(\sum_{d \mid n} \lambda_d\bigr)^2 \ge 0\).
  2. Möbius Diagonalization (P04 + P05): Factor the coupled coupling \(\frac{\omega(\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2))}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2)} = \frac{\omega(d_1)\omega(d_2)}{d_1 d_2}\sum_{m \mid \gcd(d_1,d_2)} h(m)\) to convert the off-diagonal quadratic form \(\sum_{d_1, d_2} \lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}\frac{\omega([d_1,d_2])}{[d_1,d_2]}\) into the diagonal sum of squares \(\sum_{m \le R} h(m)\,u_m^2\).
  3. Support Level Calibration (\(R^2 \ll |\mathcal{A}|\)): Choose the truncation radius \(R\) so that the \(R^2\) remainder sum \(\sum_{d_1, d_2 \le R} |r_{[d_1, d_2]}|\) (controlled by simple lattice counting or the Bombieri–Vinogradov distribution level) is of strictly smaller order than the main term \(|\mathcal{A}| / V(R)\).
Key Signature
Claim type Sharp upper bounds on primes in short intervals or twin-prime constellations (Brun–Titchmarsh, Hardy–Littlewood \(2\times\) bounds), or bounded gaps between primes via multidimensional quadratic weights.
Problem shape Counting integers \(n \in \mathcal{A}\) avoiding local residue classes \(\bmod\;p\) across many primes \(p < z\) where inclusion–exclusion has \(2^{\pi(z)} \gg |\mathcal{A}|\) terms.
Tools at hand Chinese Remainder Theorem (B04), Möbius inversion on divisor lattices, and quadratic form optimization (P04).

Three-key diagnosis. Reach for P39 whenever you need to filter out multiples of many primes at once and exact inclusion–exclusion blows up.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 · Two one-line miracles: why \(|\lambda_d^*| \le 1\) and why \(V(R) > \ln R\)

Why does Selberg's sieve give clean, explicit bounds without requiring complex analysis? Two elementary inequalities do all the heavy lifting:

  • Why \(|\lambda_d^*| \le 1\) always holds (controlling the remainder): In Box P39, for any squarefree \(d \le R\), every \(m \le R\) starts either with a divisor of \(d\) or not. Factoring \(m = r k\) with \(r \mid d\) and \(\gcd(k, d) = 1\) shows that

    \[V(R) = \sum_{m \le R} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{\phi(m)} \;\ge\; \left(\sum_{r \mid d} \frac{\mu(r)^2}{\phi(r)}\right) \sum_{\substack{k \le R/d \\ \gcd(k,d)=1}} \frac{\mu(k)^2}{\phi(k)} = \frac{d}{\phi(d)} \sum_{\substack{k \le R/d \\ \gcd(k,d)=1}} \frac{\mu(k)^2}{\phi(k)}.\]

    Comparing the right-hand side with the formula for \(\lambda_d^*\) in Box P39 shows that \(|\lambda_d^*|\) is a positive subsum of \(V(R)\) divided by \(V(R)\), so \(\bigl|\lambda_d^*\bigr| \le 1\) for every \(d \le R\) (P15)! Thus the remainder \(\sum_{d_1, d_2 \le R} |\lambda_{d_1}^*\lambda_{d_2}^*| \le R^2\) never blows up.
  • Why \(V(R) > \ln R\) (controlling the main term): For any squarefree \(m \le R\), expand \(\frac{1}{\phi(m)} = \frac{1}{m}\prod_{p \mid m}\frac{1}{1 - 1/p} = \frac{1}{m}\prod_{p \mid m}\bigl(1 + \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p^2} + \cdots\bigr)\). Every integer \(n \le R\) has all of its prime factors dividing some squarefree \(m \le R\) (namely the radical \(m = \operatorname{rad}(n) \le n \le R\)). Therefore summing \(\frac{\mu(m)^2}{\phi(m)}\) over \(m \le R\) includes \(\frac{1}{n}\) for every integer \(1 \le n \le R\):

    \[V(R) = \sum_{m \le R} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{\phi(m)} \;\ge\; \sum_{n=1}^{\lfloor R \rfloor} \frac{1}{n} > \ln R.\]

    On any interval \([X, X + Y]\), choosing \(R = \frac{\sqrt{Y}}{\ln Y}\) immediately yields the Brun–Titchmarsh theorem:

    \[\pi(X + Y) - \pi(X) \;\le\; \frac{Y}{\ln R} + R^2 = \frac{2Y}{\ln Y} + O\!\left(\frac{Y}{(\ln Y)^2}\right).\]

Exemplar 2 · Twin primes \((p, p+2)\) and Brun's theorem that \(\sum_{p\text{ twin}} \frac{1}{p} < \infty\)

In P38 (Exhibit A), the circle method failed on twin primes because the \(L^2\) energy swamped the main term. Let us bound the number \(\pi_2(X)\) of twin primes \(p \le X\) (such that \(p + 2\) is also prime) using P39. Set \(\mathcal{A} = \{n(n+2) : 1 \le n \le X\}\) and sieve by all primes \(p \le z\). How many residue classes \(n \bmod p\) make \(p \mid n(n+2)\)?

  • For \(p = 2\): \(n(n+2)\) is even iff \(n \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) (\(\omega(2) = 1\) bad residue class out of \(2\)).
  • For every odd prime \(p \ge 3\): \(p \mid n(n+2)\) iff \(n \equiv 0\) or \(n \equiv -2 \pmod p\), which are two distinct residue classes modulo \(p\) (\(\omega(p) = 2\) bad classes out of \(p\))!
Therefore \(h(p) = \frac{p - 2}{2}\) for odd \(p\), and for every squarefree odd \(m\), \(\frac{1}{h(m)} = \prod_{p \mid m} \frac{2}{p-2} \ge \frac{2^{\nu(m)}}{m} = \sum_{d \mid m} \frac{1}{m} = \frac{\tau(m)}{m}\) (where \(\tau(m) = 2^{\nu(m)}\) is the divisor count). Summing over \(m \le R\) gives a quadratic logarithm growth:

\[V_2(R) = \sum_{m \le R} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{h(m)} \;\ge\; c\,(\ln R)^2.\]

Choosing \(R = X^{1/3}\) so the remainder \(\sum_{d_1, d_2 \le R} 2^{\nu([d_1,d_2])} \ll R^2 \ln R \ll X^{2/3}\ln X\) is negligible compared to \(X / (\ln X)^2\), Box P39 proves the upper bound

\[\pi_2(X) \;\le\; \frac{X}{V_2(X^{1/3})} + O\bigl(X^{2/3}\ln X\bigr) \;\ll\; \frac{X}{(\ln X)^2}.\]

Now decompose the twin primes into dyadic shells \(2^j \le p < 2^{j+1}\) (P42): in the \(j\)-th shell there are at most \(O(2^j / j^2)\) twin primes, each with \(\frac{1}{p} \le \frac{1}{2^j}\). Summing over \(j \ge 1\) proves Viggo Brun's landmark 1919 theorem:

\[\sum_{p\text{ twin}} \frac{1}{p} \;\ll\; \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^j}\cdot\frac{2^j}{j^2} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{j^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} < \infty.\]

While the reciprocals of all primes diverge (\(\sum_p \frac{1}{p} = \infty\)), the reciprocals of twin primes converge to Brun's constant \(B_2 \approx 1.90216\).

Exemplar 3 · The Maynard–Tao multidimensional sieve and bounded gaps between primes

How can an upper-bound quadratic weight \(w(n) = \bigl(\sum \lambda_d\bigr)^2 \ge 0\) ever prove a lower bound that forces primes to exist close together? In 2013 James Maynard and Terence Tao (building on Goldston–Pintz–Yıldırım and Yitang Zhang) replaced a single divisor sum on \(n\) with a \(k\)-dimensional divisor sum on an admissible \(k\)-tuple \((n + h_1, n + h_2, \dots, n + h_k)\):

\[w(n) = \left(\sum_{\substack{d_i \mid (n + h_i) \\ d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k \le R}} \lambda_{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_k}\right)^2 \;\ge\; 0.\]

Because \(w(n) \ge 0\) is non-negative at every integer \(n \in [X, 2X]\), compare two weighted sums over \(n \in [X, 2X]\):

  • The baseline quadratic form \(S_1 = \sum_{n=X}^{2X} w(n)\): measures the total weight of all \(n\).
  • The prime-weighted quadratic form \(S_2 = \sum_{n=X}^{2X} \left(\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n + h_i)\right) w(n)\): weights each \(n\) by how many of the \(k\) shifted numbers \(n + h_1, \dots, n + h_k\) are actually prime!
Diagonalizing both \(k\)-dimensional quadratic forms simultaneously via Box P39.A (Obligation 2) and parameterizing \(\lambda_{d_1,\dots,d_k}\) by a smooth function \(F(t_1, \dots, t_k)\) on the \(k\)-simplex \(\sum_{i=1}^k t_i \le 1\) reduces the ratio \(S_2 / S_1\) to a finite-dimensional Rayleigh quotient (P29):

\[\frac{S_2}{S_1} \;\sim\; \frac{\theta}{2}\,M_k(F), \qquad M_k(F) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^k \idotsint \left(\int_0^{1-\sum_{j\ne i} t_j} F(t)\,dt_i\right)^2 dt_1\cdots\widehat{dt_i}\cdots dt_k}{\displaystyle\idotsint F(t_1, \dots, t_k)^2\,dt_1\cdots dt_k}.\]

Using the Bombieri–Vinogradov distribution level \(\theta = \frac{1}{2}\), Maynard proved \(M_k(F) \asymp \ln k \to \infty\) as \(k \to \infty\), and already at \(k = 105\) (later reduced by Polymath8b to \(k = 50\), with span \(h_{50} - h_1 = 246\)), \(\frac{\theta}{2}M_k(F) > 1\), meaning \(S_2 > S_1\), or

\[\sum_{n=X}^{2X} \left(\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n + h_i) - 1\right) w(n) > 0.\]

By the pigeonhole principle (P10), since \(w(n) \ge 0\), at least one \(n \in [X, 2X]\) must have \(\sum_{i=1}^k \mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{P}}(n + h_i) > 1\) — forcing at least two primes inside \((n + h_1, \dots, n + h_k)\) and proving \(\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1} - p_n) \le 246\)!

5 · When the trick breaks

Sieve optimization is the sharpest general tool for multiplicative filtering, but three deep structural obstructions limit what pure sieves can prove alone:

Exhibit A — Selberg's parity barrier (why pure sieves cannot distinguish \(1\) prime factor from \(2\) prime factors!). In Exemplar 1, the Brun–Titchmarsh bound \(\pi(X) \le (2 + o(1))\frac{X}{\ln X}\) overshot the true Prime Number Theorem \(\frac{X}{\ln X}\) by a factor of \(2\) — and a single 1-dimensional sieve can never produce a non-trivial lower bound for primes! Atle Selberg discovered in 1949 why: any sieve that depends only on divisor densities \(|\mathcal{A}_d| \approx \frac{\omega(d)}{d}|\mathcal{A}|\) gives the exact same asymptotic answer on the set \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{odd}}\) of integers with an odd number of prime factors (\(\lambda(n) = -1\), including all primes!) and the set \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{even}}\) of integers with an even number of prime factors (\(\lambda(n) = +1\), which contains zero primes!). Because an adversary can feed \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{even}}\) into the sieve axioms without changing a single input \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\), any valid lower bound for primes must be \(\le 0\), and any valid upper bound on all integers must cover both \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{odd}}\) and \(\mathcal{A}_{\text{even}}\) (losing a factor of \(2\))!

Mitigation: Either accept almost-primes \(P_2\) (numbers with \(1\) or \(2\) prime factors, as in Chen's theorem \(N = p + P_2\)), use the Maynard–Tao pigeonhole ratio across \(k \ge 50\) linear forms (Exemplar 3), or inject Type II bilinear information (Fouvry–Iwaniec, Friedlander–Iwaniec \(x^2 + y^4\), Harman's sieve) that breaks parity symmetry.

Price: Proving bilinear exponential-sum cancellation beyond pure divisor densities.

Default: Never expect a single-variable linear/quadratic sieve using only \(|\mathcal{A}_d|\) to isolate primes from products of two primes \(p_1 p_2\).

Exhibit B — Inadmissible \(k\)-tuples fail at a small prime \(p \le k\) (\(\omega(p) = p\)). Suppose you apply the Maynard–Tao sieve (Exemplar 3) to the triple \((n, n+2, n+4)\). Modulo \(3\), for every integer \(n\), one of \(n, n+2, n+4\) is divisible by \(3\) (\(\omega(3) = 3 = p\)), so \(h(3) = \frac{3 - 3}{3} = 0\) and \(1/h(3) = \infty\) blows up!

Mitigation: Always verify admissibility (\(\omega(p) < p\) for every prime \(p\)) before sieving a tuple \((n + h_1, \dots, n + h_k)\) — for instance, \((n, n+2, n+6)\) avoids \(1 \bmod 3\) and has \(\omega(3) = 2 < 3\).

Price: Widening the tuple diameter \(h_k - h_1\) so that at least one residue class modulo \(p\) remains free for every \(p \le k\).

Default: Check \(\omega(p) < p\) for all primes \(p \le k\) as step zero of any tuple sieve.

Exhibit C — Pushing the support cutoff \(R\) past \(X^{1/2}\) causes the \(R^2\) remainder to explode. Even under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis or the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, the remainder sum over \(d_1, d_2 \le R\) requires \(\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2) \le R^2 \ll X^{1/2 - \varepsilon}\) (so \(R \le X^{1/4 - \varepsilon}\)) when querying primes in arithmetic progressions!

Mitigation: Exploit the well-factorable structure of Rosser–Iwaniec linear sieve weights or Zhang's smooth-modulus equi-distribution beyond \(X^{1/2}\).

Price: Restricting moduli to smooth or factorable integers.

Default: Respect the \(R^2 \le X^\theta\) level-of-distribution budget in Obligation 3 of Box P39.A.

Honest scope of this chapter. Our explicit calculations diagonalize Selberg's \(\Lambda^2\) upper-bound sieve cleanly in finite terms. When constructing lower bounds for almost-primes \(P_2\) (such as Chen's theorem that every large even \(N = p + P_2\)), one pairs Selberg's quadratic upper bound on auxiliary prime-weighted sums with Buchstab's identity \(S(\mathcal{A}, z) = S(\mathcal{A}, z_0) - \sum_{z_0 \le p < z} S(\mathcal{A}_p, p)\) (or uses the Rosser–Iwaniec alternating \(\beta\)-sieve).

7 · Lineage interlude

From Eratosthenes' parchment table to Selberg's quadratic form and Maynard's polytopes (c. 240 BCE – 2013).

  • Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 240 BCE) & Adrien-Marie Legendre (1808): Eratosthenes introduced the mechanical stencil of crossing out multiples of \(2, 3, 5, \dots\), and Legendre converted the stencil into an exact algebraic formula via the Möbius function \(\mu(d)\) (P76). Yet Legendre noticed that the \(2^{\pi(z)}\) floor remainders prevented his formula from reaching past \(z \approx \ln X\).
  • Viggo Brun (1915–1919): Working in Oslo, Brun realized that truncating inclusion–exclusion at an even vs. odd number of prime factors produces rigorous upper and lower brackets (\(\sum_{\nu(d) \le 2k} \mu(d) \le \mathbf{1}_{n=1} \le \sum_{\nu(d) \le 2k+1} \mu(d)\)). With this combinatorially truncated sieve, Brun proved that the sum of the reciprocals of twin primes \(\sum_{p, p+2 \text{ prime}} (\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p+2}) < \infty\) converges — the first non-trivial result toward the twin prime conjecture in two millennia.
  • Atle Selberg (1947): Another Norwegian mathematician, Atle Selberg, replaced Brun's delicate combinatorial indicator inequalities with a single transparent algebraic move: majorize \(\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(n, P(z)) = 1}\) by the square \((\sum_{d \mid n, d \le R} \lambda_d)^2\) with \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and minimize the resulting positive-definite quadratic form (P04). In 1949, Selberg and Paul Erdős used sieve weights to give the first elementary proof of the Prime Number Theorem.
  • Chen Jingrun (1966–1973) & Henryk Iwaniec (1980): Selberg also discovered the parity barrier (1949), explaining why pure divisor-density sieves cannot distinguish primes from products of two primes \(P_2\). By combining Selberg's upper-bound sieve with prime-switching, Chen proved that every sufficiently large even integer is \(p + P_2\); Iwaniec later developed the bilinear Rosser–Iwaniec \(\beta\)-sieve.
  • Yitang Zhang, James Maynard & Terence Tao (2013): In 2013, Zhang broke the \(X^{1/2}\) Bombieri–Vinogradov barrier for smooth moduli to prove \(\liminf (p_{n+1} - p_n) < 7 \times 10^7\). Months later, Maynard and Tao generalized Selberg's quadratic weights \(\lambda_d\) to multidimensional tuple weights \(\lambda_{d_1, \dots, d_k} = \frac{\mu(d)}{d} F(\frac{\ln d_1}{\ln R}, \dots, \frac{\ln d_k}{\ln R})\) on the simplex, reducing the gap to \(600\) (and \(246\) in the Polymath8b collaboration) and proving the existence of arbitrarily many primes in bounded intervals without unproved conjectures.

Exercise P39.1 (●○○ — Diagonalizing a 3-divisor Selberg sieve by hand). Let \(\mathcal{P} = \{2, 3\}\) so \(P(3) = 6\), and suppose we truncate the divisor support at \(R = 3\), so the permissible divisor set is \(\mathcal{D} = \{1, 2, 3\}\) (dropping \(d = 6\)).

  1. Compute the diagonal capacity \(V(3) = \sum_{m \in \{1, 2, 3\}} \frac{1}{\varphi(m)}\) and the minimum main-term density \(Q_{\min} = 1 / V(3)\). Compare \(Q_{\min}\) against the exact Euler product \(\prod_{p \in \{2,3\}} (1 - \frac{1}{p}) = \frac{1}{3}\).
  2. Using Box P39.B, compute the optimal orthogonal variables \(u_m = \frac{\mu(m)}{\varphi(m) V(3)}\) for \(m \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) and invert them via \(\lambda_d = d \sum_{k : dk \in \mathcal{D}} \mu(k) u_{dk}\) to find the exact rational weights \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3\).
  3. Verify directly that \(\sum_{d_1, d_2 \in \{1, 2, 3\}} \frac{\lambda_{d_1} \lambda_{d_2}}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2)} = Q_{\min}\).
Hint

Use \(\varphi(1) = 1\), \(\varphi(2) = 1\), \(\varphi(3) = 2\). Since \(6 \notin \mathcal{D}\), for \(d = 2\) and \(d = 3\) the only multiple \(dk \in \mathcal{D}\) has \(k = 1\), so \(\lambda_2 = 2 u_2\) and \(\lambda_3 = 3 u_3\).

Worked answer

1. Diagonal capacity and minimum density. Since \(\varphi(1) = 1\), \(\varphi(2) = 1\), and \(\varphi(3) = 2\), we have

\[ V(3) \;=\; \frac{1}{\varphi(1)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(2)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(3)} \;=\; 1 + 1 + \frac{1}{2} \;=\; \frac{5}{2}, \qquad Q_{\min} \;=\; \frac{1}{V(3)} \;=\; \frac{2}{5} \;=\; 0.4. \]

Dropping the single composite divisor \(d = 6\) raises the density slightly from the exact Euler product \(\frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333\) to \(\frac{2}{5} = 0.4000\).

2. Optimal orthogonal coordinates and weights. From \(u_m = \frac{\mu(m)}{\varphi(m) V(3)}\) with \(V(3) = \frac{5}{2}\):

\[ u_1 \;=\; \frac{+1}{1 \cdot (5/2)} \;=\; \frac{2}{5}, \qquad u_2 \;=\; \frac{-1}{1 \cdot (5/2)} \;=\; -\frac{2}{5}, \qquad u_3 \;=\; \frac{-1}{2 \cdot (5/2)} \;=\; -\frac{1}{5}. \]

Now invert \(\lambda_d = d \sum_{k : dk \in \{1,2,3\}} \mu(k) u_{dk}\):

  • For \(d = 1\): \(\lambda_1 = 1 \cdot (\mu(1) u_1 + \mu(2) u_2 + \mu(3) u_3) = \frac{2}{5} - (-\frac{2}{5}) - (-\frac{1}{5}) = \frac{5}{5} = 1\).
  • For \(d = 2\): only \(k = 1\) keeps \(2k \in \{1, 2, 3\}\), so \(\lambda_2 = 2 \cdot \mu(1) u_2 = -\frac{4}{5}\).
  • For \(d = 3\): only \(k = 1\) keeps \(3k \in \{1, 2, 3\}\), so \(\lambda_3 = 3 \cdot \mu(1) u_3 = -\frac{3}{5}\).

3. Direct verification of the quadratic form. Summing \(\frac{\lambda_{d_1} \lambda_{d_2}}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2)}\) over \(d_1, d_2 \in \{1, 2, 3\}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} Q(\lambda) &\;=\; \frac{\lambda_1^2}{1} + \frac{\lambda_2^2}{2} + \frac{\lambda_3^2}{3} + 2\frac{\lambda_1 \lambda_2}{2} + 2\frac{\lambda_1 \lambda_3}{3} + 2\frac{\lambda_2 \lambda_3}{6} \\ &\;=\; 1 + \frac{16/25}{2} + \frac{9/25}{3} + \left(-\frac{4}{5}\right) + \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right) + \frac{12/25}{3} \\ &\;=\; 1 + \frac{8}{25} + \frac{3}{25} - \frac{30}{25} + \frac{4}{25} \;=\; \frac{25 + 8 + 3 - 30 + 4}{25} \;=\; \frac{10}{25} \;=\; \frac{2}{5} \;=\; Q_{\min}. \end{aligned} \]

Exercise P39.2 (●●○ — Pointwise majorant check across all residue profiles). Consider the 5-divisor Selberg sieve \(\mathcal{D} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\) from Section 2 with optimal weights \(\lambda_1 = 1\), \(\lambda_2 = -\frac{12}{13}\), \(\lambda_3 = -\frac{12}{13}\), \(\lambda_5 = -\frac{5}{13}\), \(\lambda_6 = +\frac{12}{13}\).

  1. For an integer \(n\), the Selberg weight \(W(n) = \bigl(\sum_{d \mid \gcd(n, 30),\, d \le 6} \lambda_d\bigr)^2\) depends only on \(\delta = \gcd(n, 30) \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30\}\). Compute \(W(\delta)\) for all 8 possible values of \(\delta\) and verify that \(W(\delta) \ge \mathbf{1}_{\delta = 1}\) in every case.
  2. Verify that the weighted average of \(W(\delta)\) over a full period of \(30\) consecutive integers — where each divisor \(\delta \mid 30\) occurs exactly \(\varphi(30/\delta)\) times — equals \(\frac{4}{13}\).
Hint

For each \(\delta \mid 30\), sum \(\lambda_d\) over all \(d \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\) that divide \(\delta\), then square the result. To take the average over \(1 \le n \le 30\), compute \(\frac{1}{30} \sum_{\delta \mid 30} \varphi(30/\delta) W(\delta)\).

Worked answer

1. Pointwise evaluation for all 8 greatest common divisors \(\delta = \gcd(n, 30)\).

  • \(\delta = 1\) (\(\varphi(30) = 8\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1\}\), sum \(= 1\), so \(W(1) = 1^2 = 1 = \mathbf{1}_{\delta=1}\).
  • \(\delta = 2\) (\(\varphi(15) = 8\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 2\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} = \frac{1}{13}\), so \(W(2) = \frac{1}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 3\) (\(\varphi(10) = 4\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 3\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} = \frac{1}{13}\), so \(W(3) = \frac{1}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 5\) (\(\varphi(6) = 2\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 5\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{5}{13} = \frac{8}{13}\), so \(W(5) = \frac{64}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 6\) (\(\varphi(5) = 4\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 2, 3, 6\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{12}{13} + \frac{12}{13} = \frac{1}{13}\), so \(W(6) = \frac{1}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 10\) (\(\varphi(3) = 2\) residues): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 2, 5\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{5}{13} = -\frac{4}{13}\), so \(W(10) = \frac{16}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 15\) (\(\varphi(2) = 1\) residue): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 3, 5\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{5}{13} = -\frac{4}{13}\), so \(W(15) = \frac{16}{169} \ge 0\).
  • \(\delta = 30\) (\(\varphi(1) = 1\) residue): divisors in \(\mathcal{D}\) are \(\{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\), sum \(= 1 - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{5}{13} + \frac{12}{13} = -\frac{4}{13}\), so \(W(30) = \frac{16}{169} \ge 0\).

Notice two features: first, the positive composite weight \(\lambda_6 = +\frac{12}{13}\) cancels the double-subtraction of \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) on multiples of \(6\), driving \(W(6)\) down to \(\frac{1}{169}\); second, whenever the linear sum is negative (\(\delta \in \{10, 15, 30\}\)), squaring flips it positive so \(W(\delta) \ge \mathbf{1}_{\delta=1}\) everywhere.

2. Average over one full period of length 30. Summing \(\varphi(30/\delta) W(\delta)\) over all 8 divisors:

\[ \frac{1}{30} \sum_{\delta \mid 30} \varphi(30/\delta) W(\delta) \;=\; \frac{8(169) + 8(1) + 4(1) + 2(64) + 4(1) + 2(16) + 1(16) + 1(16)}{30 \cdot 169} \;=\; \frac{1352 + 8 + 4 + 128 + 4 + 32 + 16 + 16}{5070} \;=\; \frac{1560}{5070} \;=\; \frac{4}{13} \;=\; Q_{\min}. \]

Exercise P39.3 (●●● — Exact finite Selberg sieve for twin primes). To sieve for twin primes \((n, n+2)\) using primes \(\mathcal{P} = \{2, 3, 5\}\), we forbid \(n \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) (\(\omega(2) = 1\)) and \(n \equiv 0, -2 \pmod p\) for \(p \in \{3, 5\}\) (\(\omega(3) = \omega(5) = 2\)).

  1. Using the general Selberg formula \(h(d) = \prod_{p \mid d} \frac{p - \omega(p)}{\omega(p)}\) from Exemplar 2, compute \(h(m)\) and \(1/h(m)\) for each \(m \in \mathcal{D} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\).
  2. Compute the twin-prime diagonal capacity \(V_{\text{twin}}(6) = \sum_{m \in \mathcal{D}} \frac{1}{h(m)}\) and the minimum main-term density \(Q_{\text{twin}} = 1 / V_{\text{twin}}(6)\). Compare \(Q_{\text{twin}}\) against the exact local twin-prime Euler density \(\prod_{p \in \{2,3,5\}} \bigl(1 - \frac{\omega(p)}{p}\bigr) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{5} = \frac{1}{10}\).
Hint

Evaluate \(\frac{p - \omega(p)}{\omega(p)}\) at \(p = 2\) (\(\frac{2-1}{1} = 1\)), \(p = 3\) (\(\frac{3-2}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\)), and \(p = 5\) (\(\frac{5-2}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\)), then use multiplicativity \(h(6) = h(2)h(3)\).

Worked answer

1. Multiplicative factors \(h(m)\) and \(1/h(m)\).

  • \(m = 1\): \(h(1) = 1 \implies 1/h(1) = 1\).
  • \(m = 2\): \(\omega(2) = 1 \implies h(2) = \frac{2-1}{1} = 1 \implies 1/h(2) = 1\).
  • \(m = 3\): \(\omega(3) = 2 \implies h(3) = \frac{3-2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \implies 1/h(3) = 2\).
  • \(m = 5\): \(\omega(5) = 2 \implies h(5) = \frac{5-2}{2} = \frac{3}{2} \implies 1/h(5) = \frac{2}{3}\).
  • \(m = 6 = 2 \cdot 3\): by multiplicativity, \(h(6) = h(2) h(3) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \implies 1/h(6) = 2\).

2. Twin-prime capacity \(V_{\text{twin}}(6)\) and minimum density \(Q_{\text{twin}}\). Summing \(1/h(m)\) over \(\mathcal{D} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\):

\[ V_{\text{twin}}(6) \;=\; 1 + 1 + 2 + \frac{2}{3} + 2 \;=\; 6 + \frac{2}{3} \;=\; \frac{20}{3}, \qquad Q_{\text{twin}} \;=\; \frac{1}{V_{\text{twin}}(6)} \;=\; \frac{3}{20} \;=\; 0.15. \]

Using only the 5 divisors \(d \le 6\) already brings the upper-bound density to \(0.15\), within \(50\%\) of the full \(P(5) = 30\) local density \(0.10\) (and if we include all 8 divisors of \(30\), \(V_{\text{twin}}(30) = (1+1)(1+2)(1+\frac{2}{3}) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{5}{3} = 10\), recovering \(Q_{\text{twin}} = \frac{1}{10}\) identically).

Exercise P39.4 (◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis). You want to prove an upper bound of the true order of magnitude \(O(X / \ln X)\) for the number of integers \(1 \le n \le X\) such that \(n^2 + 1\) is a prime number.

  1. Explain why both naive inclusion–exclusion and the Hardy–Littlewood circle method fail to solve this problem directly, identify the pattern from this atlas that succeeds, and write down the exact local obstruction count \(\omega(p)\) for every prime \(p\) in this setup.
  2. Using the prime ideal theorem / Dirichlet's theorem on primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) vs. \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), explain why \(\sum_{p \le R} \frac{\omega(p)}{p} = \ln\ln R + O(1)\) and conclude that the count of primes of the form \(n^2 + 1\) with \(n \le X\) is \(O(X / \ln X)\).
Worked answer

1. Pattern diagnosis and local obstruction counts \(\omega(p)\).

  • Why P76 · Sieve by inclusion-exclusion fails: Sieving \(n^2 + 1\) up to \(z = X^{1/4}\) involves \(\pi(z) \sim z / \ln z\) primes, so the \(2^{\pi(z)}\) inclusion–exclusion remainder terms exceed \(X\) by an exponential factor.
  • Why P38 · Major and minor arcs fails: We are intersecting two sparse sets of size \(\le X\) inside \([1, X^2 + 1]\) (squares plus one and primes), a binary sparse problem where the \(L^2\) energy on minor arcs exceeds the main term.
  • Why P39 · The optimized sieve succeeds: Define \(\mathcal{A} = \{n^2 + 1 : 1 \le n \le X\}\). For any modulus \(d\), the congruence \(n^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod d\) splits across prime powers by the Chinese Remainder Theorem (P30). By quadratic reciprocity / Fermat's Euler criterion, \(\omega(2) = 1\) (since \(1^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\)), \(\omega(p) = 2\) if \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), and \(\omega(p) = 0\) if \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\).

2. Capacity growth and the \(O(X / \ln X)\) upper bound. By Dirichlet's theorem in arithmetic progressions, half of the primes lie in \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) (where \(\omega(p) = 2\)) and half lie in \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) (where \(\omega(p) = 0\)). Thus the average of \(\omega(p)\) is \(\frac{1}{2}(2) + \frac{1}{2}(0) = 1\), so

\[ \sum_{p \le R} \frac{\omega(p)}{p} \;=\; \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{\substack{p \le R \\ p \equiv 1 \pmod 4}} \frac{2}{p} \;=\; 2 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2} \ln\ln R + O(1)\right) \;=\; \ln\ln R + O(1). \]

Consequently, the multiplicative Selberg capacity \(V(R) = \sum_{m \le R} \mu(m)^2 \prod_{p \mid m} \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)}\) grows as \(c \ln R\) with \(c = \frac{1}{2} \prod_{p > 2} \bigl(1 - \frac{\chi_{-4}(p)}{p-1}\bigr) > 0\). Choosing \(R = X^{1/2 - \varepsilon}\) keeps the remainder \(O(R^2 \ln^2 R) = o(X / \ln X)\), yielding

\[ \#\{n \le X : n^2 + 1 \text{ is prime}\} \;\le\; \frac{X}{V(X^{1/2 - \varepsilon})} + O(X^{1 - \varepsilon}) \;=\; O\!\left(\frac{X}{\ln X}\right). \]

Exercise P39.5 (◐ — Exploration: The Large Sieve as the dual of Selberg's sieve). In 1941, Yuri Linnik introduced the Large Sieve, which Hugh Montgomery (1968) proved is dual (P17) to Fourier exponential sums (P24):

\[ \sum_{q \le Q} \sum_{\substack{a = 1 \\ \gcd(a, q) = 1}}^{q} \left| \sum_{n = M+1}^{M+N} c_n e\!\left(\frac{a n}{q}\right) \right|^2 \;\le\; \bigl(N - 1 + Q^2\bigr) \sum_{n = M+1}^{M+N} |c_n|^2. \]

Suppose \(\mathcal{A} \subseteq [1, X]\) avoids \(\omega(p)\) residue classes modulo every prime \(p \le Q\), and let \(c_n = \mathbf{1}_{n \in \mathcal{A}}\) so \(\sum |c_n|^2 = |\mathcal{A}|\). Using Ramanujan's sum identity or Cauchy–Schwarz on each prime \(p \mid q\), one shows that for every squarefree \(q \le Q\),

\[ \sum_{\substack{a = 1 \\ \gcd(a, q) = 1}}^{q} \left| \sum_{n \in \mathcal{A}} e\!\left(\frac{a n}{q}\right) \right|^2 \;\ge\; |\mathcal{A}|^2 \prod_{p \mid q} \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)}. \]

Sum this lower bound over all squarefree \(q \le Q\) and compare with the Large Sieve inequality to deduce Montgomery's universal upper bound for \(|\mathcal{A}|\). Why is this called a "large" sieve compared to Selberg's combinatorial sieve?

Hint

Factor \(|\mathcal{A}|^2\) out of the sum over squarefree \(q \le Q\), note that \(\sum_{q \le Q} \mu(q)^2 \prod_{p \mid q} \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)} = V(Q)\) is the exact Selberg capacity, and divide both sides by \(|\mathcal{A}| V(Q)\).

Worked answer

1. Deducing Montgomery's inequality. Summing the Ramanujan lower bound over all squarefree \(q \le Q\) and bounding above by the Large Sieve inequality with \(c_n = \mathbf{1}_{n \in \mathcal{A}}\) (\(\sum |c_n|^2 = |\mathcal{A}|\), \(N = X\)) gives

\[ |\mathcal{A}|^2 \sum_{q \le Q} \mu(q)^2 \prod_{p \mid q} \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)} \;\le\; \sum_{q \le Q} \sum_{\substack{a=1 \\ \gcd(a,q)=1}}^{q} \left|\sum_{n \in \mathcal{A}} e\!\left(\frac{an}{q}\right)\right|^2 \;\le\; \bigl(X + Q^2\bigr) |\mathcal{A}|. \]

Recognizing the sum on the left as the exact Selberg capacity \(V(Q) = \sum_{q \le Q} \mu(q)^2 \prod_{p \mid q} \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)}\) and dividing both sides by \(|\mathcal{A}| V(Q)\) yields

\[ |\mathcal{A}| \;\le\; \frac{X + Q^2}{V(Q)}. \]

2. Why it is a "large" sieve. In Selberg's combinatorial divisor sieve, each prime \(p\) has an individual remainder term of size \(O(R^2)\), which diverges if \(\omega(p)\) grows like \(\frac{1}{2} p\). In the Large Sieve, the analytic \(L^2\) orthogonality of the Farey fractions \(a/q\) absorbs all remainder errors simultaneously into the single additive factor \(+Q^2\) in the numerator! Thus \(\omega(p)\) can be a large proportion of the residues modulo \(p\) (for example \(\omega(p) = \frac{p-1}{2}\) when sieving out quadratic non-residues) without blowing up the remainder.
