The Patterns Atlas · P65 · the twentieth move of tier 5

Floer homology from holomorphic strips

When the energy landscape has infinitely many downhill directions at every rest point, stop counting dimensions and start counting the rigid two-dimensional strips stretched between them.

Phase space, Lagrangians, and the Cauchy–Riemann strip equation. A mechanical system on a curve or surface \(Q\) has a phase space \(T^*Q\) whose points \((q,p)\) record both position \(q\) and momentum \(p\), equipped with the signed area form \(\omega = dp \wedge dq\). A Lagrangian submanifold \(L \subset T^*Q\) is a slice of half the total dimension on which signed area vanishes — on a two-dimensional cylinder \((q \bmod 2\pi,\, p \in \mathbb{R})\), any smooth loop \(p = g(q)\) is a Lagrangian curve. A holomorphic strip is a map \(u(s,t) = (q(s,t), p(s,t))\) from the infinite ribbon \(\mathbb{R}_s \times [0,1]_t\) into phase space satisfying the Cauchy–Riemann equations \(\partial_s q - \partial_t p = 0\) and \(\partial_s p + \partial_t q = 0\), with its bottom edge \(t=0\) on \(L_0\) and its top edge \(t=1\) on \(L_1\).

1 · Before we begin

P29 taught us how a monotone energy ledger controls the long-time fate of an evolving field, and P43 used that ledger — on the dented blob \(r(\theta) = 1 + 0.35\cos(3\theta)\) and the pinching peanut surface — to simplify geometric objects by flowing downhill and cutting cleanly when necks form. P33 showed us how a boundary-of-a-boundary rule \(\delta \circ \delta = 0\) (first seen when three mountain surveyors recorded elevation legs \(+40\,\text{m}\), \(+60\,\text{m}\), and \(-90\,\text{m}\)) extracts global topological invariants out of local discrepancies.

Here is the tension that closes Tier 5. On a finite-dimensional surface, we can count rest points of a height function \(f\) by their Morse index — the number of independent directions in which \(f\) curves downward — and connect index-\(k\) rest points to index-\((k-1)\) rest points along steepest-descent paths. But in mechanics and symplectic geometry, the natural functional lives on the infinite-dimensional space of all paths \((q(t),p(t))\), and at every stationary path there are infinitely many Fourier modes curving upward and infinitely many curving downward. The Morse index is \(\infty - \infty\), and the gradient flow is an ill-posed partial differential equation that explodes backward and forward at the same time. How do you run a steepest-descent census when every critical point sits at an infinite-dimensional saddle?

2 · Meet the problem: four crossings on a cylinder, two of them fake

Roll a flat strip of paper into a vertical cylinder of circumference \(2\pi\), with angle \(q \in [0, 2\pi)\) running around the waist and height \(p \in \mathbb{R}\) running up the wall. Draw the equator circle \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\). Now deform \(L_0\) by an area-preserving vertical shear \(p = f'(q)\), where the potential is

\[f(q) = 2\cos q + \cos(2q), \qquad L_1 = \bigl\{\,(q, p) \in S^1 \times \mathbb{R} \;:\; p = f'(q) = -2\sin q - 2\sin(2q)\,\bigr\}.\]

Why must \(L_1\) cross \(L_0\)? The net signed area between \(L_1\) and \(L_0\) around the whole cylinder is \(\int_0^{2\pi} f'(q)\,dq = f(2\pi) - f(0) = 0\). A periodic curve of zero net area that does not vanish identically must cross the equator at least twice — once going down, once going up. Yet if we solve \(f'(q) = -2\sin q\,(1 + 2\cos q) = 0\) on \([0,2\pi)\), we find not two crossings, but four:

Crossing \(x_i\) Angle \(q_i\) Action \(f(q_i)\) Slope \(f''(q_i)\) Relative Grade \(\mu(x_i)\) Geometric Role on \(f(q)\)
\(M_1\) \(0\) \(+3\) \(-6 < 0\) \(1\) Global peak (tall maximum)
\(m_1\) \(\frac{2\pi}{3}\) \(-\frac{3}{2}\) \(+3 > 0\) \(0\) Left trough (global minimum)
\(M_2\) \(\pi\) \(-1\) \(-2 < 0\) \(1\) Shallow dimple peak (local maximum)
\(m_2\) \(\frac{4\pi}{3}\) \(-\frac{3}{2}\) \(+3 > 0\) \(0\) Right trough (global minimum)

Two of those four crossings — the shallow peak \(M_2\) at \(q=\pi\) and one of the troughs beside it — were born purely because we wiggled the \(\cos(2q)\) harmonic hard enough to push a small fold across \(p=0\). If we dial the second harmonic down to \(f_\varepsilon(q) = 2\cos q + \varepsilon\cos(2q)\) with \(|\varepsilon| < \frac{1}{2}\), the factor \(1 + 2\varepsilon\cos q\) never vanishes, \(M_2\) merges with a trough and disappears, and only \(q=0\) and \(q=\pi\) remain.

How can we tell — looking only at the static snapshot of \(L_0\) and \(L_1\) — which crossings are forced by the topology of the cylinder and which can be peeled away by a deformation? Look at the shaded lunes (bigons) trapped between \(L_0\) and \(L_1\) in Fig P65.1. Each lune is bounded by an arc of \(L_0\) at the bottom, an arc of \(L_1\) at the top, and two corners where \(L_0\) and \(L_1\) meet. By the Riemann mapping theorem, each shaded lune is conformally a flat strip \(\mathbb{R}_s \times [0,1]_t\) running from an index-1 peak \(M_i\) at \(s=-\infty\) to an index-0 trough \(m_j\) at \(s=+\infty\). And the area of each strip is an exact difference of endpoint actions:

\[\operatorname{Area}(M_1 \to m_1) = \int_{0}^{2\pi/3} \bigl(-f'(q)\bigr)\,dq = f(0) - f\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = 3 - \left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{9}{2},\]

\[\operatorname{Area}(M_2 \to m_1) = \int_{2\pi/3}^{\pi} f'(q)\,dq = f(\pi) - f\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -1 - \left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2}.\]

By symmetry across \(q=\pi\), the two right-hand strips have areas \(\operatorname{Area}(M_2 \to m_2) = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(\operatorname{Area}(M_1 \to m_2) = \frac{9}{2}\). Now build a boundary operator \(\partial : C_1 \to C_0\) that counts the oriented strips leaving each peak: from \(M_1\) one strip goes right to \(m_1\) (\(+m_1\)) and one wraps left across \(2\pi\) to \(m_2\) (\(-m_2\)); from \(M_2\) one strip goes left to \(m_1\) (\(+m_1\)) and one goes right to \(m_2\) (\(-m_2\)). Thus:

\[\partial(M_1) = m_1 - m_2, \qquad \partial(M_2) = m_1 - m_2.\]

Look at the kernel and cokernel of \(\partial\). The two troughs \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) become homologous (\(m_1 - m_2 = \partial M_2\) is a boundary), leaving a one-dimensional quotient \(HF_0 = \mathbb{Z}\langle [m_1] \rangle\). Meanwhile, the difference cycle \(M_1 - M_2\) has \(\partial(M_1 - M_2) = (m_1 - m_2) - (m_1 - m_2) = 0\), leaving a one-dimensional kernel \(HF_1 = \mathbb{Z}\langle [M_1 - M_2] \rangle\). The two small strips of area \(\frac{1}{2}\) pair the false peak \(M_2\) against the duplicate trough, cancelling two superfluous intersections and leaving behind the true homology of the circle: \(\operatorname{rank} HF_1 = 1\), \(\operatorname{rank} HF_0 = 1\), total essential intersections \(= 2\).

3 · Name the move

Andreas Floer realized in 1988 that the lune-counting dictionary on the cylinder is not a coincidence of one-dimensional calculus. In any symplectic manifold \((M, \omega)\), a path between two Lagrangian submanifolds \(L_0, L_1\) is an element of the infinite-dimensional path space \(\mathcal{P}(L_0, L_1) = \{\gamma : [0,1] \to M \mid \gamma(0) \in L_0,\, \gamma(1) \in L_1\}\). On this path space, the symplectic action functional \(\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\) measures the symplectic area swept out by \(\gamma\) relative to a reference path, and its stationary points \(\delta \mathcal{A}(\gamma) = 0\) are precisely the constant paths at the intersections \(x \in L_0 \cap L_1\).

Relative Maslov index and Fredholm index. At any intersection \(x \in L_0 \cap L_1\), the Hessian of the symplectic action \(\mathcal{A}\) on path space has infinitely many positive and infinitely many negative eigenvalues, so no finite Morse index exists. However, along any strip \(u(s,t)\) connecting \(x_-\) at \(s=-\infty\) to \(x_+\) at \(s=+\infty\), the tangent planes of \(L_0\) and \(L_1\) rotate relative to one another by a well-defined integer winding number called the relative Maslov index \(\mu(x_-, x_+) \in \mathbb{Z}\). By the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer index theorem, the space \(\widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x_-, x_+)\) of holomorphic strips from \(x_-\) to \(x_+\) is a finite-dimensional smooth manifold of dimension \(\mu(x_-, x_+)\) (and dimension \(\mu(x_-, x_+) - 1\) after dividing out the shift \(s \mapsto s + s_0\)).

If we write the formal \(L^2\)-gradient flow \(\partial_s \gamma_s = -\nabla_{L^2}\mathcal{A}(\gamma_s)\) for a family of paths \(\gamma_s(t) = u(s,t)\) using an \(\omega\)-compatible almost-complex structure \(J\) (a quarter-turn rotation rule \(J^2 = -\operatorname{Id}\) on each tangent space), the ill-posed infinite-dimensional ODE turns into an elliptic partial differential equation on the two-dimensional strip \(\mathbb{R}_s \times [0,1]_t\) — the Cauchy–Riemann equation \(\partial_s u + J(u)\,\partial_t u = 0\).

Pattern P65 — Floer homology from holomorphic strips. Let \(\mathcal{A}\) be a symplectic action functional whose critical points \(\operatorname{Crit}(\mathcal{A})\) have infinite Morse index and co-index, so that the \(L^2\)-gradient ODE on path space is ill-posed. Rewrite a family of paths \(\gamma_s(t)\) as a two-dimensional strip \(u : \mathbb{R} \times [0,1] \to M\) satisfying the perturbed Cauchy–Riemann equation

\[\frac{\partial u}{\partial s} + J(u)\!\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} - X_H(u)\right) = 0, \qquad \lim_{s \to -\infty} u(s,t) = x_-(t), \quad \lim_{s \to +\infty} u(s,t) = x_+(t).\]

Grade the generators \(CF_* = \bigoplus_{x \in \operatorname{Crit}(\mathcal{A})} \mathbb{Z}\langle x \rangle\) by the relative Maslov (or Conley–Zehnder) index, and define the boundary map \(\partial x_-\) by counting rigid (\(\mu(x_-, x_+) = 1\)) holomorphic strips modulo \(s\)-translation. Compactness and gluing of \(\mu(x_-, z) = 2\) strips force \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\), making the homology \(HF_* = \ker \partial / \operatorname{im} \partial\) invariant under Hamiltonian deformations.

Or, in words: When infinite-dimensional steepest descent explodes as an initial-value ODE, reinterpret the trajectory of a path as a two-dimensional soap-film strip with fixed endpoints; count the rigid strips of index difference \(1\) to build the boundary map, and let broken two-stage strips of index difference \(2\) cancel in pairs so the homology survives every deformation.

Box P65.A — The three obligations of a Floer complex.

  1. The Energy Identity (Gromov compactness lock, P29): Every solution of the strip equation satisfies \(\mathcal{E}(u) = \int_{\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]} |\partial_s u|^2\,ds\,dt = \mathcal{A}(x_-) - \mathcal{A}(x_+) \ge 0\). Strips flow strictly down the action ledger; bounded action drop prevents infinite-energy oscillation.
  2. Transversality and Relative Index (Fredholm lock): For a generic metric/almost-complex pair \((g, J)\), the unparametrized moduli space \(\mathcal{M}(x_-, x_+) = \widehat{\mathcal{M}}(x_-, x_+) / \mathbb{R}\) has dimension \(\mu(x_-, x_+) - 1\). When \(\mu(x_-, x_+) = 1\), \(\mathcal{M}(x_-, x_+)\) is a finite set of isolated points.
  3. Broken-Strip Compactification and Gluing (P33 + P43): When \(\mu(x_-, z) = 2\), \(\mathcal{M}(x_-, z)\) is a one-dimensional manifold whose ends compactify (absent sphere/disk bubbling) exclusively by two-story broken strips \(\bigsqcup_{\mu(x_-, y)=1} \mathcal{M}(x_-, y) \times \mathcal{M}(y, z)\). Since the signed boundary count of any compact 1-manifold is zero, \(\langle \partial^2 x_-, z \rangle = 0\).
Key Signature
Claim type A lower bound on intersections \(\#(L_0 \cap \phi_H^1(L_1))\) or periodic orbits of a Hamiltonian system that persists under arbitrarily large symplectic deformations.
Problem shape Variational functional \(\mathcal{A}\) on loop/path space whose second variation has unbounded positive and negative spectrum (\(\text{index} = \infty\)), blocking classical Morse theory (P41).
Tools at hand Symplectic form \(\omega\), compatible almost-complex structure \(J\), action ledger \(\mathcal{A}\), and Fredholm/gluing theory for elliptic Cauchy–Riemann operators.

Three-key diagnosis. Reach for P65 whenever a geometric intersection or periodic-orbit problem is governed by an action principle whose critical points have infinite Morse index, but whose connecting trajectories satisfy a two-dimensional elliptic PDE with a positive energy drop.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 · The closed-form broken-strip diamond (\(\partial \circ \partial = 0\) by hand)

Why do two-stage broken trajectories always cancel in pairs? We can watch the entire compactification and gluing mechanism (P43 + P33) unfold in closed form on the four-corner potential in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (equivalently, for the exact Lagrangian \(L_1 = \{p = \nabla F(x,y)\}\) crossing \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\) in \(T^*\mathbb{R}^2\)):

\[F(x,y) = \left(x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right) + \left(y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right), \qquad \nabla F(x,y) = \bigl(1 - x^2,\; 1 - y^2\bigr).\]

Setting \(\nabla F(x,y) = (0,0)\) pins down (P01) four rest points at the corners of the square \([-1,1]^2\), with Hessian matrix \(\operatorname{Hess} F(x,y) = \operatorname{diag}(-2x, -2y)\):

  • Peak \(P = (1,1)\): \(\operatorname{Hess} F = \operatorname{diag}(-2, -2)\) has two negative eigenvalues, so relative index \(\mu(P) = 2\) and action \(F(P) = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = +\frac{4}{3}\).
  • Two saddles \(S_a = (1,-1)\) and \(S_b = (-1,1)\): \(\operatorname{Hess} F\) has signs \((-2, +2)\) and \((+2, -2)\), so \(\mu(S_a) = \mu(S_b) = 1\) and action \(F(S_a) = F(S_b) = 0\).
  • Bowl \(B = (-1,-1)\): \(\operatorname{Hess} F = \operatorname{diag}(+2, +2)\) has zero negative eigenvalues, so \(\mu(B) = 0\) and action \(F(B) = -\frac{4}{3}\).

For a strip \(u(s,t)\) independent of \(t\) (the zero-mode locus where Cauchy–Riemann reduces to steepest descent \(\dot{u}(s) = -\nabla F(u(s))\)), the equations decouple (P05) into \(\dot{x} = x^2 - 1\) and \(\dot{y} = y^2 - 1\). Because \(\frac{d}{ds}(-\tanh s) = -\operatorname{sech}^2 s = \tanh^2 s - 1\), the unique downward connector on \([-1,1]\) modulo time shift is \(-\tanh(s)\). Therefore:

  • The rigid (\(\Delta\mu = 1\)) moduli spaces each consist of a single trajectory: \(\mathcal{M}(P, S_a) = \{(1, -\tanh s)\}\), \(\mathcal{M}(P, S_b) = \{(-\tanh s, 1)\}\), \(\mathcal{M}(S_a, B) = \{(-\tanh s, -1)\}\), and \(\mathcal{M}(S_b, B) = \{(-1, -\tanh s)\}\). Each dissipates exact energy (P29) \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \operatorname{sech}^4(s)\,ds = \frac{4}{3} = \Delta F\).
  • The index-difference-2 (\(\Delta\mu = 2\)) trajectories from \(P=(1,1)\) to \(B=(-1,-1)\) have two independent shift times \(s_1, s_2\): \(x(s) = -\tanh(s - s_1)\) and \(y(s) = -\tanh(s - s_2)\). Dividing out overall time translation \(s \mapsto s + s_1\) leaves a single internal delay parameter \(\tau = s_2 - s_1 \in (-\infty, +\infty)\):

    \[u_\tau(s) = \bigl(-\tanh(s),\; -\tanh(s - \tau)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal{E}(u_\tau) = \frac{4}{3} + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{8}{3} = F(P) - F(B).\]

Now inspect the two ends of the moduli interval \(\tau \in (-\infty, +\infty)\) illustrated in Fig P65.2. When \(\tau \to -\infty\), \(y(s)\) completes its drop from \(+1\) to \(-1\) around \(s \approx \tau \ll 0\) while \(x(s) \approx +1\) is still parked at the top edge; only later around \(s \approx 0\) does \(x(s)\) drop from \(+1\) to \(-1\) along \(y = -1\). That limit is the broken two-story trajectory \((P \to S_a) \,\#\, (S_a \to B)\). Conversely, when \(\tau \to +\infty\), \(x(s)\) drops first around \(s \approx 0\) to reach \(S_b = (-1,1)\), and \(y(s)\) drops second around \(s \approx \tau \gg 0\) to reach \(B = (-1,-1)\) — the broken trajectory \((P \to S_b) \,\#\, (S_b \to B)\). In matrix form, with standard determinant orientations on the tangent spaces:

\[\partial_2 = \begin{pmatrix} +1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} : \mathbb{Z}\langle P \rangle \to \mathbb{Z}\langle S_a, S_b \rangle, \qquad \partial_1 = \begin{pmatrix} +1 & +1 \end{pmatrix} : \mathbb{Z}\langle S_a, S_b \rangle \to \mathbb{Z}\langle B \rangle,\]

\[\partial_1 \circ \partial_2(P) = (+1)(+1)\,B + (+1)(-1)\,B = (1 - 1)\,B = 0.\]

How you could have found this: whenever two independent coordinates each want to jump from \(+1\) to \(-1\), there is one continuous degree of freedom measuring which coordinate jumps first (\(\tau = s_2 - s_1\)). The two boundary extremes of "who goes first" are "coordinate \(y\) goes infinitely earlier" (\(\tau = -\infty\), passing through \(S_a\)) and "coordinate \(x\) goes infinitely earlier" (\(\tau = +\infty\), passing through \(S_b\)).

Exemplar 2 · Why the naive Morse index is \(\infty - \infty\): Fourier modes on loop space

To see why classical Morse theory (P41) fails on loop spaces while the Cauchy–Riemann strip equation succeeds, take the simplest phase space \(M = \mathbb{R}^2 \cong \mathbb{C}\) with coordinate \(z = q + ip\) and quarter-turn multiplication \(J_0 = i\). On the space of smooth 1-periodic loops \(\gamma : \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{C}\), the unperturbed symplectic action is the signed area enclosed by the loop:

\[\mathcal{A}_0(\gamma) = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 \langle -i\dot{\gamma}(t),\, \gamma(t) \rangle\,dt.\]

Split the loop into waves (P24) via its Fourier expansion \(\gamma(t) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} c_k e^{2\pi i k t}\) with \(c_k \in \mathbb{C}\). Because \(\dot{\gamma}(t) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} (2\pi i k) c_k e^{2\pi i k t}\) and \(-i(2\pi i k) = 2\pi k\), orthogonality of the modes \(\int_0^1 e^{2\pi i(k-m)t}\,dt = \delta_{km}\) diagonalizes \(\mathcal{A}_0\) immediately:

\[\mathcal{A}_0(\gamma) = \pi \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} k\,|c_k|^2 = \pi\bigl(|c_1|^2 + 2|c_2|^2 + 3|c_3|^2 + \cdots\bigr) - \pi\bigl(|c_{-1}|^2 + 2|c_{-2}|^2 + 3|c_{-3}|^2 + \cdots\bigr).\]

At the critical point \(\gamma \equiv 0\), the Hessian operator \(-i\frac{d}{dt}\) has eigenvalue \(\lambda_k = 2\pi k\) on the \(k\)-th Fourier mode. Every positive winding number \(k \ge 1\) curves upward (\(\lambda_k > 0\)), and every negative winding number \(k \le -1\) curves downward (\(\lambda_k < 0\)). Both the Morse index and co-index are \(\infty\). Worse, the formal downward gradient flow \(\partial_s c_k(s) = -2\pi k\,c_k(s)\) has solution

\[c_k(s) = c_k(0)\,e^{-2\pi k s}.\]

Look at what happens for \(k = -100\): \(c_{-100}(s) = c_{-100}(0)\,e^{+200\pi s}\) grows by a factor of \(e^{200\pi} \approx 10^{272}\) in a single unit of \(s\)! As an initial-value problem given \(\gamma(0,t)\), the equation explodes instantaneously. Floer's move (P65) rescues the PDE by switching from an initial-value problem at \(s=0\) to a two-sided boundary value problem on \(s \in (-\infty, +\infty)\) with bounded total energy \(\mathcal{E}(u) = \sum_{k \in \mathbb{Z}} (2\pi k)^2 \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |c_k(s)|^2\,ds < \infty\): positive modes \(k > 0\) can only decay forward (\(s \to +\infty\)), negative modes \(k < 0\) can only decay backward (\(s \to -\infty\)), and only finitely many modes can cross zero when a Hamiltonian perturbation \(H(t,z)\) shifts the spectrum.

Exemplar 3 · Arnold's conjecture on the 2-torus \(\mathbb{T}^2\): four fixed points where topology predicts zero

On the two-dimensional torus \(\mathbb{T}^2 = (\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})^2\) with area form \(\omega = dq_1 \wedge dq_2\), the Euler characteristic is \(\chi(\mathbb{T}^2) = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0\). A pure translation \((q_1, q_2) \mapsto (q_1 + c_1, q_2 + c_2)\) moves every point and has zero fixed points. Vladimir Arnold conjectured that if a transformation is generated by a periodic Hamiltonian \(H_t(q_1, q_2)\) (meaning zero net momentum drift around the cycles), then any non-degenerate time-1 map \(\phi_H^1\) must have at least \(\sum_{i=0}^{2} b_i(\mathbb{T}^2) = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4\) fixed points.

Let us verify how Hamiltonian Floer homology proves this on the explicit autonomous Hamiltonian \(H(q_1, q_2) = \alpha\cos q_1 + \beta\cos q_2\) with \(0 < \beta < \alpha < \frac{1}{2}\). Hamilton's equations \(\dot{q}_1 = \partial H / \partial q_2 = -\beta\sin q_2\) and \(\dot{q}_2 = -\partial H / \partial q_1 = \alpha\sin q_1\) have four stationary 1-periodic orbits at \((q_1, q_2) \in \{0, \pi\}^2\):

Orbit \(x\) Coordinates \((q_1, q_2)\) Action \(\mathcal{A}_H(x) = H(x)\) Conley–Zehnder Index \(\mu_{\text{CZ}}(x)\) Connecting Strips to Lower Grade
\(x_{++}\) \((0, 0)\) \(+\alpha + \beta\) \(2\) \(2\) strips to \(x_{+-}\) (area \(2\beta\)), \(2\) strips to \(x_{-+}\) (area \(2\alpha\))
\(x_{+-}\) \((0, \pi)\) \(+\alpha - \beta\) \(1\) \(2\) strips to \(x_{--}\) (area \(2\alpha\))
\(x_{-+}\) \((\pi, 0)\) \(-\alpha + \beta\) \(1\) \(2\) strips to \(x_{--}\) (area \(2\beta\))
\(x_{--}\) \((\pi, \pi)\) \(-\alpha - \beta\) \(0\) None (\(\mu = 0\))

Along each circle factor of \(\mathbb{T}^2\), the two connecting strips between \(0\) and \(\pi\) run down the left half \((-\pi, 0)\) and the right half \((0, \pi)\) with identical action drops (\(2\alpha\) or \(2\beta\)) and opposite orientations \((+1) + (-1) = 0\). Therefore the Floer boundary operator vanishes identically on all grades: \(\partial_2 = 0\) and \(\partial_1 = 0\). By invariance of \(HF_*(\mathbb{T}^2)\) under any time-dependent continuation \(H_s\), every non-degenerate Hamiltonian diffeomorphism of \(\mathbb{T}^2\) must generate a chain complex \(CF_*\) whose homology has rank at least \(\operatorname{rank} HF_2 + \operatorname{rank} HF_1 + \operatorname{rank} HF_0 = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4\). Hence \(\# \operatorname{Fix}(\phi_H^1) \ge 4\) always holds.

5 · When the trick breaks

Every pattern page owes an honest accounting of where its machinery breaks down. In Floer homology, three structural hazards can destroy the identity \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\) or drain the homology entirely:

Exhibit A — Disk and sphere bubbling steal energy and break \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\). Box P65.A relied on the claim that a 1-dimensional family of \(\Delta\mu = 2\) strips can only degenerate by splitting lengthwise into a two-story broken strip \((x_- \to y) \,\#\, (y \to z)\). In general symplectic manifolds, a family of strips \(u_\tau\) can concentrate energy \(\hbar > 0\) at a single interior point or boundary point and pinch off a non-constant holomorphic sphere \(v : S^2 \to M\) or holomorphic disk \(w : (D^2, \partial D^2) \to (M, L_i)\) (just like the neckpinch singularity in P43). When a disk with Maslov index \(\mu(w) = 2\) bubbles off the side of a stationary constant strip \(x_-\), it creates a brand-new boundary endpoint in \(\mathcal{M}(x_-, x_-)\) that has no partner to cancel it, leaving \(\partial^2 x_- \ne 0\).

Mitigation: Work in exact symplectic manifolds (\(\omega = d\lambda\)) with exact Lagrangians (\(\lambda|_{L_i} = df_i\)), or assume \(\pi_2(M) = \pi_2(M, L_i) = 0\) (aspherical targets such as \(\mathbb{T}^{2n}\) or surfaces of genus \(g \ge 1\)). By Stokes' theorem, any sphere or disk bubble in an exact setting has area \(\int_{D^2} \omega = \int_{\partial D^2} df_i = 0\), so non-constant bubbles cannot exist. When bubbles cannot be ruled out, upgrade the boundary equation to an \(A_\infty\) curved algebra (Fukaya–Oh–Ohta–Ono obstruction theory, connecting to P53 and P64).

Price: Full virtual perturbation and bounding-cochain machinery if \(\omega\) is not exact.

Default: Always check the exactness audit \(\int_{S^2}\omega = 0\) and \(\int_{D^2}\omega = 0\) before writing \(\partial^2 = 0\).

Exhibit B — Non-Hamiltonian drifts push every intersection off to infinity. On the cylinder \(T^*S^1\), if we shift \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\) upward by a constant momentum \(c \ne 0\) to \(L_c = \{p = c\}\), then \(L_0 \cap L_c = \varnothing\) — there are zero intersections! Why didn't Floer invariance forbid this? Because \(p = c\) has net area \(\int_0^{2\pi} c\,dq = 2\pi c \ne 0\); it is generated by the multi-valued angle \(c\,q\), which is a closed 1-form (\(c\,dq\)) that is not exact on \(S^1\).

Mitigation: Verify the flux condition (\([L_1 - L_0] = 0 \in H^1(Q;\mathbb{R})\)) before claiming intersection persistence.

Price: One cohomology check on the perturbing 1-form.

Default: Restrict deformations to globally defined single-valued Hamiltonians \(H_t : M \to \mathbb{R}\).

Exhibit C — Non-compact ends let strips escape to infinity. On a non-compact phase space such as a cotangent bundle \(T^*Q\), a sequence of strips \(u_k\) with bounded energy \(\mathcal{E}(u_k) \le C\) could bulge infinitely far out along the momentum fiber \(|p| \to \infty\), violating Gromov compactness even when no bubbles form.

Mitigation: Impose convexity at infinity (or a maximum-principle barrier via subharmonic functions \(\Delta |p|^2 \ge 0\)) so strips cannot cross a high-momentum shell.

Price: Controlling the asymptotic behavior of \(J\) and \(H\) outside a compact set.

Default: Either work on compact symplectic manifolds or enforce standard conical/contact structure at infinity.

Honest scope of this orientation page. Our explicit calculations on the cylinder \(T^*S^1\), the two-variable diamond potential, and the torus \(\mathbb{T}^2\) operate in exact or aspherical settings where transversality is visible by inspection and bubble energy vanishes identically. In general non-exact manifolds, proving that perturbed moduli spaces are smooth manifolds requires infinite-dimensional Sard–Smale Fredholm theory on Sobolev spaces \(W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,1])\), and handling multiply-covered sphere bubbles of negative Chern class requires virtual fundamental cycles (P64).

6 · What to try next

  • When sphere or disk bubbles appear at the boundary of moduli space: Do not throw away the degenerating maps — package the bubble trees systematically using P54 · Compactify by stable degenerations, which replaces missing limit maps by nodal trees of curves with finite automorphism groups.
  • When multiply-covered bubbles break transversality so \(\mathcal{M}(x_-, x_+)\) has the wrong dimension: Pass to P64 · Virtual classes from obstruction theory (and P53 · Deformation cohomology), which extracts a cycle of the expected Fredholm dimension from the Kuranishi obstruction bundle.
  • When the two Lagrangians are small perturbations \(L_1 = \operatorname{graph}(df)\) of the same base \(L_0\): Adiabatic rescaling (P56 · Rescale for asymptotic geometry) collapses holomorphic strips of width \(\varepsilon \to 0\) directly onto finite-dimensional Morse gradient trajectories of \(f\) on \(L_0\), recovering P29 and P41 as the thin-strip limit of P65.

7 · Lineage interlude

From Poincaré's last geometric theorem to Gromov's pseudoholomorphic curves and Floer's strips. In 1912 Henri Poincaré conjectured (and George Birkhoff proved in 1913) that an area-preserving twist map of an annulus must have at least two fixed points. Vladimir Arnold generalized this in the 1960s (Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, Appendix 9) to the conjecture that Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms on a closed symplectic manifold \((M,\omega)\) possess at least as many fixed points as a smooth function has critical points. In 1983 Charles Conley and Eduard Zehnder proved Arnold's conjecture for the flat torus \(\mathbb{T}^{2n}\) by Fourier cutoff (P24), reducing the loop space to a high-dimensional finite slab. Two years later, Mikhail Gromov (Inventiones Mathematicae, 1985) revolutionized symplectic topology by introducing pseudoholomorphic curves \(\bar{\partial}_J u = 0\) and proving his compactness theorem via bubble trees (P54). In 1988–1989 Andreas Floer (Journal of Differential Geometry 28, 513–547; Communications on Pure and Applied Mathematics 42, 335–362) fused Witten's 1982 Morse complex (\(\partial^2 = 0\) from gradient trajectories) with Gromov's elliptic Cauchy–Riemann curves, creating both Lagrangian intersection Floer homology and instanton Floer homology on 3-manifolds.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Six intersections on the cylinder and bigon cancellation

On the phase cylinder \(T^*S^1 = S^1_q \times \mathbb{R}_p\), let \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\) and let \(L_1 = \{p = f'(q)\}\) for the potential

\[f(q) = 3\cos q + \cos(3q), \qquad q \in [0, 2\pi).\]

(a) Use the triple-angle identity \(\sin(3q) = 3\sin q - 4\sin^3 q\) to factor \(f'(q)\) completely and find all intersections \(x \in L_0 \cap L_1\) on \([0,2\pi)\), together with their actions \(f(q)\) and relative grades \(\mu(x) \in \{0,1\}\).
(b) Compute the exact geometric area \(\int |f'(q)|\,dq\) of every bigon between adjacent intersections, write the Floer boundary matrix \(\partial : CF_1 \to CF_0\), and verify that \(\operatorname{rank} HF_1 = \operatorname{rank} HF_0 = 1\).

Hint

Differentiate to get \(f'(q) = -3\sin q - 3\sin(3q) = -6\sin q\,(2 - 2\sin^2 q) = -12\sin q\,\cos^2 q\)? Be careful with signs: \(\sin q + \sin(3q) = \sin q + (3\sin q - 4\sin^3 q) = 4\sin q\,(1 - \sin^2 q) = 4\sin q\cos^2 q\).Wait — if \(\cos^2 q\) appears, \(q = \pi/2\) is a degenerate grazing tangency! To get six transverse intersections, flip the sign: consider \(g(q) = 2\cos q - \cos(3q)\), so \(g'(q) = -2\sin q + 3\sin(3q) = \sin q\,(7 - 12\sin^2 q)\). Let us solve both: first check why \(3\cos q + \cos(3q)\) has degenerate grazing points at \(q = \pi/2, 3\pi/2\), and then compute the six transverse intersections and bigon areas for \(g(q) = 2\cos q - \cos(3q)\).

Worked answer

(a) Degenerate vs. transverse potentials: For \(f(q) = 3\cos q + \cos(3q)\), differentiating gives \(f'(q) = -3\sin q - 3\sin(3q) = -3\sin q\,(4 - 4\sin^2 q) = -12\sin q\cos^2 q\). This vanishes at \(q = 0, \pi\) (transverse: \(f''(0) = -12 \ne 0\), \(f''(\pi) = +12 \ne 0\)) and at \(q = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\) where \(\cos^2 q = 0\) creates a double root \(f''(\pi/2) = 0\) (a cubic inflection point where \(L_1\) kisses \(L_0\) without crossing).
Now perturb or flip to \(g(q) = 2\cos q - \cos(3q)\), where \(g'(q) = -2\sin q + 3\sin(3q) = \sin q\,(7 - 12\sin^2 q) = 0\). This gives six transverse intersections on \([0,2\pi)\): two at \(\sin q = 0\) (\(q = 0, \pi\)) and four at \(\sin^2 q = \frac{7}{12}\) (\(\cos^2 q = \frac{5}{12}\)). Using \(\cos(3q) = \cos q\,(1 - 4\sin^2 q) = \cos q\,(1 - \frac{7}{3}) = -\frac{4}{3}\cos q\), we have \(g(q) = 2\cos q - (-\frac{4}{3}\cos q) = \frac{10}{3}\cos q\) at the four side roots:

  • \(q_1 = 0\): \(g(0) = 2 - 1 = +1\), \(g''(0) = -2 + 9 = +7 > 0\) (\(\mu = 0\), local minimum).
  • \(q_2 = \arcsin\sqrt{7/12} \in (0, \frac{\pi}{2})\): \(\cos q_2 = +\sqrt{5/12}\), \(g(q_2) = +\frac{10}{3}\sqrt{\frac{5}{12}} = \frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9} \approx +2.1517\) (\(\mu = 1\), global maximum).
  • \(q_3 = \pi - q_2 \in (\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi)\): \(\cos q_3 = -\sqrt{5/12}\), \(g(q_3) = -\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9} \approx -2.1517\) (\(\mu = 0\), global minimum).
  • \(q_4 = \pi\): \(g(\pi) = -2 - (-1) = -1\), \(g''(\pi) = 2 - 9 = -7 < 0\) (\(\mu = 1\), local maximum).
  • \(q_5 = \pi + q_2\): \(g(q_5) = -\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9}\) (\(\mu = 0\), global minimum).
  • \(q_6 = 2\pi - q_2\): \(g(q_6) = +\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9}\) (\(\mu = 1\), global maximum).

(b) Bigon areas and boundary matrix: The six intersections alternate in grade \(\mu = 0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1\) around the circle. The six bigon areas \(\Delta g = g(\text{max}) - g(\text{min})\) are:

  • Two short bigons flanking \(q_1 = 0\): \(\operatorname{Area}(q_2 \to q_1) = \operatorname{Area}(q_6 \to q_1) = \frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9} - 1 \approx 1.1517\).
  • Two tall bigons crossing \(q = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(q = \frac{3\pi}{2}\): \(\operatorname{Area}(q_2 \to q_3) = \operatorname{Area}(q_6 \to q_5) = \frac{10\sqrt{15}}{9} \approx 4.3033\).
  • Two short bigons flanking \(q_4 = \pi\): \(\operatorname{Area}(q_4 \to q_3) = \operatorname{Area}(q_4 \to q_5) = -1 - \left(-\frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9}\right) = \frac{5\sqrt{15}}{9} - 1 \approx 1.1517\).

In the chain complex \(CF_1 = \mathbb{Z}\langle q_2, q_4, q_6 \rangle \xrightarrow{\partial} CF_0 = \mathbb{Z}\langle q_1, q_3, q_5 \rangle\), each index-1 generator sends one strip to its left neighbor and one to its right neighbor: \(\partial(q_2) = q_3 - q_1\), \(\partial(q_4) = q_5 - q_3\), and \(\partial(q_6) = q_1 - q_5\). Notice that \(\partial(q_2 + q_4 + q_6) = (q_3 - q_1) + (q_5 - q_3) + (q_1 - q_5) = 0\), while \(\operatorname{rank}(\partial) = 2\). Therefore \(\ker \partial = \mathbb{Z}\langle q_2 + q_4 + q_6 \rangle\) has rank \(3 - 2 = 1\), and \(\operatorname{coker} \partial = CF_0 / \operatorname{im}\partial\) has rank \(3 - 2 = 1\), confirming \(\operatorname{rank} HF_1 = \operatorname{rank} HF_0 = 1\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — Gluing delay and energy partition on an asymmetric diamond

Modify the two-variable diamond potential of Exemplar 1 so the two coordinates have unequal steepness parameters \(a, b > 0\):

\[F_{a,b}(x,y) = a\!\left(x - \frac{x^3}{3}\right) + b\!\left(y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right), \qquad (x,y) \in [-1,1]^2.\]

(a) Find the actions \(F_{a,b}\) and Morse/Maslov indices at all four corners \(P=(1,1)\), \(S_a=(1,-1)\), \(S_b=(-1,1)\), and \(B=(-1,-1)\). When \(a \ne b\), do the two intermediate saddles \(S_a, S_b\) sit at the same action height?
(b) Solve the steepest-descent equations \(\dot{x} = -a(1-x^2)\), \(\dot{y} = -b(1-y^2)\) explicitly, verify that the total energy \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2)\,ds\) along any trajectory in \(\mathcal{M}(P, B)\) is independent of the delay parameter \(\tau\), and verify that \(\partial_1 \circ \partial_2 = 0\) still holds even when \(F_{a,b}(S_a) \ne F_{a,b}(S_b)\).

Hint

Rescale time in each coordinate: if \(\dot{x} = a(x^2 - 1)\), then \(x(s) = -\tanh(a(s - s_1))\). Compute \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \dot{x}(s)^2\,ds\) by substituting \(u = a(s - s_1)\).

Worked answer

(a) Corner actions and indices: Since \(\nabla F_{a,b}(x,y) = (a(1-x^2),\, b(1-y^2))\) and \(\operatorname{Hess} F_{a,b}(x,y) = \operatorname{diag}(-2ax, -2by)\) with \(a, b > 0\), the signs of the eigenvalues at the four corners are unchanged:

  • \(P = (1,1)\): eigenvalues \((-2a, -2b) \Rightarrow \mu(P) = 2\), action \(F_{a,b}(P) = \frac{2}{3}(a + b)\).
  • \(S_a = (1,-1)\): eigenvalues \((-2a, +2b) \Rightarrow \mu(S_a) = 1\), action \(F_{a,b}(S_a) = \frac{2}{3}(a - b)\).
  • \(S_b = (-1,1)\): eigenvalues \((+2a, -2b) \Rightarrow \mu(S_b) = 1\), action \(F_{a,b}(S_b) = \frac{2}{3}(b - a) = -F_{a,b}(S_a)\).
  • \(B = (-1,-1)\): eigenvalues \((+2a, +2b) \Rightarrow \mu(B) = 0\), action \(F_{a,b}(B) = -\frac{2}{3}(a + b)\).

When \(a \ne b\), the two saddles sit at different action heights \(\pm \frac{2}{3}(a-b)\).

(b) Explicit trajectories and cancellation: The decoupled ODEs \(\dot{x} = a(x^2-1)\) and \(\dot{y} = b(y^2-1)\) have general downward solutions \(x(s) = -\tanh(a(s - s_1))\) and \(y(s) = -\tanh(b(s - s_2))\). Modulo overall shift \(s_1 = 0\), the 1-parameter family is parameterized by \(\tau = s_2 \in (-\infty, +\infty)\):

\[u_\tau(s) = \bigl(-\tanh(as),\; -\tanh(b(s - \tau))\bigr).\]

The energy along \(u_\tau\) is

\[\mathcal{E}(u_\tau) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} a^2\operatorname{sech}^4(as)\,ds + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} b^2\operatorname{sech}^4(b(s-\tau))\,ds = a\!\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) + b\!\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) = \frac{4}{3}(a+b) = F_{a,b}(P) - F_{a,b}(B),\]

which is completely independent of \(\tau\). Along the \(\tau \to -\infty\) end, the trajectory breaks at \(S_a\) with energy split \(\frac{4}{3}b + \frac{4}{3}a\); along the \(\tau \to +\infty\) end, it breaks at \(S_b\) with energy split \(\frac{4}{3}a + \frac{4}{3}b\). Both ends have total energy \(\frac{4}{3}(a+b)\) and opposite boundary orientations on the interval \(\tau \in [-\infty, +\infty]\), so \(\partial_1 \partial_2(P) = (+1)B + (-1)B = 0\) holds regardless of the asymmetry \(a \ne b\).

Exercise 3 ●●● — Explicit Cauchy–Riemann strip and energy identity on \(\mathbb{R} \times [0,1]\)

Consider the flat symplectic plane \(\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}_q \times \mathbb{R}_p\) with \(z(s,t) = q(s,t) + i p(s,t)\), \(J_0 = i\), and two linear Lagrangian lines crossing transversely at the origin: \(L_0 = \mathbb{R}\) (where \(p = 0\) at \(t = 0\)) and \(L_1 = e^{i\theta}\mathbb{R}\) (where \(p = (\tan\theta)\,q\) at \(t = 1\), with \(0 < \theta < \pi\)), closed at the right by a vertical perturbation at \(q = 1\).
(a) Verify that the holomorphic wedge map \(z(s,t) = e^{\theta(s + it)}\) on the strip \((s,t) \in (-\infty, 0] \times [0,1]\) satisfies the Cauchy–Riemann equation \(\partial_s z + i\partial_t z = 0\), maps \(t=0\) to \(L_0\), maps \(t=1\) to \(L_1\), and converges to the intersection \(z=0\) as \(s \to -\infty\).
(b) Compute the Dirichlet energy \(\mathcal{E}(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{0}\int_{0}^{1} |\partial_s z(s,t)|^2\,dt\,ds\) directly by integration, and verify that it equals the exact symplectic area \(\iint_{(-\infty,0]\times[0,1]} z^*(dp \wedge dq)\) of the circular sector of radius \(1\) and angle \(\theta\).

Hint

Differentiate \(z(s,t) = e^{\theta s}(\cos(\theta t) + i\sin(\theta t))\) with respect to \(s\) and \(t\). Note that \(|\partial_s z|^2 = \theta^2 e^{2\theta s}\).

Worked answer

(a) Cauchy–Riemann and boundary conditions: Writing \(z(s,t) = e^{\theta s} e^{i\theta t}\), we have \(\partial_s z = \theta e^{\theta(s+it)}\) and \(\partial_t z = i\theta e^{\theta(s+it)}\), so

\[\partial_s z + i\,\partial_t z = \theta e^{\theta(s+it)} + i(i\theta e^{\theta(s+it)}) = (\theta - \theta)e^{\theta(s+it)} = 0.\]

At \(t = 0\), \(z(s,0) = e^{\theta s} \in \mathbb{R} = L_0\). At \(t = 1\), \(z(s,1) = e^{\theta s} e^{i\theta} \in e^{i\theta}\mathbb{R} = L_1\). Since \(\theta > 0\), \(\lim_{s \to -\infty} |z(s,t)| = \lim_{s \to -\infty} e^{\theta s} = 0\), so the strip asymptotes to the intersection \(0 \in L_0 \cap L_1\) at rate \(e^{\theta s}\) (governed by the crossing angle \(\theta\)).

(b) Dirichlet energy equals sector area: Integrating \(|\partial_s z(s,t)|^2 = \theta^2 e^{2\theta s}\) over \(t \in [0,1]\) and \(s \in (-\infty, 0]\) gives

\[\mathcal{E}(z) = \int_{-\infty}^{0} \left(\int_{0}^{1} \theta^2 e^{2\theta s}\,dt\right) ds = \theta^2 \left[\frac{e^{2\theta s}}{2\theta}\right]_{s=-\infty}^{s=0} = \frac{\theta^2}{2\theta}(1 - 0) = \frac{\theta}{2}.\]

Meanwhile, the image of \((-\infty, 0] \times [0,1]\) under \(z(s,t) = r e^{i\phi}\) (with \(r = e^{\theta s} \in (0,1]\) and \(\phi = \theta t \in [0,\theta]\)) is the circular sector of radius \(R = 1\) and central angle \(\theta\), whose Euclidean/symplectic area is \(\frac{1}{2}R^2\theta = \frac{\theta}{2}\). The analytic energy and the symplectic area match identically.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis: three intersection claims

For each scenario below, decide whether P65 (Floer homology from holomorphic strips) is the right tool, or whether a simpler pattern from earlier in the Atlas solves or invalidates the claim:

  1. On the cylinder \(T^*S^1\), you compare \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\) against \(L_1 = \{p = 2 + \cos q\}\) and want to prove \(L_0 \cap L_1 \ne \varnothing\).
  2. On the flat 2-torus \(\mathbb{T}^2\), you apply a time-dependent Hamiltonian flow generated by \(H(t, q_1, q_2) = \sin(q_1)\cos(2\pi t) + \cos(q_2)\sin(2\pi t) + \frac{1}{2}\sin(q_1 + q_2)\) and want to prove the time-1 map \(\phi_H^1\) has at least \(4\) fixed points (assuming non-degeneracy).
  3. On the unit 2-sphere \(S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3\), you rotate every point around the \(z\)-axis by angle \(\alpha \in (0, 2\pi)\) and want to prove there are at least \(2\) fixed points.
Worked answer
  1. False claim — fails the zero-flux requirement (Exhibit B): Since \(\cos q \ge -1\), we have \(p = 2 + \cos q \ge 1 > 0\) everywhere on \(S^1\), so \(L_0 \cap L_1 = \varnothing\)! P65 does not apply because \(L_1\) has non-zero net flux \(\int_0^{2\pi} (2 + \cos q)\,dq = 4\pi \ne 0\) and is not a Hamiltonian deformation of \(L_0\). A direct bound (P15) settles it immediately.
  2. Genuine P65 (Hamiltonian Floer homology): Because \(H(t, q_1, q_2)\) is a single-valued periodic Hamiltonian on \(\mathbb{T}^2\), yet the coupling term \(\frac{1}{2}\sin(q_1 + q_2)\) and explicit time dependence prevent reducing \(\phi_H^1\) to a static gradient flow on \(\mathbb{T}^2\), the fixed points correspond to 1-periodic orbits of an infinite-dimensional action functional. Invariance of \(HF_*(\mathbb{T}^2) \cong H_*(\mathbb{T}^2)\) under P65 guarantees at least \(\sum b_i(\mathbb{T}^2) = 4\) non-degenerate fixed points.
  3. Simpler finite-dimensional pattern suffices (P29 / P33 / Euler characteristic): While rotation around the \(z\)-axis is generated by the Hamiltonian \(H(z) = \alpha z\) on \(S^2\), you do not need infinite-dimensional strip PDEs: \(S^2\) has Euler characteristic \(\chi(S^2) = 2 \ne 0\), so the classical Lefschetz/Poincaré–Hopf theorem (or finite-dimensional Morse theory on the height function \(z\)) already forces the North and South poles as fixed points.

Exercise 5 ◐ — Exploration: Catenoid neck-pinching vs. Novikov ring weights when strips wrap around the cylinder

In Section 2, we worked with an exact deformation \(L_1 = \{p = f'(q)\}\) on \(T^*S^1\). Now replace \(T^*S^1\) with a compact torus \(\mathbb{T}^2 = S^1_q \times S^1_p\) of total area \(A > 0\), where the momentum coordinate \(p\) is also periodic modulo \(A\). Let \(L_0 = \{p = 0\}\) and let \(L_1\) be a Hamiltonian perturbation that crosses \(L_0\) at two points \(x_+, x_-\) splitting the equator into two segments.
(a) Notice that in \(\mathbb{T}^2\), strips from \(x_+\) to \(x_-\) don't just form two bigons of areas \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) (with \(a_1 = a_2\) by zero flux); a strip can also wrap \(k \ge 0\) additional full times around the vertical \(S^1_p\) cylinder, acquiring area \(a_1 + kA\)! Why does an unweighted sum \(1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots\) over all wrapping numbers \(k \ge 0\) fail to converge?
(b) Formulate how weighting each strip \(u\) by the formal power \(T^{\operatorname{Area}(u)}\) (the Novikov ring \(\Lambda = \{\sum c_i T^{\lambda_i} \mid \lambda_i \to +\infty\}\)) restores convergence and turns \(\partial\) into a well-defined matrix of formal power series.

Hint

Group the strips by their symplectic area \(\mathcal{E}(u) = a_1 + kA\). Since \(A > 0\), how many strips have area below any given cutoff \(E_{\max}\)?

Worked answer

(a) Divergence of unweighted counts: When the vertical fiber \(S^1_p\) is compact, every homotopy class of strips wrapping \(k \ge 0\) times around the vertical cylinder contains rigid holomorphic strips of Maslov index \(1\) and symplectic area \(a_1 + kA\). An unweighted count \(\langle \partial x_+, x_- \rangle = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} (\pm 1)\) is an infinite series of integers that does not converge in \(\mathbb{Z}\).

(b) Novikov weight restoration: By Gromov compactness (Box P65.A, Obligation 1), for any fixed energy ceiling \(E_{\max} > 0\), there are only finitely many rigid strips with \(\operatorname{Area}(u) \le E_{\max}\) (here \(k \le (E_{\max} - a_1)/A\)). If we weight each strip \(u\) by \(T^{\operatorname{Area}(u)}\), the matrix entry becomes

\[\langle \partial x_+, x_- \rangle = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \bigl(\varepsilon^+_k - \varepsilon^-_k\bigr)\,T^{a_1 + kA} \in \Lambda.\]

Because the exponents \(a_1 + kA \to +\infty\) as \(k \to \infty\), the coefficient of every individual power \(T^\lambda\) is a finite sum that cancels pairwise (\((+1) + (-1) = 0\)) at each energy level \(\lambda = a_1 + kA\) independently. The Novikov parameter \(T\) records the area ledger directly inside the algebra of the chain complex.
