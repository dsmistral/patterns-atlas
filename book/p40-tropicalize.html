The Patterns Atlas · P40 · the eleventh move of tier 4

Tropicalize

Some families of problems are ruled not by the sizes of their terms but by the gaps between them — by which term wins, and by how many powers of the parameter separate the winner from the rest. The move: throw away the numbers and keep only the orders of magnitude. Addition becomes minimum, multiplication becomes addition, and a curve melts into a piecewise-linear skeleton that still knows where the roots are, where the edges go, and which paths are shortest. The arithmetic of winners is cruder than the arithmetic it replaces — and it is exactly as fine as the questions that were about winning all along.

New word: order of magnitude, made into arithmetic. Fix a small positive parameter t — think \(t = 0.01\), and smaller. A quantity built from t behaves, as t shrinks, like a power \(c \cdot t^{a}\): the order of magnitude is the exponent a, and the constant c is noise by comparison — \(t^{2}\) and \(7t^{2}\) are the same order, and both dwarf \(t^{3}\) when t is small. The map that sends each quantity to its order — written \(\nu(q) = a\) when \(q\) behaves like \(t^{a}\) — turns sums into minimums (the smallest exponent wins, since small t makes small exponents big) and products into sums (exponents add). An order that no term attains — a quantity vanishing faster than every power we track, or a term that is simply absent — is written \(\infty\): it never wins.
New word: the winners-only arithmetic. Keep the real numbers, but change the rules: declare \(a \oplus b = \min(a, b)\) and \(a \odot b = a + b\). This is a working arithmetic — addition is associative and commutative, \(\infty\) plays zero (\(a \oplus \infty = a\)), \(0\) plays one (\(a \odot 1 = a\)), and \(\odot\) distributes over \(\oplus\), because \(\min(a + c,\, b + c) = \min(a,b) + c\). Polynomials survive the translation intact: \(\oplus\) takes the place of \(+\) between terms and \(\odot\) the place of multiplication inside them, so \(x^{2} - tx + t^{4}\) — read by orders, with subtraction dropped because only the winning term matters — becomes the piecewise-linear function \(\min(2x,\; 1 + x,\; 4)\). Everything this page does runs on that one translation.

1 · Before we begin

Two pages built this one. P27 taught us to stop treating a variable as a bookmark and ask where the object breaks — and its boxed statement is the spine this page bends into a new shape:

Pattern P27 — Complex analysis for integer problems. When a question about integers — the growth of a family of counts, the exact weight of an infinite sum, the law hiding in a table — is filed as a function \(A(z) = \sum a_n z^n\), stop treating \(z\) as a bookmark. Let \(z\) roam the plane and ask the three analytic questions: where does the object break (the singularities), which break is nearest the origin (its distance is the radius, its reciprocal the shelves' growth rate), and what shape does the object have near that break (a pole prices a pure geometric; a branch point prices a geometric times a power of \(n\); a wall of breaks prices nothing — the read stops). Then read the integer answer off the break: the growth law from the location, the correction from the shape, the mysterious constants — the \(1/\sqrt{\pi}\), the \(\pi^2/6\) — from the local form of the wreckage. Factoring is the act that reveals the structure: factoring a denominator to find its zeros, factoring an entire function over its zeros, factoring the integers into primes to build the function at all.

And P16 taught us that a problem may be the same problem in different clothes, and that a dictionary which carries the relations the question runs on licenses letting the solved sibling answer. Its boxed statement, byte for byte:

Shape of the claim. "I have solved this before — haven't I?" The problem arrives in unfamiliar dress: cards instead of cells, discs instead of digits, a scrambling machine instead of two stretches. But its question has the shape of a solved one. The claim to check is strong: the two problems share not a look but their entire structure, so that the solved sibling's answers are answers here.

The move. Do not solve. Build a dictionary: pair each object of the problem with its twin in the solved sibling. Check the three promises — the pairing is one-for-one; the relations the question runs on are carried by it; the reverse pairing carries them too. If all three clear, translate the question over, let the sibling answer, and translate the answer home. If any promise jams, stop: §5 is the gallery of what each broken promise costs.

When to reach for it. When a problem resembles a solved one but its dress resists — the sums will not scan, the tangle will not decouple, the rule will not compress. When a solved sibling's verdict, strategy, or formula would settle the question outright. When re-deriving would repeat work a dictionary could carry. And in the negative direction: when no dictionary exists, that failure is itself a finding — P12's obstruction, hunted by this move.

The tension this page resolves: P27's machinery reads answers off breaks, but the breaks themselves — the factoring, the continuation, the local forms — can cost more than the answer is worth, and some families of equations refuse every exact factorization we own while their answers sit in plain sight, frozen into exponents. The question is whether the orders of magnitude alone — the winners-only arithmetic of the onramps — form a world complete enough to solve problems in. The claim of this page is that they do: the collapsed world is a bona fide geometry, its curves are honest piecewise-linear objects, and its answers transfer back.

2 · The encounter — a quadratic that freezes

Take the family of quadratic equations

\[ x^{2} \;-\; t\,x \;+\; t^{4} \;=\; 0, \]

where t is a small positive number, and solve it — honestly, numerically, at a sequence of shrinking values. At \(t = 0.01\) the quadratic formula gives roots \(9.999 \times 10^{-3}\) and \(1.000 \times 10^{-6}\). At \(t = 0.001\) it gives \(1.000 \times 10^{-3}\) and \(1.000 \times 10^{-9}\). At \(t = 0.0001\), \(1.000 \times 10^{-4}\) and \(1.000 \times 10^{-12}\). The census, with the exponents read off:

\(10^{-5}\)\(1.000 \times 10^{-5}\)\(1.000 \times 10^{-15}\)1.00003.0000

Every number in the table moves — and the two right-hand columns do not move at all. The larger root is \(t\) times a nudge (\(1 - t^{2}\) to leading order, by the quadratic formula), the smaller root is \(t^{3}\) times the reciprocal nudge; as \(t\) shrinks the nudges die and the roots freeze onto pure powers. The exponents are not approximately stable; they are stable to four decimal places at every scale we ran, and the stability is not an accident of this family's kindness — it is what the coefficients were saying all along. Look at the coefficients' own exponents: the \(x^{2}\) term carries none (exponent 0 in \(t\)), the \(x\)-term carries exponent 1, the constant carries exponent 4.

Now do the childish thing, which turns out to be the whole move: plot the three points \((0, 4)\), \((1, 1)\), \((2, 0)\) — degree against coefficient exponent — and rest a weight on the point-cloud from below. The weight settles on a bent line: a steep segment from \((0,4)\) down to \((1,1)\), then a shallow one from \((1,1)\) down to \((2,0)\). The two segments have slopes \(-3\) and \(-1\) — and the roots froze at exponents 3 and 1. Each root's exponent is the negative of a segment's slope: the steep segment prices the small root, the shallow one prices the large root, and the bend sits at the \(x\)-term's own point \((1,1)\), which is why that coefficient is a player here. The census table, the two frozen columns, is a theorem about this bent line wearing a numerical costume (Fig P40.1).

Two honest notes before we name anything. First, the freeze is a detector, not a proof: four scales of numerical agreement would catch a wrong exponent instantly, but the reason the exponents freeze — and when they are allowed not to — is the business of the pages after this one. Second, the family next door misbehaves instructively: \(x^{2} - t^{3}x + t^{4} = 0\), the same equation with the \(x\)-term's exponent moved from 1 to 3, has roots \(\pm\, i\,t^{2}\) times a nudge — both roots freeze to the same exponent 2, and the naive guess "one root at the \(x\)-term's exponent 3" is not merely off by a little, it is not a root at any scale: substituting \(x = t^{3/2}\) leaves the equation holding a full un-canceled \(t^{3}\). The point \((1,3)\) sat above the weight's resting line, and coefficients above the line are shadows, not players. What law separates the players from the shadows — and what, exactly, is the world in which the bent line, not the quadratic, is the honest object? That is the move this page names (Fig P40.2).

3 · Name the move

The encounter's bent line is not a trick for quadratics; it is a world. Give it its name.

Pattern P40 — Tropicalize. When a family of problems depends on a parameter \(t\) and what decides each question is which term wins as \(t\) shrinks — not by how much — replace every quantity by its order of magnitude: addition becomes minimum, multiplication becomes addition. A polynomial collapses to the lower hull of its coefficient-exponent points, a piecewise-linear function whose slopes and corners carry the answers: the hull's segment slopes, read with a minus sign, are the orders of magnitude of the roots, and the corners — where the winning term changes — are the roots' tropical locations. The collapsed object is cruder than the original, and deliberately so: it cannot see constants, and it cannot see units. What it sees, it sees exactly — and its answers lift back to the original family whenever the lifting hypotheses hold. The skeleton is a certificate about the family, never a replacement for it.

In words: stop computing with the numbers and keep only the powers of \(t\). Under the winners-only arithmetic — \(\oplus\) is \(\min\), \(\odot\) is \(+\) — the equation \(x^{2} - tx + t^{4} = 0\) becomes the function \(\min(2x,\, 1 + x,\, 4)\), a bent line whose corners at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\) are precisely the exponents the numerical census froze onto. The correspondence is not a coincidence of this example: it is the same fact seen from two sides, the bent line being the lower hull of the points \((0,4)\), \((1,1)\), \((2,0)\), and the hull's slopes \(-3\) and \(-1\) reading backwards as the roots' orders 3 and 1. The word for the whole maneuver — geometry melted into combinatorics, answers surviving the melt — is tropical, a name borrowed in honor of the Brazilian mathematicians who worked its arithmetic out; the lineage section tells that story honestly.

The three-key diagnosis. Claim type: the claims this move settles are claims about orders of magnitude — a growth law, a degeneration's shape, a count's leading behavior at scale — never exact values; if the question asks "how much", the move answers "on which scale, like which power". Problem shape: the data is a sum of terms that compete by size under a parameter — a polynomial in one or several variables, a system of them, a network of path-weights — with a scale at which one term dominates each region of the question. Tools at hand: the winners-only arithmetic of the onramps, the lower hull of the exponent points (the pencil-and-paper engine — plot the points, rest the weight, read the slopes), and a lifting theorem quoted with its hypotheses, never assumed. All three keys are checked before the move is reached for: an exact-value question tropicalized is a question answered with the wrong precision, and a term competition with no dominating scale is a hull with no hull.

4 · Worked exemplars

Moves used: P40 throughout — the winners-only arithmetic of the onramps, the lower hull of §3 · P16 carrying the dictionary between matrix products and walk costs, checked promise by promise before it is trusted.

Exhibit A — the showcase, factored. Return to \(x^{2} - tx + t^{4}\) with everything the last two sections built. The tropicalization is \(\min(2x,\, 1 + x,\, 4)\); its corners sit at \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\), and the hull behind it has segments of slopes \(-3\) and \(-1\). The correspondence theorem of §3 — machine-checked on five families, including two cubics and two degenerates, by two independent routes (exact integer hull arithmetic against floating-point root work) — reads the roots off the hull: the shallow segment prices the large root at \(t^{1}\), the steep segment prices the small root at \(t^{3}\). The factorization follows: the tropical polynomial factors as \((x \oplus 1) \odot (x \oplus 3)\), and the factorization is visible in the bent line — each corner is a factor's constant, each segment's run between corners is the next factor's reach. The cousin from §2 shows what the eyes must not do: its \(x\)-term's point \((1,3)\) floats above the hull, contributes no corner, and prices no root — a shadow. The hull is the arbiter of who counts, and it does the arbitrating exactly, by geometry.

Exhibit B — shortest paths are a tropical matrix product. Here the melt runs in the other direction: a classical combinatorial problem turns out to be tropical algebra wearing its everyday clothes — the dictionary move of P16, with the winners-only arithmetic playing the solved sibling. Take a directed graph on five nodes with integer edge costs, and file its costs in a matrix \(W\): entry \([i][j]\) is the cost of the edge \(i \to j\), or \(\infty\) if there is none, with diagonal 0. Now multiply matrices the tropical way — \((A \odot B)[i][j]\) is the minimum over \(k\) of \(A[i][k] + B[k][j]\). What is one product? A choice of intermediate node \(k\): the entry is the cheapest two-edge journey from \(i\) to \(j\). Products of three factors are cheapest three-edge journeys, and the tropical closure — the product of enough copies of \(W\) — is the cheapest journey of any length: the all-pairs shortest-path table. We ran it on a fixed five-node, eight-edge graph, two independent ways: the classical Floyd–Warshall sweep, and the iterated tropical product. All twenty-five cells agreed, exactly — including the hand-traced cheapest route \(0 \to 1 \to 3 \to 4\), cost \(3 + 5 + 1 = 9\), which the closure's \([0][4]\) entry carries without ever being told the route. The algebraic laws survive the melt too: the tropical product is associative, exactly, on every cell we checked, and it distributes over the tropical sum — because \(\min(a + c,\, b + c) = \min(a,b) + c\), the one line the whole arithmetic rests on. The price is paid in kind: the tropical product cannot count how many cheap journeys there are — it collapses ties, keeps no multiplicity of routes, and answers only "how cheap", never "how many". (Counting walks instead of pricing them is the classical matrix product over the ordinary arithmetic — the two products are the same shape of computation in two arithmetics, and which one answers your question is the three-key diagnosis of §3, key one.)

Both exhibits run the same move in opposite directions, and that is the point. Exhibit A melted an algebra problem down to its geometry and read the roots off the skeleton; Exhibit B found a combinatorial problem already living in the melted world, and let the skeleton's algebra — associativity, distributivity, the closure — do the work. The pattern is one move, not two: find the scale at which the question is decided, and work there.

5 · When the trick breaks

The skeleton is a leading-order object, and every one of its blindnesses is a leading-order statement pushed too far. Three failures, each run rather than asserted.

The constants are invisible — at leading order. The families \(x^{2} - 7tx + t^{4}\) and \(x^{2} - tx + t^{4}\) have the same exponent points, hence the same hull, hence the same tropical roots \(\{1, 3\}\) — and their classical roots genuinely differ, by a factor of 7 on the large root and 7 on the small one's denominator. Any question whose answer lives in the constants — "which multiple of \(t\)" — is out of the skeleton's reach by construction. The honest refinement our measurements caught: the constants are not invisible to everything, only to the leading order. The two families' roots are \(7t(1 - t^{2}/49)\) and \(t(1 - t^{2})\) — the nudges differ, and a secant valuation (the difference of log-moduli across two scales, the estimator our verification labs use) feels that difference at order \(t^{2}\): a gap of \(4 \times 10^{-5}\) where a single-scale reading shows \(0.42\). The skeleton answers "on which scale"; the constants whisper back only two octaves down.

Ties split at second order. When three exponent points are collinear — \((0,2)\), \((1,1)\), \((2,0)\), as in \((x - t)(x - t - t^{2}) = x^{2} - (2t + t^{2})x + (t^{2} + t^{3})\) — the hull has one segment, one slope, and predicts a double tropical root. The classical roots are exactly \(t\) and \(t + t^{2}\): distinct, real, and separated by \(t^{2}\), a gap the skeleton cannot represent. Tropical multiplicity is a statement about leading orders; whether a double tropical root is a genuine double root, or two roots that will split one order down, is decided by the constants — the very thing the skeleton threw away. Reading multiplicity off the hull without this caveat sells a leading-order accident as a theorem.

No small parameter, no guarantee. The whole apparatus runs on \(t\) being small, and "small" is not a formality. At \(t = 0.9\), the showcase family's roots both have modulus exactly \(t^{2}\) — valuations \(\{2, 2\}\), nowhere near the tropical prediction \(\{1, 3\}\); the freeze sets in gradually, not by decree: our census reads \(\{2,2\}\) at \(t = 0.5\), \(\{1.004, 2.996\}\) at \(t = 0.1\), \(\{1, 3\}\) at \(t = 0.01\). And the collision family of §2 keeps its own lesson: its classical roots \(\pm\, i\,t^{2}\) differ by a unit the skeleton cannot see, so tropical multiplicity 2 is all it will ever say. Numerics pay the same tax from the other side — near a degenerate family, root-finders lose digits in proportion to the splitting, which is the skeleton's blindness visited on the machine.

One polynomial is a family; a system is a treaty. Nothing above prepares the reader for pairs, and pairs misbehave in a new way. Run the simplest pair: \(f_{1} = x + y + 1\) and \(f_{2} = x + y + 2\) over \(\mathbb{C}^{2}\) — two parallel lines with no common zero (subtracting the equations gives \(0 = 1\)). Tropicalize each term-by-term and both become the same tropical line \(\min(X, Y, 0) = 0\): the constants' values, 1 and 2, wear the same exponent 0, so the hull cannot tell them apart — and the two tropicalizations coincide along an entire line where the classical curves never meet. Cancellation between equations destroys the naive tropicalization of the pair, exactly as invisible constants destroy it for one polynomial — the same blind spot, one dimension up. The honest handling is elimination before tropicalization — ideal arithmetic, the work of a later page (P32); this page's contract covers one polynomial at a time.

Honest scope. This page tropicalizes one-variable polynomials in one small parameter, with the correspondence verified case by case rather than proved in general. Beyond the desk, honestly named and owed: the general lifting theorem (the skeleton's answers transfer to the original family under hypotheses this page states but does not prove — P27's obligation three, echoed); the multi-variable theory, where the hull becomes a fan and the corners become a polytopal complex; the max-plus convention (identical theory with \(\max\) for \(\min\), standard in the scheduling literature, and wrong — not merely different — for the small-\(t\) questions of this page, as our poison runs confirm); and the precision tax near degeneracy, which bounds what double-precision arithmetic can certify. Constants, ties, and non-asymptotic scales are the three standing blind spots — each machine-demonstrated above, each a question the skeleton answers with silence.

6 · What to try next

When the tropical read comes back silent — a hull with one segment and a question that needed two, constants that matter, a split the multiplicity swallows — the mitigation is always the same in kind: descend one order. Carry the next correction alongside the skeleton: keep the nudges (\(t\) times \(1 - t^{2}\), not just \(t\)), or equivalently work one order down the Puiseux expansion, where the ties split and the constants speak. The price is real: the second-order world is no longer piecewise linear — the melt re-freezes into algebra, and the cheapness that made the skeleton attractive is gone. The default we recommend: tropicalize first, read what the skeleton gives, and pay for the descent only in the regions where the skeleton is silent — the tie points, the shadow coefficients, the constants that a comparison actually turns on. Most of a problem usually lives on the skeleton; the price is paid locally, not globally.

Two published pages continue this story from here. P56 makes the scale itself the object — zooming out until a family's spaces become their own limiting shapes, the geometric cousin of the exponent-freeze we watched numerically in §2. And P54 degenerates families on purpose — adding the boundary the family was missing and counting there — which is the same trade this page made: give up the generic case, keep the degenerate skeleton, and read the original question off it. Two more doors sit adjacent. When the question is a system and cancellation has eaten the naive read, §5's treaty failure is the door to P32 — eliminate first, tropicalize second. And when the convex energy itself is the object, P17 holds the smooth-side twin of the hull's slope-reading: the Legendre–Fenchel duality that the tropical Newton polygon degenerates from.

7 · Lineage interlude

The thread runs long. Newton introduced the fractional-exponent series in 1676 — the polygon that bears part of his name appears in his letters on algebraic equations — and Victor Puiseux rediscovered and systematized the construction in 1850; the Newton–Puiseux theorem, that every branch of an algebraic curve is described by such a series, is the classical ancestor of everything this page does. George Bergman took the logarithmic limit-sets of algebraic varieties seriously as mathematical objects in 1971, in the paper that modern readers recognize as the first general tropicalization. Robert Bieri and John Groves carried the construction to full generality in 1984, proving that the set of characters induced by a valuation is a polyhedron — the one-variable hull of this page is its simplest shadow — and the amoeba picture that made the geometry visible to a wide audience names the 1994 book of Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky. In the same decade, Victor Maslov and his collaborators in Moscow founded idempotent analysis — the \(\hbar \to 0\) "dequantization" that melts the linear Schrödinger equation into a piecewise-linear Hamilton–Jacobi one — the same melt this page runs on polynomials, arrived at from physics. Oleg Viro invented patchworking in 1979–81, gluing real algebraic curves from combinatorial data — the first place the piecewise-linear skeleton did visible geometric work. The name tropical honors Imre Simon, the Hungarian-born Brazilian mathematician and computer scientist who founded the subject's arithmetic — so named because he lived and worked in Brazil. Around 2000, Kapranov's recognition theorem connected the tropical variety of a polynomial to the valuations of its classical variety — the precise statement of the corner–root correspondence this page verifies case by case — and in 2005 Grigory Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem turned the skeleton into a counting machine for enumerative geometry. The standard modern reference is the 2015 book of Diane Maclagan and Bernd Sturmfels, where a reader who has followed this page can watch every machinery keyword — valuations, semirings, polyhedral complexes — land on mathematics they have already met in miniature.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — A hull of your own. Take the family \(x^{2} - t^{2}x + t^{6} = 0\). (a) Plot the three coefficient-exponent points and find the lower hull. (b) State the tropical roots and the predicted classical root orders. (c) Check the prediction numerically at \(t = 0.01\) and \(t = 0.001\).

Hint

The points are \((0,6)\), \((1,2)\), \((2,0)\); rest the weight and read the two segment slopes.

Worked answer

(a) The lower hull runs from \((0,6)\) to \((1,2)\) to \((2,0)\): both coefficient points are players, and the segment slopes are \(-4\) and \(-2\). (b) The tropical roots are the negated slopes: orders \(2\) and \(4\) — the large classical root freezes at \(t^{2}\), the small one at \(t^{4}\). (c) The machine census agrees at both scales: the secant valuations (log-modulus differences across the two scales) read \(1.99996\) and \(4.00004\) — the tiny offsets are the nudges, \(t^{2}(1 - t^{2})\) and \(t^{4}(1 + t^{2})\), exactly as the theory of Exercise 3 predicts.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — A tropical matrix product, by hand. Take the three-node directed graph with edge costs \(0 \to 1\) : 2, \(0 \to 2\) : 5, \(1 \to 2\) : 1, \(2 \to 0\) : 3, diagonal zeros, \(\infty\) for missing edges. (a) Compute the tropical product \(W \odot W\) entry by entry. (b) Which entries changed from \(W\), and which cheapest two-edge journeys do the changes correspond to? (c) Compute \(W \odot W \odot W\) and explain why the table stops changing.

Hint

Entry \([i][j]\) of the product is \(\min_k\) of \(W[i][k] + W[k][j]\) — three candidates per entry, four of them usually \(\infty\). For (c): what would a fourth factor add?

Worked answer

(a) Row by row: \([0][\cdot] = (0, 2, 3)\) — the 3 is the journey \(0 \to 1 \to 2\), cost \(2 + 1\), beating the direct edge's 5; \([1][\cdot] = (4, 0, 1)\) — the 4 is \(1 \to 2 \to 0\), cost \(1 + 3\); \([2][\cdot] = (3, 5, 0)\) — the 5 is \(2 \to 0 \to 1\), cost \(3 + 2\). (b) Three entries dropped below their direct edges: \([0][2]\) (5 → 3), \([1][0]\) (\(\infty\) → 4), \([2][1]\) (\(\infty\) → 5); each is a cheapest two-edge journey, and the machine check confirms all nine cells. (c) The third product changes nothing: every cheapest journey in this graph uses at most two edges (any longer route repeats a node, and the repeated cycle has positive cost, so excising it cannot raise the price). The closure has stabilized — the all-pairs shortest-path table is \(W \odot W\), and the machine confirms the third product equals it cell for cell.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The corner–root theorem for quadratics, proved and made exact. Let \(2k < m\) and consider \(x^{2} - t^{k}x + t^{m} = 0\). (a) Show the hull has two segments with slopes \(-k\) and \(-(m-k)\). (b) Solve exactly: the roots are \(t^{k}\,\frac{1 + \sqrt{1 - 4t^{m-2k}}}{2}\) and \(t^{m-k}\,\frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - 4t^{m-2k}}}{2 t^{m-2k}}\), and deduce the valuations \(k\) and \(m-k\). (c) The nudges are not decoration: verify numerically, for the four families \((k,m) = (1,4), (2,6), (3,7), (2,5)\), that the secant valuations equal the negated slopes plus the exact nudge correction — the difference of \(\log_{10}\) of the closed-form nudge factors across \(t = 0.01\) and \(t = 0.001\) — to within \(10^{-9}\).

Hint

(a) Which points can the weight rest on when \(2k < m\)? (b) Quadratic formula; factor \(t^{k}\) out of the square root. (c) The point-valuation \(\log|t^{k} \cdot nudge| / \log t\) wobbles by the nudge's own size; the secant across two scales wobbles by the difference of the nudges' logs — compute that difference from the closed form and compare.

Worked answer

(a) The points are \((0, m)\), \((1, k)\), \((2, 0)\). The line from \((0,m)\) to \((2,0)\) has height \(m/2\) at \(x = 1\); since \(k < m/2\), the point \((1,k)\) lies below it, so the hull bends there: slopes \(-k\) and \(-(m-k)\), both segments present. (b) The discriminant is \(t^{2k} - 4t^{m} = t^{2k}(1 - 4t^{m-2k})\) with \(m - 2k > 0\), so the square root is \(\pm\, t^{k}\sqrt{1 - 4t^{m-2k}}\), and the displayed closed forms follow; since the nudge factors tend to 1 and \(1/t^{m-2k}\)-scaled 1 respectively, the valuations tend to \(k\) and \(m - k\) — and the machine check confirms the secant valuations match the negated slopes plus the exact nudge correction to \(10^{-9}\) on all four families (largest residual \(2.1 \times 10^{-11}\), floating-point noise on an exact identity). (c) is the same computation read as a verification: the corrections are \(-4.3 \times 10^{-5}\) for the \(g = m - 2k = 2\) families and \(-3.97 \times 10^{-3}\) for the \(g = 1\) families — the O(\(t\)) versus O(\(t^{2}\)) distinction of §5, visible in the numbers.

Exercise 4 ◆ — The silent coefficient. The family \(x^{3} - t^{4}x^{2} + t^{9} = 0\) has coefficient-exponent points \((0,9)\), \((2,4)\), \((3,0)\). Without hints: state its tropical roots, describe how its classical roots actually behave, and name the pattern whose diagnosis decides the case — explaining why the naive reading ("roots live at the coefficients' exponents") fails here, and which check from this page catches the failure before any root is computed.

Worked answer

The point \((2,4)\) is a shadow: the line from \((0,9)\) to \((3,0)\) has height 3 at \(x = 2\), and \(4 > 3\), so the hull is the single segment of slope \(-3\) — a triple tropical root \(\{3, 3, 3\}\), and no root at order 4 despite the \(x^{2}\)-term wearing that exponent. Classically the picture is stranger than "three equal roots": the machine census shows the three roots' moduli all freezing to order \(t^{3}\) (secant valuations \(2.999\), \(3.001\), \(3.001\)), but they are not equal — one real root near \(-t^{3}\) and a complex pair near \(t^{3} e^{\pm i\pi/3}\), separated by angles the skeleton cannot see, a unit-level structure invisible to the hull exactly as §5's collision family is. The pattern that decides the case is this page's own: P40, key three of its diagnosis — the lower hull is the arbiter of which coefficients are players, and the pre-computation check that catches the naive reading is the hull test itself: plot the points, rest the weight, and observe that \((2,4)\) never touches the resting line. The naive reading fails because it treats every coefficient as a player; the hull test is precisely the check that demotes shadows before any arithmetic is spent on them.

Exercise 5 ◐ — The two tropical worlds. This page works with \(\min\)-plus as \(t \to 0\); the \(\max\)-plus world — same arithmetic with \(\max\) for \(\min\) — governs \(t \to \infty\), where the natural read is a root's degree in \(t\). Explore the swap: (a) verify numerically that at \(t = 100\) and \(t = 1000\) the showcase \(x^{2} - tx + t^{4}\) has both root moduli of degree 2, while the collision family \(x^{2} - t^{3}x + t^{4}\) splits into degrees 1 and 3; (b) explain the swap through the substitution \(t \mapsto 1/t\) (which family's exponent points does the showcase's large-\(t\) problem become?); (c) explore the intermediate scales — at what \(t\) does each family's reading flip, and do the two flip at the same place? Report what you find, including anything that surprised you.

Hint

For (b), apply the \(t \mapsto 1/t\) dictionary to the exponent points \((0, m)\), \((1, k)\), \((2, 0)\) and see which family re-emerges. For (c), the discriminant's sign is the flip's mechanism — when does \(t^{2k} - 4t^{m}\) change sign?

Worked answer (one honest exploration path)

(a) The machine census confirms the swap exactly: at both \(t = 100\) and \(t = 1000\), the showcase's roots have degree 2 and 2 (its large-\(t\) discriminant \(t^{2} - 4t^{4}\) is negative — a complex pair whose modulus is exactly \(t^{2}\)), while the collision family reads degrees 1 and 3 (discriminant \(t^{6} - 4t^{4} > 0\), two real roots). The family that min-plus degenerates into a double root is precisely the family max-plus reads cleanly, and conversely. (b) Under \(t \mapsto 1/t\), a term \(c\,t^{e}\) of degree \(e\) becomes a small-\(t\) term of order \(e\) — no change — but the polynomial's roots invert, and the collision family's exponent points \((0,4)\), \((1,3)\), \((2,0)\) are exactly the showcase's \((0,4)\), \((1,1)\), \((2,0)\) with the \(x\)-coefficient's exponent reflected: the showcase's large-\(t\) problem is the collision family's small-\(t\) problem, which is why their readings swap — the machine check confirms the min-plus valuations of the collision family (\(\{2,2\}\)) equal the max-plus degrees of the showcase. (c) The flip happens where the discriminant vanishes: \(t^{2k} = 4t^{m}\), i.e. \(t = 4^{1/(2k - m)}\) — for the showcase at \(t = 1/2\), for the collision family at \(t = 2\); the two flips are the same point seen from the two sides of the dictionary, mirroring (b). An honest explorer notices the asymmetry this page's min-plus convention hid: the "same" polynomial is degenerate in one world and clean in the other, and which world you live in is decided by which direction your parameter runs — the three-key diagnosis, key two, applied to the parameter itself.
