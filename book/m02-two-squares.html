Multi-Pattern Synthesis · Landmark Theorems

M02 · Sums of two squares

Why a prime \(p\) is a sum of two squares if and only if \(p = 2\) or \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) — synthesizing Brahmagupta's norm identity (P06) with Fermat–Euler's descent (P13), Thue's pigeonhole grid (P10), and Zagier's involution (P78).

Moves Combined: P06 · Compose with identity + P13 · Infinite descent (with P09 · Find the invariant, P10 · Force a collision & P30 · Localize at a prime)

On-ramp 1 — The pattern of primes that split into two squares. Test the first ten primes to see which ones can be written as \(p = x^2 + y^2\) for integers \(x, y\):

  • Split into two squares: \(2 = 1^2 + 1^2\), \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2\), \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\), \(17 = 4^2 + 1^2\), \(29 = 5^2 + 2^2\).
  • Cannot be written as \(x^2 + y^2\): \(3,\; 7,\; 11,\; 19,\; 23\).

Look at the remainders when divided by \(4\): aside from \(2\), every prime on the first list has remainder \(1 \pmod 4\), and every prime on the second list has remainder \(3 \pmod 4\)! In a Christmas Day letter to Marin Mersenne (December 25, 1640), Pierre de Fermat announced that this pattern holds for all primes.

On-ramp 2 — The Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity as complex norm multiplication. In P06 · Compose with identity, we met the algebraic identity

\[ (x^2 + y^2)(r^2 + s^2) \;=\; (xr + ys)^2 \,+\, (xs - yr)^2, \]

which comes from taking the squared modulus \(|\cdot|^2\) of the Gaussian integer product \((x + yi)(r - si) = (xr + ys) - (xs - yr)i\). Not only does this identity multiply two sums of two squares into a new sum of two squares, it also lets us divide a large sum of two squares \(x^2 + y^2 = k p\) by a smaller sum of two squares \(r^2 + s^2 = m k\) to shrink the multiplier \(k \mapsto m\)!

1 · Before we begin

Proving the easy half of Fermat's Christmas Theorem — that no integer \(N \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) can ever equal \(x^2 + y^2\) — is a one-line modulo-\(4\) invariant (P09): every integer satisfies \(x^2 \equiv 0\) or \(1 \pmod 4\), so \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0, 1,\) or \(2 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4\).

Why is the reverse direction — proving that every prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) actually splits as \(p = x^2 + y^2\) — so much deeper? Because knowing \(p = 4n + 1\) gives no obvious values for \(x\) and \(y\). To bridge the gap from \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) to \(p = x^2 + y^2\), we must synthesize four patterns from our Atlas:

  1. Local Congruence Seed (P30): When \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(-1\) is a quadratic residue modulo \(p\): there exists \(u \in \{1, \dots, \frac{p-1}{2}\}\) such that \(u^2 + 1^2 = k p\) is a multiple of \(p\) (with \(1 \le k < p\)).
  2. Descent or Collision to \(k = 1\) (P06 + P13, or P10, or P78): Shrink the auxiliary multiplier \(k\) from \(u^2 + 1^2 = kp\) down to \(k = 1\), arriving at \(x^2 + y^2 = p\).

2 · Meet the problem: shrinking \(13 \times 89\) down to \(1 \times 89\)

Take the prime \(p = 89 = 4(22) + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). By Wilson's theorem (or quick modular arithmetic), \(u = 34\) satisfies \(34^2 + 1^2 = 1156 + 1 = 1157 = 13 \times 89\). That gives us a sum of two squares equal to \(13 \times 89\) (multiplier \(k_0 = 13\)), not \(1 \times 89\)!

How did Fermat and Euler shrink the unwanted multiplier \(k = 13\) down to \(1\)? Watch their two-pattern machine (P06 + P13) run on \((x, y) = (34, 1)\) with \(k = 13\):

  1. Step 1 — Take centered remainders modulo \(k\) to build a smaller multiplier \(m \le k/2\): Divide \(x = 34\) and \(y = 1\) by \(k = 13\), choosing the nearest integer multiples of \(13\) so the remainders \(r, s\) lie in the symmetric interval \([-k/2, k/2] = [-6, 6]\): \[ 34 \;=\; 3(13) - 5 \implies r = -5, \qquad 1 \;=\; 0(13) + 1 \implies s = +1. \] Because \(r \equiv x \pmod{13}\) and \(s \equiv y \pmod{13}\), we have \(r^2 + s^2 \equiv x^2 + y^2 = 13 \times 89 \equiv 0 \pmod{13}\). Indeed, \(r^2 + s^2 = (-5)^2 + 1^2 = 26 = 2 \times 13\), giving the strictly smaller multiplier \(m = 2 \le \frac{13}{2}\)!
  2. Step 2 — Compose with Brahmagupta–Fibonacci (P06) and divide out \(k^2\): Multiply \(x^2 + y^2 = kp\) by \(r^2 + s^2 = mk\): \[ (xr + ys)^2 + (xs - yr)^2 \;=\; (x^2 + y^2)(r^2 + s^2) \;=\; (kp)(mk) \;=\; m\,k^2\,p. \] Because \(r \equiv x \pmod k\) and \(s \equiv y \pmod k\), both parenthesized terms on the left are exact multiples of \(k\): \(xr + ys \equiv x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod k\) and \(xs - yr \equiv xy - yx = 0 \pmod k\)! Dividing both terms by \(k = 13\) gives a new integer pair \((x_1, y_1)\) with multiplier \(m = 2 < 13\): \[ x_1 \;=\; \frac{|34(-5) + 1(1)|}{13} \;=\; \frac{169}{13} \;=\; 13, \qquad y_1 \;=\; \frac{|34(1) - 1(-5)|}{13} \;=\; \frac{39}{13} \;=\; 3, \] satisfying \(13^2 + 3^2 = 169 + 9 = 178 = 2 \times 89\)!
Descent step \(t\) Current equation \(x_t^2 + y_t^2 = k_t \cdot 89\) Centered remainders \((r, s) \bmod k_t\) New multiplier \(m = \frac{r^2+s^2}{k_t}\) Brahmagupta quotient \(\bigl(\frac{|xr+ys|}{k_t},\, \frac{|xs-yr|}{k_t}\bigr)\)
Start (\(t = 0\)) \(34^2 + 1^2 = 13 \times 89\) (\(k_0 = 13\)) \((-5,\; +1) \in [-6, 6]^2\) \(\frac{25+1}{13} = \mathbf{2}\) \(\bigl(\frac{169}{13}, \frac{39}{13}\bigr) = (13, 3)\)
Step 1 (\(t = 1\)) \(13^2 + 3^2 = 2 \times 89\) (\(k_1 = 2\)) \((+1,\; +1) \in [-1, 1]^2\) \(\frac{1+1}{2} = \mathbf{1}\) \(\bigl(\frac{13+3}{2}, \frac{13-3}{2}\bigr) = (8, 5)\)
Done (\(t = 2\)) \(8^2 + 5^2 = 64 + 25 = 1 \times 89\)! — \(k_2 = 1\) (Halts!) \(89 = 8^2 + 5^2\)

Because each step shrinks the multiplier by at least half (\(1 \le m \le \frac{(k/2)^2 + (k/2)^2}{k} = \frac{k}{2}\)), the descent is guaranteed to hit \(k = 1\) in at most \(\lfloor \log_2 p \rfloor\) steps!

3 · Name the synthesis

Box M02 · The Multi-Pattern Synthesis for Sums of Two Squares

A positive integer \(N\) is a sum of two integer squares \(N = x^2 + y^2\) if and only if in its prime factorization \(N = 2^a \prod_{p \equiv 1 (4)} p^{b_p} \prod_{q \equiv 3 (4)} q^{c_q}\), every prime \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) appears with an even exponent \(c_q\). This landmark theorem is built by interlocking four patterns:

  1. Modulo-\(4\) Invariant (P09): Rules out primes \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) since \(x^2 + y^2 \in \{0, 1, 2\} \pmod 4\).
  2. Local Congruence Seed (P30): For \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), Wilson's theorem gives \(u = \bigl(\frac{p-1}{2}\bigr)!\) satisfying \(u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p\).
  3. Prime Representation (\(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4 \implies p = x^2 + y^2\)): Proved via Fermat–Euler Descent (P06 + P13), Thue's Pigeonhole Grid (P10 + P15), or Zagier's Dual Involutions (P78 + P70).
  4. Multiplicative Composition (P06): Combines \(2 = 1^2 + 1^2\), \(p = x_p^2 + y_p^2\), and \(q^2 = q^2 + 0^2\) via the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity to build any valid composite \(N\).

Box M02.A · How the Patterns Hand Off the Baton

  1. Why P30 (Localize at \(p\)) is needed first: Over \(\mathbb{Z}\), \(x^2 + y^2\) does not factor. Modulo \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), \(-1 \equiv u^2 \pmod p\) has a square root, so \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv x^2 - (uy)^2 = (x - uy)(x + uy) \pmod p\) factors into linear forms!
  2. Why P06 + P13 (or P10) is needed next: Factoring modulo \(p\) only gives \(x^2 + y^2 = k p\) for some multiplier \(k\). Descent (P13) or Pigeonhole (P10) forces \(k = 1\).
  3. Why P06 returns at the end: Once every prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is a sum of two squares, P06 multiplies them together to handle arbitrary composite integers \(N\).

Box M02.B · Three-Key Synthesis Diagnosis

  • Claim type: Classifying which integers \(N\) can be represented by a binary quadratic form \(x^2 + d y^2\) (or counting the number of representations \(r_2(N)\)).
  • Problem shape: Interaction between a multiplicative norm \(N(\alpha\beta) = N(\alpha)N(\beta)\) (P06) and local congruence obstructions modulo \(4\) or \(p\) (P09 + P30).
  • Tools at hand: Brahmagupta–Fibonacci composition (P06), centered modular remainders + descent (P13), Thue's pigeonhole lemma (P10), and fixed-point parity of involutions (P78 + P70).

4 · Worked synthesis exemplars

We now compare three celebrated proofs that every prime \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) is a sum of two squares, followed by the complete classification and counting formula for composite integers \(N = x^2 + y^2\).

Exemplar 1 · Thue's Pigeonhole Grid (P30 + P10 + P15)

Theorem (Axel Thue, 1902). If \(p\) is a prime and \(u \in \mathbb{Z}\) satisfies \(u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p\), then there exist integers \(x, y\) with \(1 \le x, y \le \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor\) such that \(x^2 + y^2 = p\).

Synthesis proof (converting Fermat–Euler's multi-step descent into a single Pigeonhole collision):

  1. Step 1 — Localize at \(p\) via Wilson's theorem (P30): Write \(p = 4n + 1\). In the product \((p-1)! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdots (2n) \cdot (2n+1) \cdots (4n)\), each upper factor \(p - j\) satisfies \(p - j \equiv -j \pmod p\). Setting \(u = (2n)!\), Wilson's theorem \((p-1)! \equiv -1 \pmod p\) gives \[ -1 \;\equiv\; (p-1)! \;\equiv\; \bigl(1 \cdot 2 \cdots 2n\bigr)\bigl((-1)(-2)\cdots(-2n)\bigr) \;=\; (-1)^{2n}\,((2n)!)^2 \;=\; u^2 \pmod p. \] Hence \(p \mid (u^2 + 1)\). (For example, when \(p = 13 = 4(3)+1\), \(u = 6! = 720 \equiv 5 \pmod{13}\), and \(5^2 + 1 = 26 = 2 \times 13\).)
  2. Step 2 — Force a modular collision on a square grid (P10): Let \(m = \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor\). Since \(p\) is prime, \(p\) is not a square, so \(m < \sqrt{p} < m + 1\). Consider the \((m+1) \times (m+1)\) grid of integer pairs \[ G \;=\; \bigl\{(a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 \;:\; 0 \le a \le m,\;\; 0 \le b \le m\bigr\}. \] Because \(|G| = (m + 1)^2 > (\sqrt{p})^2 = p\), there are strictly more grid pairs \((a, b) \in G\) than residue classes in \(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\). By the Pigeonhole Principle (P10), the linear evaluation map \((a, b) \mapsto (a - ub) \bmod p\) must collide on two distinct pairs \((a_1, b_1) \ne (a_2, b_2)\) in \(G\): \[ a_1 - u b_1 \;\equiv\; a_2 - u b_2 \pmod p \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a_1 - a_2) \;\equiv\; u(b_1 - b_2) \pmod p. \]
  3. Step 3 — Square and bound the norm (P15): Set \(x = |a_1 - a_2|\) and \(y = |b_1 - b_2|\). Then \(x^2 \equiv u^2 y^2 \equiv -y^2 \pmod p\), so \(p \mid (x^2 + y^2)\). On the other hand, because \((a_1, b_1) \ne (a_2, b_2)\) and \(0 \le a_i, b_i \le m < \sqrt{p}\), we have \(0 \le x, y \le m\) (not both zero), which squeezes \(x^2 + y^2\) between two consecutive multiples of \(p\): \[ 0 \;<\; x^2 + y^2 \;\le\; m^2 + m^2 \;<\; p + p \;=\; 2p. \] The only multiple of \(p\) strictly between \(0\) and \(2p\) is \(1 \cdot p\) itself! Thus \(x^2 + y^2 = p\). \(\blacksquare\)

Exemplar 2 · Zagier's One-Sentence Dual-Involution Proof (P78 + P70 + P09)

Theorem (Don Zagier, 1990, condensing Roger Heath-Brown, 1984). For any prime \(p = 4n + 1\), the finite set of positive integer triples

\[ S \;=\; \bigl\{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^3 \;:\; x^2 + 4yz = p\bigr\} \]

admits two involutions (\(f \circ f = \mathrm{id}_S\) and \(g \circ g = \mathrm{id}_S\)): an algebraic involution \(f\) with unique fixed point \((1, 1, n)\), and the flip involution \(g(x, y, z) = (x, z, y)\). Therefore \(|S|\) is odd, so \(g\) must also have at least one fixed point \((x, y, y) \in S\), giving \(x^2 + (2y)^2 = p\).

Synthesis proof (Reversible Involution P78 + Parity Double Counting P70):

  • Why \(S\) is finite and why \(g\)-fixed points solve our problem: Since \(x, y, z \ge 1\) and \(x^2 + 4yz = p\), we have \(x < \sqrt{p}\) and \(4yz = p - x^2\), which has only finitely many divisor pairs \((y, z)\). A triple \((x, y, z) \in S\) is fixed by \(g(x, y, z) = (x, z, y)\) if and only if \(y = z\), which says \(x^2 + 4y^2 = x^2 + (2y)^2 = p\)!
  • Zagier's piecewise linear involution \(f : S \to S\) (P78): Define \[ f(x, y, z) \;=\; \begin{cases} (x + 2z,\;\; z,\;\; y - x - z) & \text{if } x < y - z, \\[4pt] (2y - x,\;\; y,\;\; x - y + z) & \text{if } y - z < x < 2y, \\[4pt] (x - 2y,\;\; x - y + z,\;\; y) & \text{if } x > 2y. \end{cases} \] Notice first that the boundary cases \(x = y - z\) and \(x = 2y\) cannot occur in \(S\) because \(p\) is prime: \(x = y - z \implies p = (y-z)^2 + 4yz = (y+z)^2\), while \(x = 2y \implies p = 4y^2 + 4yz = 4y(y+z)\). Second, direct expansion confirms \(x'^2 + 4y'z' = x^2 + 4yz = p\) in all three branches, and applying \(f\) a second time swaps the first and third branches while sending the middle branch to itself!
  • Counting fixed points (P70): Only the middle branch \((2y - x, y, x - y + z)\) can equal \((x, y, z)\), which happens if and only if \(2y - x = x \iff x = y\). Substituting \(x = y\) into \(x^2 + 4yz = p\) gives \(x(x + 4z) = p\). Because \(p = 4n + 1\) is prime, its only positive factorization of this form is \(x = 1\) and \(x + 4z = p \implies z = n\), yielding the unique fixed point \((1, 1, n)\). Since every involution partitions \(S\) into \(2\)-cycles and fixed points, \(|S| \equiv |\mathrm{Fix}(f)| = 1 \pmod 2\) is odd — forcing \(|\mathrm{Fix}(g)| \equiv |S| \equiv 1 \pmod 2\) to be non-empty! \(\blacksquare\)

Exemplar 3 · Composite Numbers & Gaussian Integer Factorization (P06 + P32)

Theorem (Fermat–Euler Composite Criterion & Jacobi's Counting Formula). A positive integer \(N = 2^a \prod_{p \equiv 1(4)} p^{b_p} \prod_{q \equiv 3(4)} q^{c_q}\) is a sum of two integer squares if and only if every exponent \(c_q\) of a prime \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) is even. Moreover, the total number of signed representations \(r_2(N) = \#\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = N\}\) equals

\[ r_2(N) \;=\; 4\,\bigl(d_1(N) \,-\, d_3(N)\bigr), \]

where \(d_1(N)\) and \(d_3(N)\) count the positive divisors of \(N\) congruent to \(1\) and \(3 \pmod 4\), respectively.

Synthesis proof (Gaussian integer norm P06 + Unique factorization in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\)):

  1. Step 1 — Prime divisors \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) must divide both \(x\) and \(y\): Suppose \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) divides \(N = x^2 + y^2\). If \(q \nmid y\), then \(y\) has a multiplicative inverse \(y^{-1} \pmod q\), so \((x y^{-1})^2 \equiv -1 \pmod q\). Raising both sides to the odd power \(\frac{q-1}{2}\) contradicts Euler's criterion (Fermat's Little Theorem): \[ 1 \;\equiv\; (x y^{-1})^{q-1} \;\equiv\; (-1)^{(q-1)/2} \;=\; -1 \pmod q \quad (\text{since } \tfrac{q-1}{2} \text{ is odd for } q \equiv 3 \pmod 4). \] Hence \(q \mid y\) and therefore \(q \mid x\). Dividing \(N = x^2 + y^2\) by \(q^2\) gives \(N/q^2 = (x/q)^2 + (y/q)^2\). Repeating this descent strips out \(q^2\) factors one by one, proving that \(c_q = v_q(N)\) must be even!
  2. Step 2 — Factoring in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\) (P06): In the Euclidean ring of Gaussian integers \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\), writing \(N = x^2 + y^2 = (x + yi)(x - yi) = \mathrm{N}(\alpha)\) is equivalent to finding \(\alpha = x + yi \in \mathbb{Z}[i]\) of norm \(N\). The rational primes split into three types in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\):
    • Ramified prime \(2\): \(2 = (1 + i)(1 - i) = -i(1+i)^2\).
    • Split primes \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 4\): \(p = \pi_p \overline{\pi_p}\) factors into two conjugate Gaussian primes \(\pi_p = a + bi\) and \(\overline{\pi_p} = a - bi\). For \(p^{b_p}\), there are \(b_p + 1\) choices \(\pi_p^j \overline{\pi_p}^{b_p - j}\) (\(0 \le j \le b_p\)) of norm \(p^{b_p}\).
    • Inert primes \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\): \(q\) remains prime in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\) of norm \(q^2\), so \(q^{c_q}\) has a factor of norm \(q^{c_q}\) if and only if \(c_q\) is even (giving the single choice \(q^{c_q/2}\)).
    Multiplying by the \(4\) units \(\{\pm 1, \pm i\}\) gives \(r_2(N) = 4 \prod_{p \equiv 1(4)} (b_p + 1)\) when all \(c_q\) are even (and \(0\) otherwise), which matches \(4(d_1(N) - d_3(N))\)! For instance, for \(N = 65 = 5 \times 13\), we have \(b_5 = b_{13} = 1\), so there are \(r_2(65) = 4(2)(2) = 16\) signed solutions (from \((\pm 8, \pm 1)\) and \((\pm 7, \pm 4)\), plus swaps). \(\blacksquare\)

5 · Where each proof breaks — and why new patterns are born

Testing where Fermat's two-square synthesis breaks reveals three major frontiers of modern number theory:

  • Exhibit A — Why primes \(q \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) fail at Step 1 (No local root of \(-1\)): Try to run Thue's or Fermat–Euler's proof on \(q = 7\) or \(q = 11\). Before any descent or pigeonhole step can begin, we need a seed \(u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod q\). Because the multiplicative group \((\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^\times\) has order \(q - 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 4\), it has no elements of order \(4\), so \(u^2 \equiv -1 \pmod q\) has no solution. And in Zagier's proof, the set \(S = \{x^2 + 4yz = q\}\) is completely empty because \(x^2 + 4yz \equiv x^2 \equiv 1 \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4\)!
  • Exhibit B — Why \(p = x^2 + d y^2\) can fail when \(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-d}]\) lacks unique factorization (\(d = 5, 6, 14\)): For \(d = 2\) or \(d = 3\), Fermat–Euler descent still works (giving \(p = x^2 + 2y^2 \iff p \equiv 1, 3 \pmod 8\) and \(p = x^2 + 3y^2 \iff p = 3\) or \(p \equiv 1 \pmod 3\)). But try \(d = 5\): for \(p = 29 \equiv 1, 9 \pmod{20}\), \(p = 3^2 + 5(2)^2 = 29\) succeeds, whereas for \(p = 3 \text{ or } 7 \pmod{20}\) (such as \(p = 7\)), \(-5 \equiv 2 \equiv 3^2 \pmod 7\) is a quadratic residue modulo \(7\) (\(3^2 + 5(1)^2 = 14 = 2 \times 7\)), yet \(7 \ne x^2 + 5y^2\)! Where does the descent from \(2 \times 7\) to \(1 \times 7\) get stuck? At multiplier \(k = 2\): the centered remainders modulo \(2\) satisfy \(r^2 + 5s^2 = 1^2 + 5(1)^2 = 6 = 3 \times 2\), so the new multiplier is \(m = 3 > 2\) — it grows instead of shrinking! Algebraically, \(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]\) has class number \(h(-20) = 2\) (witnessed by \(6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})\)), so primes split into two classes of quadratic forms: \(p = x^2 + 5y^2\) (for \(p \equiv 1, 9 \pmod{20}\)) versus \(p = 2x^2 + 2xy + 3y^2\) (for \(p \equiv 3, 7 \pmod{20}\)).
  • Exhibit C — Sums of three vs. four squares (Legendre & Lagrange): What if we allow three or four squares? By a modulo-\(8\) invariant (P09), since \(x^2 \in \{0, 1, 4\} \pmod 8\), no integer of the form \(4^a(8b + 7)\) is a sum of three squares (Legendre's Three-Square Theorem, 1798). Moreover, sums of three squares are not closed under multiplication (\(3 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2\) and \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2 + 0^2\), yet \(3 \times 5 = 15 \equiv 7 \pmod 8\) is not a sum of three squares!) because \(\mathbb{R}^3\) has no norm-multiplicative division algebra. In dimension \(4\), however, Hamilton's quaternions give Euler's four-square identity (P06), and Fermat–Euler descent proves Lagrange's Four-Square Theorem (1770): every positive integer is a sum of four integer squares!

Scope note — Why Fermat–Euler descent for \(x^2 + d y^2\) requires \(d \le 3\): In Step 1 of the Fermat–Euler descent modulo \(k\), the centered remainders satisfy \(|r|, |s| \le k/2\), so the new multiplier \(m = \frac{r^2 + d s^2}{k}\) is bounded by \[ m \;\le\; \frac{(k/2)^2 + d(k/2)^2}{k} \;=\; \frac{1 + d}{4}\,k. \] Strict descent (\(m < k\)) is guaranteed for every \(k > 1\) if and only if \(\frac{1+d}{4} \le 1\) (with equality handled separately), which holds for \(d = 1, 2, 3\)! For \(d \ge 5\), \(\frac{1+d}{4} > 1\), and the failure of strict descent corresponds to the nontrivial ideal class group of \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})\).

7 · Lineage interlude

From Diophantus and Brahmagupta to Fermat, Euler, Gauss, and Zagier. The multiplicative identity \((x^2 + y^2)(r^2 + s^2) = (xr \pm ys)^2 + (xs \mp yr)^2\) appears implicitly in Diophantus's Arithmetica (Book III, Problem 19, c. 250 CE) to construct numbers representable as a sum of two squares in multiple ways (such as \(65 = 8^2 + 1^2 = 7^2 + 4^2\)), and in full generality for \(x^2 - d y^2\) in Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE) and Fibonacci's Liber Quadratorum (1225). On December 25, 1640, Pierre de Fermat wrote to Marin Mersenne stating that every prime \(p = 4n + 1\) is a unique sum of two squares, later explaining in a 1659 letter to Pierre de Carcavi that he had proved it by his method of infinite descent (P13).

The first published proof was achieved by Leonhard Euler in two memoirs (1749 and 1754) after seven years of work, using centered modular remainders to descend from \(u^2 + 1 = kp\) to \(x^2 + y^2 = p\). Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1773) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, 1801) placed the theorem inside the theory of binary quadratic forms and Gaussian integers \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\). In 1902, Axel Thue replaced the descent loop with a single Pigeonhole step (P10); and in 1984, Roger Heath-Brown discovered a non-constructive involution proof that Don Zagier (1990) compressed into his famous "one-sentence proof" on \(x^2 + 4yz = p\) (P78).

Exercise M02.1 (●○○) — Running the Fermat–Euler Descent on \(p = 97\). Let \(p = 97 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). Given that \(u = 22\) satisfies \(22^2 + 1^2 = 485 = 5 \times 97\) (multiplier \(k_0 = 5\)), perform the Fermat–Euler descent algorithm step by step to find positive integers \(x, y\) such that \(x^2 + y^2 = 97\).

Hint

Start with \((x_0, y_0) = (22, 1)\) and \(k_0 = 5\). Reduce \(22\) and \(1\) modulo \(5\) into the symmetric interval \([-2, 2]\), compute the new multiplier \(m = (r^2 + s^2)/5\), and apply the Brahmagupta quotient formula \(\bigl(\frac{|x_0 r + y_0 s|}{5},\, \frac{|x_0 s - y_0 r|}{5}\bigr)\).

Worked answer

Starting from \(22^2 + 1^2 = 5 \times 97\) with \(k_0 = 5\):

  1. Centered remainders modulo \(k_0 = 5\): Since \(22 = 4(5) + 2\) and \(1 = 0(5) + 1\), the remainders in \([-2, 2]\) are \(r = 2\) and \(s = 1\).
  2. New multiplier: We compute \[ r^2 + s^2 \;=\; 2^2 + 1^2 \;=\; 5 \;=\; 1 \times 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_1 \;=\; m \;=\; 1! \] The descent reaches \(k_1 = 1\) in a single step!
  3. Brahmagupta–Fibonacci quotient (P06): Dividing by \(k_0 = 5\) yields \[ x_1 \;=\; \frac{|22(2) + 1(1)|}{5} \;=\; \frac{45}{5} \;=\; 9, \qquad y_1 \;=\; \frac{|22(1) - 1(2)|}{5} \;=\; \frac{20}{5} \;=\; 4. \] Check: \(9^2 + 4^2 = 81 + 16 = 97\). \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise M02.2 (●●○) — Tracing Zagier's Involution on \(p = 17\). For \(p = 17 = 4(4) + 1\):

  1. List all positive integer triples \((x, y, z) \in S = \{(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^3 : x^2 + 4yz = 17\}\).
  2. Compute Zagier's involution \(f(x, y, z)\) on each element of \(S\), verify that \((1, 1, 4)\) is the unique fixed point of \(f\), and identify the unique fixed point \((x, y, y)\) of the flip involution \(g(x, y, z) = (x, z, y)\).
Hint

Since \(x \ge 1\) is odd and \(x^2 < 17\), the only possible values for \(x\) are \(x = 1\) (leaving \(4yz = 16 \implies yz = 4\)) and \(x = 3\) (leaving \(4yz = 8 \implies yz = 2\)).

Worked answer
  1. Listing \(S\): Since \(x \in \{1, 3\}\):
    • For \(x = 1\): \(yz = 4\) has \(3\) positive factorizations: \((1, 1, 4),\; (1, 2, 2),\; (1, 4, 1)\).
    • For \(x = 3\): \(yz = 2\) has \(2\) positive factorizations: \((3, 1, 2),\; (3, 2, 1)\).
    Thus \(|S| = 5\) (an odd number of triples).
  2. Action of \(f\) and \(g\):
    • At \((1, 1, 4)\): \(y - z = -3 < 1 < 2 = 2y\) (middle branch), so \(f(1, 1, 4) = (2(1)-1,\, 1,\, 1-1+4) = \mathbf{(1, 1, 4)}\) (unique \(f\)-fixed point!).
    • At \((1, 4, 1)\) and \((3, 1, 2)\): \(f(1, 4, 1) = (1 + 2(1),\, 1,\, 4 - 1 - 1) = (3, 1, 2)\), and \(f(3, 1, 2) = (3 - 2(1),\, 3 - 1 + 2,\, 1) = (1, 4, 1)\) (a \(2\)-cycle).
    • At \((1, 2, 2)\) and \((3, 2, 1)\): \(f(3, 2, 1) = (2(2) - 3,\, 2,\, 3 - 2 + 1) = (1, 2, 2)\), and \(f(1, 2, 2) = (2(2) - 1,\, 2,\, 1 - 2 + 2) = (3, 2, 1)\) (a \(2\)-cycle).
    Meanwhile, the flip map \(g(x, y, z) = (x, z, y)\) fixes the unique triple with \(y = z\), namely \((1, 2, 2)\), which gives \(1^2 + (2 \cdot 2)^2 = 1^2 + 4^2 = 17\)! \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise M02.3 (●●●) — Multi-Way Sums of Two Squares via Brahmagupta Composition (P06). Let \(N = 1105 = 5 \times 13 \times 17\).

  1. Use Jacobi's formula (or prime splitting in \(\mathbb{Z}[i]\)) to determine how many representations \(N = x^2 + y^2\) exist with \(0 < x < y\).
  2. Given \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2\), \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\), and \(17 = 4^2 + 1^2\), apply the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity twice to find all four representations of \(1105 = x^2 + y^2\) with \(0 < x < y\).
Hint

First combine \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2\) and \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\) into two distinct representations of \(65 = 8^2 + 1^2 = 7^2 + 4^2\), then compose each of those with \(17 = 4^2 + 1^2\) using both signs \((xr \pm ys)^2 + (xs \mp yr)^2\).

Worked answer
  1. Counting representations: Since \(1105 = 5^1 \cdot 13^1 \cdot 17^1\) is a product of three distinct primes \(\equiv 1 \pmod 4\), Jacobi's formula gives \(r_2(1105) = 4(1+1)(1+1)(1+1) = 32\) signed pairs \((x, y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\). Because \(1105\) isNeither a square nor twice a square, every representation has \(0 < |x| \ne |y|\), and the \(8\) symmetries \((\pm x, \pm y), (\pm y, \pm x)\) partition the \(32\) pairs into \(32 / 8 = \mathbf{4}\) unordered positive pairs \(0 < x < y\).
  2. Computing all four pairs via P06:
    • First, compose \(5 = 2^2 + 1^2\) with \(13 = 3^2 + 2^2\): \[ (2\cdot 3 + 1\cdot 2)^2 + (2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 3)^2 \;=\; 8^2 + 1^2 \;=\; 65, \qquad (2\cdot 3 - 1\cdot 2)^2 + (2\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 3)^2 \;=\; 4^2 + 7^2 \;=\; 65. \]
    • Next, compose \((8, 1)\) with \((4, 1)\) (\(17 = 4^2 + 1^2\)): \[ (8\cdot 4 + 1\cdot 1)^2 + (8\cdot 1 - 1\cdot 4)^2 \;=\; \mathbf{33^2 + 4^2} \;=\; 1089 + 16 \;=\; 1105, \] \[ (8\cdot 4 - 1\cdot 1)^2 + (8\cdot 1 + 1\cdot 4)^2 \;=\; \mathbf{31^2 + 12^2} \;=\; 961 + 144 \;=\; 1105. \]
    • Finally, compose \((7, 4)\) with \((4, 1)\): \[ (7\cdot 4 + 4\cdot 1)^2 + |7\cdot 1 - 4\cdot 4|^2 \;=\; \mathbf{32^2 + 9^2} \;=\; 1024 + 81 \;=\; 1105, \] \[ (7\cdot 4 - 4\cdot 1)^2 + (7\cdot 1 + 4\cdot 4)^2 \;=\; \mathbf{24^2 + 23^2} \;=\; 576 + 529 \;=\; 1105. \]
    Thus the four unordered positive representations \((x, y)\) with \(0 < x < y\) are \((4, 33),\; (9, 32),\; (12, 31),\; (23, 24)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise M02.4 (◆) — Pattern-Unknown Synthesis Diagnosis: Primes of the Form \(x^2 + 2y^2\). Prove that every prime \(p \equiv 1 \text{ or } 3 \pmod 8\) can be written in the form \(p = x^2 + 2y^2\) for integers \(x, y\) by identifying and executing the appropriate synthesis of patterns from this chapter (given that \(-2\) is a quadratic residue modulo every prime \(p \equiv 1, 3 \pmod 8\)).

Worked answer

Diagnosis: We synthesize Localize at \(p\) (P30) + Thue's Pigeonhole Grid (P10) + Bound Both Sides (P15) (or Fermat–Euler descent with the weighted norm identity \((x^2 + 2y^2)(r^2 + 2s^2) = (xr + 2ys)^2 + 2(xs - yr)^2\) from P06):

  1. Local seed (P30): Since \(p \equiv 1 \text{ or } 3 \pmod 8\), there exists \(u \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(u^2 \equiv -2 \pmod p\).
  2. Pigeonhole collision (P10): Let \(m = \lfloor\sqrt{p}\rfloor\). As in Exemplar 1, the grid \(G = \{0, 1, \dots, m\}^2\) has \((m+1)^2 > p\) pairs \((a, b)\), so two distinct pairs collide modulo \(p\) under \((a, b) \mapsto a - ub \bmod p\). Their difference \((x, y) = (|a_1 - a_2|, |b_1 - b_2|) \ne (0, 0)\) satisfies \(x \equiv \pm u y \pmod p\), so \[ x^2 + 2y^2 \;\equiv\; u^2 y^2 + 2y^2 \;=\; (u^2 + 2)y^2 \;\equiv\; 0 \pmod p. \]
  3. Bounding \(x^2 + 2y^2\) (P15) and handling \(k = 2\): Because \(0 \le x, y \le m < \sqrt{p}\) (not both zero), we have \[ 0 \;<\; x^2 + 2y^2 \;<\; p + 2p \;=\; 3p. \] Hence \(x^2 + 2y^2\) equals either \(p\) or \(2p\):
    • If \(x^2 + 2y^2 = p\), we are done!
    • If \(x^2 + 2y^2 = 2p\), then \(x^2 = 2(p - y^2)\) is even, so \(x = 2z\) is even. Substituting \(x = 2z\) into \(x^2 + 2y^2 = 2p\) gives \(4z^2 + 2y^2 = 2p\), and dividing by \(2\) yields \(y^2 + 2z^2 = p\)! \(\blacksquare\)

Exercise M02.5 (◐) — Exploration: Why Every Prime Divides a Sum of Three Squares (\(w^2 + x^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p\)). In Lagrange's Four-Square Theorem (Exhibit C), the descent starts from a multiple \(w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = kp\) with \(1 \le k < p\). Prove using the Pigeonhole Principle (P10) that for every odd prime \(p\) (even when \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\), where \(x^2 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod p\) has no solution!), there exist integers \(w, x \in \{0, 1, \dots, \frac{p-1}{2}\}\) such that

\[ w^2 + x^2 + 1 \;\equiv\; 0 \pmod p. \]
Hint

Rewrite the congruence as \(w^2 \equiv -1 - x^2 \pmod p\), and count the size of the two sets \(A = \{w^2 \bmod p : 0 \le w \le \frac{p-1}{2}\}\) and \(B = \{(-1 - x^2) \bmod p : 0 \le x \le \frac{p-1}{2}\}\) inside the \(p\)-element set \(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\).

Worked answer
  1. Distinctness of squares on \(\{0, 1, \dots, \frac{p-1}{2}\}\): If \(0 \le w_1 < w_2 \le \frac{p-1}{2}\), then \(0 < w_2 - w_1 < w_2 + w_1 < p\), so \(p \nmid (w_2 - w_1)(w_2 + w_1) = w_2^2 - w_1^2\). Thus the map \(w \mapsto w^2 \bmod p\) is injective on \(\{0, 1, \dots, \frac{p-1}{2}\}\).
  2. Pigeonhole overlap (P10): Consequently, both sets of residues modulo \(p\), \[ A \;=\; \Bigl\{w^2 \bmod p \;:\; 0 \le w \le \frac{p-1}{2}\Bigr\} \qquad\text{and}\qquad B \;=\; \Bigl\{(-1 - x^2) \bmod p \;:\; 0 \le x \le \frac{p-1}{2}\Bigr\}, \] have cardinality \(|A| = |B| = \frac{p-1}{2} + 1 = \frac{p+1}{2}\). Since \(A\) and \(B\) are subsets of \(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}\) (which has only \(p\) elements) and \[ |A| + |B| \;=\; \frac{p+1}{2} + \frac{p+1}{2} \;=\; p + 1 \;>\; p, \] the Pigeonhole Principle guarantees that \(A \cap B \ne \emptyset\)! Choosing \(w^2 \equiv -1 - x^2 \pmod p\) in \(A \cap B\) gives \(w^2 + x^2 + 1^2 + 0^2 = kp\) with \(0 < w^2 + x^2 + 1 < 2\bigl(\frac{p}{2}\bigr)^2 + 1 < p^2\), seeding Lagrange's four-square descent with \(1 \le k < p\) for every odd prime \(p\). \(\blacksquare\)
