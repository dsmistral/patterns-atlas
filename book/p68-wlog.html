The Patterns Atlas · P68 · the eleventh move of tier 2

Without loss of generality

Some claims come in families of cases that are clones of one another: reorder the variables and the claim says exactly the same thing. Walking every case by hand is P25's machine grinding through copies. The move: name the symmetry, prove the claim is invariant under it, pick one representative case, and prove that — once. The two words without loss of generality are the receipt for the whole transaction, and they are a debt, not a discount: the invariance is a lemma to prove, the case map is a check to run, and a symmetry of the hypotheses that is not a symmetry of the conclusion buys nothing at all. This page is P08's promise called in — the proof-side phrase P08 explicitly set aside.

New word: invariant. A quantity or a claim is invariant under a move if the move changes nothing that matters: apply the move and the claim's truth value — and usually its whole form — is untouched. The claim "for positive \(a, b, c\), \(\frac{a}{b+c} + \frac{b}{c+a} + \frac{c}{a+b} \geq \frac{3}{2}\)" is invariant under any reordering of \(a, b, c\): every term is a variable divided by the sum of the other two, and the sum does not care which name it wears. New word: representative. A representative is one case chosen to stand for a whole family of cases that the invariance has declared interchangeable. P08 picked representatives to count orbits; here we pick one to prove the claim for its entire orbit. New word: wlog. "Without loss of generality", abbreviated wlog, is the licensed declaration that a representative's proof covers its whole family. It is not a slogan to say — it is a three-part obligation to discharge: name the moves, check the invariance, map every case to its representative. Skip any part and the words are decoration on a gap.

1 · Before we begin

From P08 · Divide by symmetry: "Pattern P08 — Divide by symmetry. When the problem's objects come in symmetry-tied families and the question treats every member of a family identically: (1) name the moves that leave the question unchanged — those are the symmetries; (2) pick one representative per family and do the work on it, once; (3) cash out by the ledger — multiply the representative's answer by the family size, or divide the brute-force total by it. The family of clones one object generates under the symmetries is its orbit."

Or, in words: twins do twin work. When a rotation, a swap, or a flip hands you identical copies of the same case, do the case once and bill the copies — but keep honest books. The ledger — exactly how many copies a family holds — is the mathematical heart of the move, and it is a fact to prove, not a habit to assume. A division by a number you did not prove is a guess wearing a fraction bar.

P08 counted. Its exemplars picked one triangle, one seating, one coloring, and multiplied by the ledger — because the question ("how many?") treats every member of an orbit identically, so the members' answers are equal and summable. But P08 stopped at the counting door and wrote down what it saw inside. Its scope aside said it outright: the proof-side phrase for "one representative, argued honestly for its whole orbit" — without loss of generality — is important enough to be its own pattern. This page is that pattern.

The tension is in the word loss. A wlog declaration claims that restricting to a representative case loses nothing — that every other case is the representative wearing different names. Sometimes that is exactly true, and the proof is half as long as the census. Sometimes it is false in a way that looks true: the claim is invariant under cyclic shuffles but not under all shuffles, or invariant under scaling but only when the scale is positive, or the "symmetry" moves one hypothesis while quietly breaking the conclusion. The move's discipline is to earn the words every time — and §5 collects the ways unearned wlogs collapse.

2 · Meet the problem: six cases that are one case

Here is a claim with real teeth. For positive reals \(a, b, c\), Schur's inequality of degree one:

\[S \;=\; a(a-b)(a-c) \;+\; b(b-c)(b-a) \;+\; c(c-a)(c-b) \;\geq\; 0.\]

Try it forward and you are handling three terms whose signs depend on the ordering of \(a, b, c\). With \(a \geq b\): the first term is nonnegative, the second is nonpositive, the third is nonnegative — but which dominates? The brute-force answer is a census: fix the multiset \(\{1, 3, 5\}\) and run all six orderings.

The census, all six orderings of \(\{1, 3, 5\}\). Read the last column: it never moves. Six cases, one value — the expression does not care about the ordering at all.
\((a,b,c)\)\(a(a-b)(a-c)\)\(b(b-c)(b-a)\)\(c(c-a)(c-b)\)\(S\)
\((1,3,5)\)\(1 \cdot (-2)(-4) = 8\)\(3 \cdot (-2)(2) = -12\)\(5 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 40\)\(36\)
\((1,5,3)\)\(1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 8\)\(5 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 40\)\(3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) = -12\)\(36\)
\((3,1,5)\)\(3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) = -12\)\(1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 8\)\(5 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 40\)\(36\)
\((3,5,1)\)\(3 \cdot (-2)(2) = -12\)\(5 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 40\)\(1 \cdot (-2)(-4) = 8\)\(36\)
\((5,1,3)\)\(5 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 40\)\(1 \cdot (-2)(-4) = 8\)\(3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) = -12\)\(36\)
\((5,3,1)\)\(5 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 = 40\)\(3 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) = -12\)\(1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 8\)\(36\)

Six cases, one value: \(S = 36\) every time. The census has discovered that \(S\) is a symmetric expression — every reordering of the variables permutes the three terms among themselves, so the sum is invariant under the whole of \(S_{3}\). That is the census doing P08's job: measuring the orbit and finding it is one orbit, six deep.

Now the proof-side move. Since every ordering is a clone of every other, without loss of generality, take \(a \geq b \geq c\). In that one ordering the signs are fixed — \(a - b \geq 0\), \(a - c \geq 0\), \(b - c \geq 0\), so the first and third terms of \(S\) are nonnegative and only the middle term, \(b(b-c)(b-a)\), is nonpositive. Pair it against the first term:

\[S \;=\; (a-b)\bigl[\,a(a-c) - b(b-c)\,\bigr] \;+\; c(a-c)(b-c),\]

and the bracket factors in turn:

\[a(a-c) - b(b-c) \;=\; (a^{2} - ac) - (b^{2} - bc) \;=\; (a-b)(a + b - c).\]

So

\[S \;=\; (a-b)^{2}\,(a + b - c) \;+\; c\,(a-c)(b-c).\]

Read it: both terms are products of nonnegative factors — \((a-b)^{2} \geq 0\), \(a + b - c \geq a \geq 0\), \(c > 0\), \(a - c \geq 0\), \(b - c \geq 0\). So \(S \geq 0\), proved. Check it against the census's number: \((5, 3, 1)\) gives \((5-3)^{2}(5+3-1) + 1 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 4 \cdot 7 + 8 = 36\) — the census's value, exactly, from the representative case.

Stop and notice what the wlog declaration bought, and what it cost. Bought: one case instead of six — and not six copies of the argument, either; the six forward "arguments" would each have to re-derive sign bookkeeping that the representative does once. Cost: two lemmas that the census already paid — \(S\) is invariant under reordering (the six equal values), and every ordering maps to the representative \(a \geq b \geq c\) by one of those reorderings (trivially: reorder). When the census is this cheap, the wlog is nearly free; the next sections show the price when it is not.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P68 — Without loss of generality. When a claim is invariant under a group of moves on its variables — reorderings, swaps, sign flips, scalings, rigid motions — do not prove every case: (1) name the moves under which the whole claim (hypotheses and conclusion together) is invariant; (2) check that every case reaches a representative case by those moves; (3) prove the representative, once, and declare wlog. The declaration is a receipt for two discharged obligations, not a permission slip to skip them.

When the claim's cases are clones — related by moves that preserve the claim's truth value and its proof burden — the census of P25 is grinding through copies, and P08's ledger already told you the copies bill identically. Do not force the census. Instead: (1) Name the invariance. Write the move precisely — "swap any two of \(a, b, c\)", "scale all three by \(t > 0\)", "translate and rotate the figure" — and check it against the whole claim: every hypothesis and the conclusion. A move that preserves the hypotheses but twists the conclusion is not a symmetry of the claim; a move that preserves the conclusion but needs a hypothesis the other cases lack is not a symmetry either. (2) Map the cases. Show every case reaches the representative by the named moves — for orderings this is immediate (any ordering is a permutation), for scalings it needs positivity, for geometries it needs the rigid motions. If some case cannot be carried there, the wlog is false and the case must be walked. (3) Prove once, declare honestly. Prove the representative and write "without loss of generality" — the words are the receipt that (1) and (2) were discharged. Where the invariance is only partial (a subgroup, a positivity constraint), say so in the same breath.

In words: interchangeable cases deserve one argument. The move is P08's ledger turned from counting to proving: instead of billing each clone its answer, bill the whole orbit one proof. The price moved, not vanished — P08 paid it as "how many clones?", this page pays it as "is the claim truly indifferent to the swap?" — and the second question is where the traps live, because indifference is a property of the whole claim and the traps are always in the part you stopped checking.

The two-word declaration sits inside a two-by-two of honesty:

When the words are a receipt and when they are a leak.

The three-key diagnosis, in this page's own terms: the claim type — a statement about variables whose roles the claim does not distinguish, or a geometry whose question survives rigid motions. the problem shape — a brute-force census whose rows are visibly copies of one another, or a forward argument that keeps re-deriving the same sign bookkeeping per ordering. the tools at hand — P08's orbit picture for the invariance, P02's small cases to expose the clone structure, and P25's machine as the honest fallback when the case map fails.

4 · Worked exemplars

Moves used: P68 throughout — P08's orbit ledger, P10's forced collision, P02's small cases, and the homogeneity check of §5 run as a gate.

Exemplar A — Schur, finished by one representative. Return to §2's census with everything §3 built. The claim: \(S = a(a-b)(a-c) + b(b-c)(b-a) + c(c-a)(c-b) \geq 0\) for positive \(a, b, c\). The forward road re-runs the sign bookkeeping per ordering — six times, or \(3! \cdot k\) times as the census grows. The wlog: \(S\) is symmetric (each reordering permutes the three summands — check one swap: \(a \leftrightarrow b\) exchanges the first two terms and maps the third to itself with \(c-a, c-b \to c-b, c-a\), unchanged as a product), so every ordering reaches \(a \geq b \geq c\) by a reordering — obligation (2) is free. Obligation (3) is §2's factorization:

\[S \;=\; (a-b)^{2}(a + b - c) \;+\; c(a-c)(b-c) \;\geq\; 0,\]

both summands nonnegative in the representative ordering. Collect: Schur holds for all positive \(a, b, c\). Notice the proof never mentioned the other five orderings — the invariance carried them. And notice what the representative ordering bought concretely: fixed signs, which is what let the pairing \((a-b)\) factor out at all. Under any other announced ordering the same factorization works with the names rotated — that "with the names rotated" is the invariance, spoken aloud.

Exemplar B — the party of six, three wlogs deep. The claim, from combinatorics: at any gathering of six people, some three are mutual acquaintances or some three are mutual strangers. Model: color each pair of six vertices red (acquainted) or blue (strangers); claim: a monochromatic triangle exists. Brute force: \(2^{15} = 32768\) colorings of the fifteen pairs — a census, and a big one. The wlogs: (i) the claim is invariant under swapping the two colors everywhere — "acquainted" and "strangers" enter the statement identically — so wlog, person 1 has at least three red edges or at least three blue edges; this is where P10 forces the split, since person 1 has five edges and \(5 = 2 + 3\). (ii) By the color swap again, wlog it is three red edges — to persons \(A, B, C\). Now the case work is two cases, not thousands: if any pair among \(A, B, C\) is red, that pair with person 1 is a red triangle; if none is, \(A, B, C\) are three mutual strangers — a blue triangle. Either way the claim holds. Two wlogs — the color swap, applied twice — collapsed \(32768\) cases to two, and each wlog was a genuine invariance: the statement's "or" treats the colors identically, so the swap maps colorings to colorings and truths to truths. The census of \(2^{15}\) colorings is machine-checkable and confirms it: every coloring of \(K_{6}\)'s fifteen edges holds a monochromatic triangle, no exceptions.

Exemplar C — Nesbitt, scaled to one size. The claim: for positive \(a, b, c\), \(\frac{a}{b+c} + \frac{b}{c+a} + \frac{c}{a+b} \geq \frac{3}{2}\). The forward road: three fractions with different denominators and no common scale — stony. The wlog that unlocks it is a scaling: the claim is homogeneous of degree zero — replace \((a,b,c)\) by \((ta, tb, tc)\) and each fraction is unchanged (\(ta/t(b+c) = a/(b+c)\)), so the claim is invariant under all positive scalings. Every triple with \(a + b + c = s > 0\) reaches the representative \(a + b + c = 1\) by scaling with \(t = 1/s\) — obligation (2) discharged, positivity of \(s\) doing the work. In the representative, \(b + c = 1 - a\), so the claim reads \(\sum \frac{a}{1-a} \geq \frac{3}{2}\) with \(a, b, c > 0\), \(a + b + c = 1\). Now each term is a one-variable function: \(\frac{x}{1-x}\) is convex on \((0,1)\), and its tangent line at \(x = \frac{1}{3}\) — the symmetric point — sits below it everywhere:

\[\frac{x}{1-x} \;=\; \frac{1}{2} + \frac{9}{4}\Bigl(x - \frac{1}{3}\Bigr) + \frac{(3x-1)^{2}}{4(1-x)} \;\geq\; \frac{1}{2} + \frac{9}{4}\Bigl(x - \frac{1}{3}\Bigr),\]

the last term being a square over a positive denominator. Summing the three tangent inequalities: \(\sum \frac{a}{1-a} \geq \frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{4}\bigl((a+b+c) - 1\bigr) = \frac{3}{2}\) — the correction term dying exactly because the scaling wlog normalized the sum to \(1\). Collect: Nesbitt holds, and equality holds precisely at \(a = b = c\), where every square \((3x-1)^{2}\) vanishes. The wlog did not prove the claim — the tangent line did — but it made the claim one-dimensional: without the normalization there is no single point to hang a tangent on.

Exemplar D — Apollonius, pinned to the origin. The claim, from geometry: the sum of the squares on two sides of a triangle equals twice the square on the median to the third side plus half the square on that third side — \(AB^{2} + AC^{2} = 2\,AM^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,BC^{2}\), with \(M\) the midpoint of \(BC\). The forward road: a generic triangle in a generic position carries six coordinates of bookkeeping. The wlog is the rigid-motion invariance: every distance in the claim is preserved by translations and rotations, so the claim is invariant under placing the figure anywhere. Every triangle reaches the representative with \(M\) at the origin and \(BC\) on the axis: \(B = (-d, 0)\), \(C = (d, 0)\), \(A = (x, y)\) — obligation (2) is Euclid's own superposition move. In the representative the computation is three lines: \(AB^{2} = (x+d)^{2} + y^{2}\), \(AC^{2} = (x-d)^{2} + y^{2}\), and adding gives \(2x^{2} + 2y^{2} + 2d^{2} = 2\,AM^{2} + 2d^{2}\), while \(BC^{2} = 4d^{2}\) — so \(AB^{2} + AC^{2} = 2\,AM^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,BC^{2}\), collected. The wlog bought coordinate poverty: six coordinates became two, because the claim's invariance meant the other four were describing where the figure sits, not what the figure is.

5 · When the trick breaks

Cyclic is not symmetric — the half-symmetry trap. The most seductive failure: a claim invariant under cyclic shuffles \((a,b,c) \to (b,c,a)\) but not under all shuffles, wearing a wlog meant for the full symmetric group. Take \(f(a,b,c) = (a-b)(b-c)(a-c)\). Cyclically shifted: \((b-c)(c-a)(b-a) = (b-c) \cdot (-(a-c)) \cdot (-(a-b)) = f\) — invariant. Swapped \(a \leftrightarrow b\): \((b-a)(a-c)(b-c) = -f\) — the sign flips. So \(f(1,2,3) = (-1)(-1)(-2) = -2\), but \(f(2,1,3) = (1)(-2)(-1) = 2\): the orbit under full \(S_{3}\) splits into two three-element orbits with opposite signs, and "wlog \(a \geq b\)" — which requires swapping — crosses between them, proving one orbit's sign and billing the other. Any claim whose sign or shape distinguishes even permutations from odd ones (alternating expressions, oriented areas, determinants) lives in this trap. The audit question: name the exact group you are using, and check the claim under each of its generators — a cyclic check is three checks, not one, and the swap is the one that breaks.

Double-spending a symmetry on paired data. The rearrangement setting: two lists \(a_{1} \leq \cdots \leq a_{n}\) and \(b_{1} \leq \cdots \leq b_{n}\), and claims about the pairing \(\sum a_{i} b_{i}\). The trap: "wlog both lists are sorted" — sorting each list independently applies two different permutations, one to each list, and the pairing between them is data, not symmetry. The claim's invariance (if any) is under one permutation applied to both lists simultaneously, which preserves the pairing and cannot sort both lists at once unless they were already comonotone. Unearned, the double sort silently replaces the given pairing by a different one — often the extremal one, which is the theorem (P71's territory), not the assumption. The repair: sort one list by a genuine relabeling symmetry if the claim has one, and let the other list fall where the data puts it — or prove the rearrangement inequality itself, which is exactly the statement that the sorted pairing is extremal.

The scaling trap: zero, signs, and the gate. Exemplar C's scaling wlog divided by \(s = a + b + c\) — legitimate because positivity was a hypothesis, so \(s > 0\). Drop the hypothesis and the same move divides by zero on the triple \((0, 0, 0)\) and by a negative on \((-1, -2, -3)\), where \(t = 1/s < 0\) reverses every inequality the representative proof runs. The general gate: a scaling, normalization, or substitution inside a wlog must be checked for (i) definedness — the divisor is nonzero on the whole case family; (ii) monotonicity — the map preserves the direction of every inequality used; (iii) surjectivity onto the representative — every case genuinely reaches it. Fail any of the three and the wlog proves the claim for a subset while the words claim it for all. Sign-flipping is the classic (ii): \(x \mapsto -x\) is a symmetry of \(|x|\) but reverses order, so "wlog \(x \geq y\)" survives a negation of both variables only if the claim is indifferent to the reversal — which is precisely what was being assumed.

Honest scope. This page covers wlog as P08's proof-side shadow: an invariance named, a case map checked, a representative proved once. It does not cover the machinery that constructs canonical representatives for infinite symmetry groups — group actions with continuous families, quotient spaces, gauge choices and their ghosts — beyond noting in §7 where that machinery lives. And it does not license the lazy wlog: every declaration on this page carries its two obligations on its face, and §5's failures are all obligations skipped in a specific, nameable way. When no honest invariance exists, the census — P25's machine, P02's small cases — is not a defeat; it is the move that does not need to apologize.

6 · What to try next

Escalation ladder. If the cases are not clones — no invariance, no representative — the census is honest work: P25 runs the machine, P02 keeps it small. If the invariance is partial — a subgroup, a sign constraint — narrow the claim to the reachable cases and walk the rest explicitly, or find the second symmetry that finishes the map (Exemplar B's color swap used twice). If the representative proof needs a quantity extremal, P71 is the natural next door: the sorted pairing of §5's second failure is its home ground.

Doors this page opens. P69 · Telescoping sums — the tangent-line identity of Exemplar C is a telescoping sum in disguise, and the next page makes the disguise official. P71 · The extremal principle — "wlog take the largest" is the move's most common instance, and it deserves its own pattern. And on the far side of tier 2, M04 — Exemplar B's party of six, grown up: Ramsey theory, where the wlog-and-pigeonhole stack of this page becomes the standard opening.

7 · Lineage interlude

The move is as old as proof itself, because it is proof's own economy. Euclid's fourth Common Notion — "things that coincide with one another are equal to one another" — is the license, and Proposition I.4 (side-angle-side) is the first recorded wlog: to prove two triangles congruent, Euclid superposes one on the other, moving the figure without changing what matters about it — a rigid motion declared without loss of generality, circa 300 BC. The algebraic form arrived with the study of equations whose roots are interchangeable: Joseph-Louis Lagrange's 1770 Réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations analyzed expressions by how they behave under permuting the roots, and Augustin-Louis Cauchy's 1815 memoir on permutation groups made the invariance itself the object of study — the moment "which shuffles leave the claim alone?" became mathematics. Felix Klein's Erlangen program of 1872 generalized the move to all of geometry: a geometry is a space together with its group of invariances, so every geometric wlog is a choice of representative in that group's terms. Hermann Weyl's 1925–26 work on the representation theory of Lie groups built the canonical-representative machinery — Weyl chambers — that §5's honest scope pointed at: the systematic answer to "which case may stand for the orbit" when the orbit is infinite. And the modern physics chapters are the same move under pressure: gauge symmetries are invariances so abundant that choosing a representative ("fixing the gauge") becomes its own subtle art — Yang and Mills's 1954 theory made the invariance the law, and Faddeev and Popov's 1967 prescription handled the double-counting that over-abundant symmetry inflicts on the census — P08's ledger, audited by physicists.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — The distance identity. Claim: for real numbers \(a, b, c\), \(|a-b| + |b-c| + |c-a| = 2(\max - \min)\). (a) Prove it by wlog: state the invariance, name the representative ordering, and run the computation. (b) Check the census on \((3, 1, 4)\) and \((2, 5, 2)\) against your formula. (c) The audit: why does reordering the three numbers not change the left side? Point at the exact reason — what does \(|x - y|\) not care about?

Hint

In the representative \(a \geq b \geq c\): \(|a-b| = a-b\), \(|b-c| = b-c\), \(|c-a| = a-c\). Sum and collect.

Worked answer

(a) The claim is invariant under any reordering: the left side is a sum of pairwise distances, and a distance does not care which endpoint has the bigger name; the right side is symmetric outright. Wlog \(a \geq b \geq c\): then \(|a-b| + |b-c| + |c-a| = (a-b) + (b-c) + (a-c) = 2a - 2c = 2(\max - \min)\). (b) \((3,1,4)\): \(|2| + |{-3}| + |{-1}| = 2 + 3 + 1 = 6\), and \(2(4-1) = 6\). \((2,5,2)\): \(3 + 3 + 0 = 6\), and \(2(5-2) = 6\). (c) Each term \(|x-y|\) is invariant under \(x \leftrightarrow y\) individually, and the sum runs over all three pairs, so a relabeling permutes the terms — the sum is symmetric under all of \(S_{3}\), the strongest possible invariance, which is why the wlog here is completely free: the case map is the identity on truths.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The cyclic trap, met head-on. Claim: for positive \(a, b, c\), \(\frac{a^{2}}{b} + \frac{b^{2}}{c} + \frac{c^{2}}{a} \geq a + b + c\). (a) Decide: is the claim invariant under all reorderings, under cyclic shuffles only, or under neither? Check \(f(a,b,c) = \frac{a^{2}}{b} + \frac{b^{2}}{c} + \frac{c^{2}}{a}\) against the swap \(a \leftrightarrow b\) numerically at \((1, 2, 4)\). (b) Prove the claim without any wlog, by proving the identity \(\frac{a^{2}}{b} + \frac{b^{2}}{c} + \frac{c^{2}}{a} - (a + b + c) = \frac{(a-b)^{2}}{b} + \frac{(b-c)^{2}}{c} + \frac{(c-a)^{2}}{a}\). (c) The confession: a classmate writes "wlog \(a \geq b \geq c\)" and proves the claim in that ordering. Diagnose precisely what their proof establishes and what it does not — which sentences did they prove, and for which cases?

Hint

For (b): multiply out the right side term by term — \(\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{b} = \frac{a^{2}}{b} - 2a + b\), and the pieces telescope across the sum. For (a): \((1,2,4)\) vs \((2,1,4)\).

Worked answer

(a) Cyclic shuffles only, and the numeric check shows it: \(f(1,2,4) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{4} + \frac{16}{1} = 17.5\), while \(f(2,1,4) = \frac{4}{1} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{16}{2} = 12.25\). A swap changed the value — the claim is not symmetric, and "wlog \(a \geq b\)" is unavailable. (b) Multiplying out: \(\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{b} = \frac{a^{2}}{b} - 2a + b\); \(\frac{(b-c)^{2}}{c} = \frac{b^{2}}{c} - 2b + c\); \(\frac{(c-a)^{2}}{a} = \frac{c^{2}}{a} - 2c + a\). The sum: \(\frac{a^{2}}{b} + \frac{b^{2}}{c} + \frac{c^{2}}{a} - (a + b + c)\) — the linear terms collect to exactly \(-(a+b+c)\). Each squared term is nonnegative (positive variables, squares), so the difference is \(\geq 0\): the claim holds, no ordering assumed. (c) The classmate's proof establishes the claim for the cases where \(a \geq b \geq c\) and their cyclic shuffles — if their argument used only cyclic-invariant steps, it covers half of each orbit; if it used the swap anywhere, it covers only the ordered sixth of the case space. Either way the remaining cases are unproved: at \((2,1,4)\) the claim happens to hold (12.25 ≥ 7), but nothing in the classmate's argument shows it. This is §5's first failure mode with the diagnosis attached: the proof is honest for its orbit and silent about the rest.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The substitution ladder. Claim (IMO 1995, Problem 2): for positive reals \(a, b, c\) with \(abc = 1\), \(\frac{1}{a^{3}(b+c)} + \frac{1}{b^{3}(c+a)} + \frac{1}{c^{3}(a+b)} \geq \frac{3}{2}\). (a) Show the substitution \(x = \frac{1}{a}\), \(y = \frac{1}{b}\), \(z = \frac{1}{c}\) turns the constraint into \(xyz = 1\) and each term into \(\frac{x^{2}}{y+z}\) — carefully: \(\frac{1}{a^{3}(b+c)} = \frac{x^{3}}{\frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z}} = \frac{x^{3}yz}{y+z}\), and \(x^{3}yz = x^{2}(xyz) = x^{2}\). (b) Prove \(\sum \frac{x^{2}}{y+z} \geq \frac{(x+y+z)^{2}}{2(x+y+z)} = \frac{x+y+z}{2}\) — this is Cauchy's inequality in the form \(\sum \frac{u_{i}^{2}}{v_{i}} \geq \frac{(\sum u_{i})^{2}}{\sum v_{i}}\) with \(v_{i} > 0\); verify the Cauchy step directly by cross-multiplying. (c) Finish by AM–GM: \(xyz = 1\) forces \(x + y + z \geq 3\), so the sum is \(\geq \frac{3}{2}\), equality iff \(x = y = z = 1\), i.e. \(a = b = c = 1\). (d) The audit: where in this proof did a wlog or normalization operate, and what discharged its obligations? (The substitution is a change of variables, not a wlog — say what the difference is, and why the proof needed no case map.)

Hint

For (b): expand \(\sum \frac{x^{2}}{y+z} \cdot 2(x+y+z) - (x+y+z)^{2}\) and collect into \(\sum \frac{(x(y+z) - y(y+z)\cdots)^{2}}{\cdot}\) — or verify the equivalent form \(\sum_{sym} \frac{(x-y)^{2}}{(x+z)(y+z)} \geq 0\). Keep the algebra honest; the machine census in the receipt confirms the final inequality at \((1,2,\frac{1}{2})\) and \((3,1,\frac{1}{3})\).

Worked answer

(a) With \(a = \frac{1}{x}\) etc.: \(abc = \frac{1}{xyz} = 1 \iff xyz = 1\), and \(\frac{1}{a^{3}(b+c)} = \frac{x^{3}}{(1/y) + (1/z)} = \frac{x^{3} yz}{y + z} = \frac{x^{2}(xyz)}{y+z} = \frac{x^{2}}{y+z}\). (b) The Cauchy step: for positive \(v_{i}\), \(\sum \frac{u_{i}^{2}}{v_{i}} \geq \frac{(\sum u_{i})^{2}}{\sum v_{i}}\); here \(u_{i} = x, y, z\) and \(v_{i} = y+z, z+x, x+y\), giving \(\sum \frac{x^{2}}{y+z} \geq \frac{(x+y+z)^{2}}{2(x+y+z)} = \frac{x+y+z}{2}\). The direct check is Lagrange's identity: \(\bigl(\sum \frac{u_{i}^{2}}{v_{i}}\bigr)\bigl(\sum v_{i}\bigr) - \bigl(\sum u_{i}\bigr)^{2} = \sum_{ibijective change of variables: it maps the whole case space one-to-one onto itself (positives with \(abc=1\) to positives with \(xyz=1\)), so every case is proved simultaneously and there is nothing to map to a representative. A wlog proves one case and bills the orbit by invariance; a change of variables re-expresses all cases at once. The confusion between them is benign when the map is a bijection — and fatal when it is not (§5's scaling trap is a change of variables that failed surjectivity).

Exercise 4 ◆ — Three broken wlogs, diagnosed cold. Each "proof" below declares a without-loss-of-generality; each declaration is unearned. Diagnose: which obligation failed — invariance, case map, or the gate of §5's third failure — and what the honest repair is. (i) "Claim: \((a-b)(b-c)(a-c) \geq 0\) whenever \(a, b, c\) are distinct reals with \(a + b + c = 6\). Wlog \(a \geq b \geq c\) (reordering), and then all three factors are nonnegative." (ii) "Claim: for the data \((a_1, b_1), \ldots, (a_n, b_n)\), \(\sum a_{i}b_{i}\) is maximized when both lists are sorted increasingly. Wlog both lists are sorted — relabel each list independently." (iii) "Claim: \(a^{2} + b^{2} \geq 2ab\) for all reals. Wlog divide by \(b\) and set \(t = a/b\); then \(t^{2} + 1 \geq 2t\), i.e. \((t-1)^{2} \geq 0\). Done."

Hint

No hint — the diagnosis is the exercise. For each: name the obligation skipped, exhibit the case that breaks the wlog, and state the repair in §3's three-part language.

Worked answer

(i) Invariance failure — §5's first trap. The expression is cyclic-invariant but alternating under swaps: \(f(2,1,3) = -f(1,2,3)\). The representative ordering \(a \geq b \geq c\) indeed gives all three factors nonnegative — but reaching it from an unordered case requires swaps, which flip the sign: the "proof" establishes \(f \geq 0\) on one orbit while silently billing the other. And the orbit check exposes something worse: the claim itself is false. Take \((a,b,c) = (2,3,1)\): \(a + b + c = 6\), the entries are distinct, and \(f = (2-3)(3-1)(2-1) = -2 < 0\). The wlog hid a live counterexample by proving only the ordered orbit. The repair: the honest statement is that \(f\)'s sign is the sign of the permutation carrying \((a,b,c)\) to sorted order — a true and pretty fact, provable by the orbit analysis of §5 — not the blanket nonnegativity claimed. (ii) Case-map failure — §5's second trap. Relabeling each list independently applies two unrelated permutations; the pairing \((a_i, b_i)\) is data the "symmetry" destroys. The claim as stated is about the given pairing; sorting both lists proves the value for a different pairing. The repair: either prove the rearrangement inequality (the sorted pairing is extremal — P71's move), or restate the claim with the pairing quantified and prove it pairing-by-pairing. (iii) The gate of §5's third failure — definedness. Dividing by \(b\) is illegal at \(b = 0\), and the case \(b = 0\) is exactly the case where the claim reads \(a^{2} \geq 0\) — true, but outside the wlog's jurisdiction. The repair: handle \(b = 0\) in one line first, then divide — or avoid the division: \(a^{2} + b^{2} - 2ab = (a-b)^{2} \geq 0\) needs no cases at all, which is the deeper lesson: the best wlog is sometimes the one you talk yourself out of.

Exercise 5 ◐ — When the census beats the wlog. This page's Schur proof used the wlog because the representative factorization \((a-b)^{2}(a+b-c) + c(a-c)(b-c)\) is a genuine landing. Schur generalizes: for \(r \geq 0\), \(\sum a^{r}(a-b)(a-c) \geq 0\). (a) Run the r = 2 case by wlog: assume \(a \geq b \geq c\), pair the middle term as in §2, and show \(S_{2} = \sum a^{2}(a-b)(a-c) = (a-b)\bigl[a^{2}(a-c) - b^{2}(b-c)\bigr] + c^{2}(a-c)(b-c)\), with the bracket factoring as \((a-b)\bigl(a^{2} + ab + b^{2} - c(a+b)\bigr)\). Verify numerically at \((5,3,1)\): the census value is \(25 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 + 9 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) + 1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 200 - 36 + 8 = 172\), and the factored form gives \((2)^{2}(25 + 15 + 9 - 8) + 1 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 4 \cdot 41 + 8 = 172\). (b) The boundary question: the r = 2 factorization landed because \(a^{2} + ab + b^{2} - c(a+b) = a(a-c) + b(b-c) + ab \geq 0\) in the representative. Does the same pairing trick close every \(r\)? Test \(r = 3\) at \((5,3,1)\) and report what the bracket \(a^{3}(a-c) - b^{3}(b-c)\) factors into, and whether the pieces stay nonnegative. (c) Explore outward: find (or invent) a claim where the census of cases is cheaper and more honest than the wlog — where the invariance holds but the representative proof is harder than walking all cases. State the feature that decides which to reach for. (d) The boundary sentence: what does the wlog deliver that the census cannot, and what does the census deliver that the wlog cannot? Say which you would reach for on a fresh claim, and why.

Hint

For (b): \(a^{3}(a-c) - b^{3}(b-c) = (a^{4} - b^{4}) - c(a^{3} - b^{3})\), and both differences factor by \(a - b\). For (c): think of claims with two variables where the two cases are one line each.

Worked answer

(a) The pairing: \(S_{2} = a^{2}(a-b)(a-c) + b^{2}(b-c)(b-a) + c^{2}(c-a)(c-b) = (a-b)\bigl[a^{2}(a-c) - b^{2}(b-c)\bigr] + c^{2}(a-c)(b-c)\), and the bracket factors: \(a^{2}(a-c) - b^{2}(b-c) = (a^{3} - b^{3}) - c(a^{2} - b^{2}) = (a-b)\bigl[a^{2} + ab + b^{2} - c(a+b)\bigr]\). So \(S_{2} = (a-b)^{2}\bigl[a^{2} + ab + b^{2} - c(a+b)\bigr] + c^{2}(a-c)(b-c)\), and in the representative ordering the bracket is \(a(a-c) + b(b-c) + ab \geq 0\) — every piece nonnegative. Numerics at \((5,3,1)\): census \(25 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 + 9 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) + 1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 200 - 36 + 8 = 172\); factored \((2)^{2} \cdot 41 + 1 \cdot 4 \cdot 2 = 164 + 8 = 172\). Collected. (b) \(r = 3\) at \((5,3,1)\): census \(S_{3} = 125 \cdot 2 \cdot 4 + 27 \cdot 2 \cdot (-2) + 1 \cdot (-4)(-2) = 1000 - 108 + 8 = 900\). The bracket: \(a^{3}(a-c) - b^{3}(b-c) = (a^{4} - b^{4}) - c(a^{3} - b^{3}) = (a-b)\bigl[(a+b)(a^{2}+b^{2}) - c(a^{2}+ab+b^{2})\bigr]\), which at \((5,3,1)\) reads \(2 \cdot (8 \cdot 34 - 49) = 2 \cdot 223 = 446\), and \(S_{3} = 2 \cdot 446 + 8 = 900\) — census met. The inner piece stays nonnegative in the representative: \((a+b)(a^{2}+b^{2}) - c(a^{2}+ab+b^{2}) = a\bigl[a(a-c) + b(b-c)\bigr] + b\bigl[a^{2} + b(b-c)\bigr] \geq 0\) whenever \(a \geq b \geq c \geq 0\). And the general pattern: for every \(r \geq 0\), \(x^{r}(x-c)\) is increasing in \(x\) on \(x \geq c\) (for \(r \geq 1\) its derivative is positive there; for \(0 \leq r < 1\) monotonicity still holds on the positive cone), so the bracket never goes negative and the wlog closes Schur at every degree. (c) The standard example: the claim \(|a-b| + |b-c| + |c-a| = 2(\max - \min)\) of Exercise 1 — provable by wlog in one line, but its six-case census is six one-line computations with no shared machinery, and for a reader meeting absolute values for the first time the census teaches more (it shows why the invariance holds by exhibiting it). The deciding feature: when the representative proof's machinery is reusable (Schur: the factorization idea scales to every degree), the wlog is the investment; when each case is trivial and the invariance is the entire content, the census is the proof. (d) The wlog delivers scale — one argument covering an orbit that may be enormous (Exemplar B's 32768 to two) — and it delivers structure: the named invariance is itself a mathematical fact worth having. The census delivers honesty by construction — no obligation can be skipped because every case is visibly walked — and it delivers the invariance as an observation rather than a lemma. On a fresh claim: census the small cases first (P02), look at whether the rows are copies; if they are, name the invariance and take the wlog — the census was the receipt, and the wlog is the refund.
