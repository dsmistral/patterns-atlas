Tier 2 · Canon Foundations

P68 · Without loss of generality

Exploit the symmetry group of a problem to sort variables, normalize scales, or pin down coordinates — collapsing \(n!\) identical branches of casework into a single canonical chamber.

Prerequisites: P08 · Divide by symmetry, P05 · Put it in canonical form

On-ramp 1 — What "Without loss of generality" (WLOG) actually means. When a proof says "Without loss of generality, assume \(a \ge b \ge c\)", it is not ignoring the other five orderings of \((a, b, c)\); it is making a formal promise: any other valid input can be transformed into one satisfying \(a \ge b \ge c\) by a symmetry that leaves both the hypothesis and the conclusion unchanged. Once the claim is proved in the sorted region \(a \ge b \ge c\), applying the inverse relabeling immediately proves it everywhere else.

On-ramp 2 — Full permutation symmetry (\(S_n\)) vs. cyclic symmetry (\(C_n\)) vs. homogeneity. Three distinct symmetry families power \(95\%\) of WLOG moves:

  • Full permutation symmetry (\(S_n\)): Swapping any two variables leaves the problem unchanged (e.g., \(a + b + c\) or \(abc\)). Since any tuple can be sorted, you may assume \(a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \cdots \ge a_n\).
  • Cyclic symmetry (\(C_n\)): Rotating the variables in a circle \((a, b, c) \mapsto (b, c, a) \mapsto (c, a, b)\) preserves the problem, but swapping two adjacent variables \((a, b, c) \mapsto (b, a, c)\) changes it (e.g., \(a^2 b + b^2 c + c^2 a\)). Here you have only \(3\) rotations, not \(6\) permutations: you may assume \(a = \max(a, b, c)\), which leaves two cases (\(a \ge b \ge c\) and \(a \ge c \ge b\)).
  • Scaling homogeneity (\(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\)): If multiplying all variables by \(\lambda > 0\) scales both sides of an inequality by the same factor \(\lambda^d\), you may normalize \(a + b + c = 1\) (or \(abc = 1\)).

1 · Before we begin

In P08 · Divide by symmetry, we used the three-fold symmetry of an equilateral triangle to prove Viviani's theorem (\(h_a + h_b + h_c = H\)) by summing three symmetric areas at once. And in P05 · Put it in canonical form, we saw how choosing one standard representative per equivalence class lets us compare objects at a glance.

Now combine those two ideas with the casework we encountered in P67 · Prove the contrapositive. In Exemplar 1 of P67, negating "\(3 \mid a\) and \(3 \mid b\)" gave "\(3 \nmid a\) or \(3 \nmid b\)". Notice that the subcase "\(3 \nmid a\) and \(3 \mid b\)" is identical to "\(3 \mid a\) and \(3 \nmid b\)" if we simply swap the labels \(a \leftrightarrow b\)! Whenever a symmetry group \(G\) acts on a problem without altering its hypotheses or conclusion, Without loss of generality (P68) chooses a single canonical slice (a fundamental domain) that intersects every symmetry orbit, dividing your casework by \(|G|\).

2 · Meet the problem: Schur's inequality and the six-way sign maze

Let \(a, b, c \ge 0\) be any three nonnegative real numbers. Prove Schur's Inequality (for degree \(r = 1\)):

\[ S(a, b, c) \;:=\; a(a - b)(a - c) \,+\, b(b - a)(b - c) \,+\, c(c - a)(c - b) \;\ge\; 0. \]

Try evaluating the three summands on the numbers \(\{1, 3, 5\}\) across all \(3! = 6\) possible assignments of \((a, b, c)\):

Ordering of \((a, b, c)\) First term \(a(a-b)(a-c)\) Second term \(b(b-a)(b-c)\) Third term \(c(c-a)(c-b)\) Total sum \(S(a,b,c)\)
\((5, 3, 1)\) [\(a \ge b \ge c\)] \(5(2)(4) = +40\) \(3(-2)(2) = -12\) \(1(-4)(-2) = +8\) \(+36\)
\((5, 1, 3)\) [\(a \ge c \ge b\)] \(5(4)(2) = +40\) \(1(-4)(-2) = +8\) \(3(-2)(2) = -12\) \(+36\)
\((3, 5, 1)\) [\(b \ge a \ge c\)] \(3(-2)(2) = -12\) \(5(2)(4) = +40\) \(1(-2)(-4) = +8\) \(+36\)
\((3, 1, 5)\) [\(c \ge a \ge b\)] \(3(2)(-2) = -12\) \(1(-2)(-4) = +8\) \(5(2)(4) = +40\) \(+36\)
\((1, 5, 3)\) [\(b \ge c \ge a\)] \(1(-4)(-2) = +8\) \(5(4)(2) = +40\) \(3(2)(-2) = -12\) \(+36\)
\((1, 3, 5)\) [\(c \ge b \ge a\)] \(1(-2)(-4) = +8\) \(3(2)(-2) = -12\) \(5(4)(2) = +40\) \(+36\)

Look at the table: in every row, the middle number produces the negative term (\(-12\)), while the largest and smallest numbers produce positive terms (\(+40\) and \(+8\)). If we do not fix which variable is largest, middle, and smallest, we cannot know which of the three terms is negative!

Now observe that permuting the three labels \((a, b, c)\) simply permutes the three summands of \(S(a, b, c)\) without changing their sum or the condition \(a, b, c \ge 0\). Therefore:

\[ \textbf{Without loss of generality, assume } a \ge b \ge c \ge 0. \]

Once \(a \ge b \ge c \ge 0\) is locked in, every difference has a known sign: \(a - b \ge 0\), \(a - c \ge b - c \ge 0\). Factoring \(a - b\) out of the first two terms (where \(b - a = -(a - b)\)) gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} S(a, b, c) &\;=\; (a - b)\Bigl[a(a - c) \,-\, b(b - c)\Bigr] \,+\, c(a - c)(b - c) \\ &\;\ge\; (a - b)\Bigl[b(a - c) \,-\, b(b - c)\Bigr] \,+\, c(a - c)(b - c) \\ &\;=\; b(a - b)^2 \,+\, c(a - c)(b - c) \;\ge\; 0, \end{aligned} \]

since \(b, c, (a-b)^2, (a-c), (b-c)\) are all nonnegative! One line of algebra in the sorted chamber \(a \ge b \ge c\) settles all six orderings at once.

3 · Name the move

Box P68 · The Without Loss of Generality Move

Suppose a mathematical statement \(\forall x \in X,\; P(x)\) is invariant under the action of a symmetry group \(G\) on the state space \(X\) — meaning that for every \(g \in G\) and \(x \in X\),

\[ x \in X \iff g \cdot x \in X \qquad\text{and}\qquad P(x) \iff P(g \cdot x). \]

Choose a fundamental domain (canonical slice) \(F \subseteq X\) that intersects every \(G\)-orbit (so \(\bigcup_{g \in G} g \cdot F = X\)). Declare "Without loss of generality, let \(x \in F\)" — such as sorting \(a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \cdots \ge a_n\) under \(S_n\), normalizing \(a + b + c = 1\) under \(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\) scaling, or placing a geometric figure at the origin under Euclidean isometries — and prove \(P(x)\) inside \(F\).

Box P68.A · The Four Verification Obligations of a Valid WLOG

  1. Name the Symmetry Group \(G\) Explicitly: State what transformation maps a general input into your assumed form — full permutation \(S_n\), cyclic shift \(C_n\), sign/color swap \(\mathbb{Z}_2\), positive scaling \(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\), or Euclidean rigid motion \(E(n)\).
  2. Check Simultaneous Invariance of Hypotheses & Goal: Verify that applying \(g \in G\) preserves both the allowed domain and the truth value of the conclusion. Never assume full \(S_n\) ordering \(a \ge b \ge c\) on a merely cyclic expression like \(a^2 b + b^2 c + c^2 a\).
  3. Verify Orbit Coverage (\(G \cdot F = X\)): Confirm that every valid input can be brought into \(F\) by some \(g \in G\). For example, scaling \(a + b + c = 1\) requires \(a + b + c > 0\) (you must check \(a = b = c = 0\) separately if \(0\) is allowed!).
  4. Never Spend the Same Symmetry Twice (Budget Audit): Once you use \(S_n\) to sort \(a_1 \le a_2 \le \cdots \le a_n\) in a problem with paired variables \((a_i, b_i)\), the indices \(1, \dots, n\) are locked; you cannot also assume \(b_1 \le b_2 \le \cdots \le b_n\)!

Box P68.B · Three-Key Recognition Diagnosis

  • Claim type: An universal inequality, divisibility theorem, combinatorial existence proof, or geometric identity whose statement treats several variables, colors, or directions interchangeably.
  • Problem shape: Absolute values \(|a - b|\), \(\max(a, b, c)\), products of differences \((a - b)(a - c)\), homogeneous fractions \(\frac{a}{b+c}\), or unpinned geometric figures floating in \(\mathbb{R}^d\).
  • Tools at hand: Sorting permutations \(S_n\), cyclic shifts \(C_n\), scaling substitutions \(x_i \mapsto \lambda x_i\), and coordinate frame alignment.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 (Combinatorics & Graph Theory) · Three stacked WLOGs in the Party Problem (\(R(3,3) = 6\))

Problem. At a gathering of \(6\) people, every pair of people is either mutual acquaintances (connected by a Red edge) or mutual strangers (connected by a Blue edge). Prove that there is either a triangle of \(3\) mutual acquaintances (all Red) or a triangle of \(3\) mutual strangers (all Blue) — without inspecting all \(2^{\binom{6}{2}} = 2^{15} = 32{,}768\) possible edge colorings of \(K_6\).

Solution.

We apply three independent WLOG reductions in sequence, each spending a different symmetry of the problem:

  1. WLOG 1 — Vertex-transitivity of \(K_6\) (spending \(S_6 / S_5\)): Because all \(6\) vertices play identical roles, fix attention on vertex \(v_1\). Vertex \(v_1\) has \(5\) incident edges connecting it to \(\{v_2, v_3, v_4, v_5, v_6\}\), each colored Red or Blue. By the Pigeonhole Principle (P10), at least \(\lceil 5/2 \rceil = 3\) of these \(5\) edges share the same color.
  2. WLOG 2 — Color-swap involution \(\text{Red} \leftrightarrow \text{Blue}\) (spending \(\mathbb{Z}_2\)): Because the target statement ("there exists an all-Red triangle or an all-Blue triangle") is invariant under swapping Red and Blue everywhere, without loss of generality assume that \(v_1\) has at least \(3\) Red edges.
  3. WLOG 3 — Relabeling the \(5\) neighbors of \(v_1\) (spending \(S_5\)): Because the labels \(v_2, v_3, v_4, v_5, v_6\) are interchangeable, without loss of generality relabel them so that the edges \(v_1 v_2,\; v_1 v_3,\; v_1 v_4\) are all Red.

Now our entire \(32{,}768\)-case search has collapsed to the single triangle of three vertices \(\{v_2, v_3, v_4\}\):

  • If any of the three edges \(v_2 v_3\), \(v_3 v_4\), or \(v_2 v_4\) is Red (say \(v_2 v_3\) is Red), then \(\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}\) is an all-Red triangle!
  • If none of the three edges \(v_2 v_3, v_3 v_4, v_2 v_4\) is Red, then all three are Blue, so \(\{v_2, v_3, v_4\}\) itself is an all-Blue triangle!

Exemplar 2 (Inequalities & Homogeneity) · Scaling \(a + b + c = 1\) in Nesbitt's inequality

Problem. For all positive real numbers \(a, b, c > 0\), prove Nesbitt's Inequality:

\[ \frac{a}{b + c} + \frac{b}{c + a} + \frac{c}{a + b} \;\ge\; \frac{3}{2}. \]

Solution.

Step 1 (Verify degree-\(0\) scaling homogeneity). For any \(\lambda > 0\), replacing \((a, b, c)\) by \((\lambda a, \lambda b, \lambda c)\) multiplies both the numerator and denominator of each fraction by \(\lambda\):

\[ \frac{\lambda a}{\lambda b + \lambda c} \;=\; \frac{a}{b + c}. \]

Since \(a, b, c > 0\), their sum \(S = a + b + c\) is strictly positive. Choosing the scaling factor \(\lambda = \frac{1}{a+b+c} > 0\), we may declare:

\[ \textbf{Without loss of generality, assume } a + b + c = 1 \quad (a, b, c \in (0, 1)). \]

Step 2 (Decouple the denominators). Under the normalization \(a + b + c = 1\), each two-variable denominator \(b + c = 1 - a\) depends on a single variable! Thus the left-hand side becomes

\[ \frac{a}{1 - a} + \frac{b}{1 - b} + \frac{c}{1 - c} \;=\; \sum_{\text{cyc}} \left(\frac{1}{1 - a} - 1\right). \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz (or Jensen's / tangent-line inequality on \(g(x) = \frac{x}{1-x}\) at \(x = \frac{1}{3}\), where \(g(\frac{1}{3}) = \frac{1}{2}\) and \(g'(\frac{1}{3}) = \frac{9}{4}\)), for every \(x \in (0, 1)\) we have

\[ \frac{x}{1 - x} - \left[\frac{1}{2} + \frac{9}{4}\left(x - \frac{1}{3}\right)\right] \;=\; \frac{(3x - 1)^2}{4(1 - x)} \;\ge\; 0. \]

Summing the inequality \(\frac{x}{1-x} \ge \frac{1}{2} + \frac{9}{4}(x - \frac{1}{3})\) over \(x \in \{a, b, c\}\) and using \(a + b + c = 1\) gives

\[ \frac{a}{1 - a} + \frac{b}{1 - b} + \frac{c}{1 - c} \;\ge\; \frac{3}{2} + \frac{9}{4}\bigl(a + b + c - 1\bigr) \;=\; \frac{3}{2}. \]

Exemplar 3 (Coordinate Geometry & Euclidean Isometries) · Pinning the midpoint to the origin in Apollonius's theorem

Problem. In any triangle \(\triangle ABC\) in the Euclidean plane, let \(M\) be the midpoint of side \(BC\). Prove Apollonius's Median Identity:

\[ AB^2 + AC^2 \;=\; 2\,AM^2 + \frac{1}{2}\,BC^2. \]

Solution.

Step 1 (Spend Euclidean rigid motion symmetry \(E(2)\)). A general triangle in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) has \(6\) coordinates \((x_A, y_A, x_B, y_B, x_C, y_C)\). However, lengths and midpoints are invariant under every Euclidean translation and rotation in \(E(2)\). Therefore, without loss of generality, translate the plane so the midpoint \(M\) sits at the origin \((0, 0)\), and rotate the plane so side \(BC\) lies along the \(x\)-axis!

Step 2 (Read off the 1-line coordinate calculation). Since \(M = (0, 0)\) is the midpoint of \(BC\) on the \(x\)-axis, the vertices \(B\) and \(C\) have coordinates \(B = (-d, 0)\) and \(C = (d, 0)\) where \(d = \frac{1}{2} BC\), and vertex \(A\) has coordinates \(A = (x, y)\) with \(AM^2 = x^2 + y^2\). Expanding \(AB^2 + AC^2\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} AB^2 + AC^2 &\;=\; \bigl[(x + d)^2 + y^2\bigr] \,+\, \bigl[(x - d)^2 + y^2\bigr] \\ &\;=\; 2(x^2 + y^2) + 2d^2 \;=\; 2\,AM^2 + \frac{1}{2}\,BC^2, \end{aligned} \]

where the cross terms \(+2xd\) and \(-2xd\) cancel automatically because \(M\) was pinned to \((0, 0)\).

5 · When the trick breaks

Exhibit A — The Cyclic vs. Full Symmetry Trap: assuming \(a \ge b \ge c\) on an expression that is only cyclically symmetric. Suppose you want to study the polynomial

\[ f(a, b, c) \;=\; \bigl(a^2 b + b^2 c + c^2 a\bigr) \,-\, \bigl(a b^2 + b c^2 + c a^2\bigr) \;=\; (a - b)(b - c)(a - c). \]

Notice that \(f(a, b, c)\) is invariant under cyclic rotations \((a, b, c) \mapsto (b, c, a) \mapsto (c, a, b)\). If you carelessly write "By symmetry, WLOG assume \(a \ge b \ge c\)", then \((a-b)(b-c)(a-c) \ge 0\) and you falsely "prove" that \(f(a, b, c) \ge 0\) for all \(a, b, c\)! In reality, on the reversed triple \((a, b, c) = (1, 2, 3)\), \(f(1, 2, 3) = (-1)(-1)(-2) = -2 < 0\).

Mitigation: For a cyclically symmetric expression in \(3\) variables, you may only assume \(a = \max(a, b, c)\) (or \(a = \min(a, b, c)\)), and you must check both subcases: Case 1 (\(a \ge b \ge c\)) and Case 2 (\(a \ge c \ge b\)).

Price: Two cases instead of one whenever reflections swap the sign or value of the expression.

Default: Always test whether swapping just two variables \(a \leftrightarrow b\) preserves the expression before writing \(a \ge b \ge c\) (Obligation 2 of Box P68.A).

Exhibit B — Double-spending the same symmetry budget on coupled variables. Suppose you want to study the dot product \(a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3\). Relabeling the index \(i \in \{1, 2, 3\}\) permutes the pairs \((a_i, b_i)\) together. Once you spend that \(S_3\) symmetry to sort \(a_1 \le a_2 \le a_3\), the sequence \((b_1, b_2, b_3)\) is locked in whatever order those partners happened to have! Assuming both \(a_1 \le a_2 \le a_3\) and \(b_1 \le b_2 \le b_3\) is illegal (in fact, the Rearrangement Inequality states that \(\sum a_i b_i\) is maximized only when \(b_i\) happens to be sorted in the same order as \(a_i\)).

Mitigation: Count the symmetry group \(G\) of the coupled system: if \(G = S_n\) acts diagonally on \((a_i, b_i)\) (not as \(S_n \times S_n\) independently), you can sort only one array.

Price: Using the Rearrangement Inequality or Abel summation (P69) to handle the second array.

Default: Track which variables are coupled by shared indices before applying a second WLOG (Obligation 4 of Box P68.A).

Exhibit C — Dividing by zero or flipping inequality signs during a scaling WLOG. If a problem allows real numbers \(a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}\) with \(a + b + c\) possibly zero or negative, normalizing \(a + b + c = 1\) fails in two ways: when \(a + b + c = 0\) no rescaling can make the sum \(1\), and when \(a + b + c < 0\) multiplying an odd-degree inequality by \(\lambda = \frac{1}{a+b+c} < 0\) reverses the inequality sign!

Mitigation: Restrict scaling WLOGs to positive quantities (\(\lambda > 0\)), or split into \(\sum a_i > 0\) (\(\implies +1\)), \(\sum a_i = 0\), and \(\sum a_i < 0\) (\(\implies -1\)).

Price: Checking the boundary hyperplanes \(\sum a_i = 0\) separately (P03).

Default: Verify \(\lambda > 0\) before every homogeneity normalization (Obligation 3 of Box P68.A).

Honest scope of this chapter. Every valid WLOG step is a quotient map \(X \to X / G\) by a group action. When the group \(G\) is finite (like \(S_n\) or \(C_n\)), a WLOG assumption simply picks one chamber out of \(|G|\) chambers. When \(G\) is a continuous Lie group (like \(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\) scaling, \(E(n)\) rigid motions, or \(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})\) Möbius transformations), WLOG picks a lower-dimensional cross-section (a slice or gauge fixing), reducing the number of continuous variables in the problem.

7 · Lineage interlude

From Euclid's superposition to Klein's Erlangen program and modern gauge fixing (c. 300 BCE – 1954).

  • Euclid (c. 300 BCE): In Book I, Proposition 4 of the Elements (SAS triangle congruence), Euclid moved one triangle by a rigid motion so its vertex and side coincided with the second triangle — the earliest implicit Euclidean-isometry WLOG argument in geometry.
  • Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1770) & Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1815): Studying how polynomials in \(n\) variables behave when their roots are permuted by \(S_n\), Lagrange and Cauchy quantified the exact ratio between full symmetric polynomials (1 orbit) and partially symmetric polynomials (such as alternating polynomials with 2 orbits under \(A_n \subset S_n\)).
  • Felix Klein (1872) & Hermann Weyl (1925): In his Erlangen Program, Klein defined a geometry as the study of properties invariant under a transformation group \(G\). To compute in any geometry, one uses \(G\) to place the figure into a canonical reference frame. Half a century later, Weyl introduced Weyl chambers — the exact geometric generalization of the sorted cone \(a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \cdots \ge a_n\) for any reflection group.
  • Chen Ning Yang & Robert Mills (1954) & Ludvig Faddeev–Victor Popov (1967): In electrodynamics and Yang–Mills gauge theory, physical fields have an infinite-dimensional symmetry group at every point of spacetime. Choosing a canonical condition like the Lorenz gauge \(\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0\) or Coulomb gauge \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{A} = 0\) ("gauge fixing") is literally a continuous WLOG choice picking one representative per gauge orbit.

8 · Exercises

Exercise P68.1 (●○○ — Dissolving absolute values in one line). Prove that for any three real numbers \(a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}\),

\[ |a - b| + |b - c| + |c - a| \;=\; 2\bigl(\max(a, b, c) \,-\, \min(a, b, c)\bigr). \]
  1. Explain why both sides of the identity are invariant under all \(3! = 6\) permutations of \((a, b, c)\).
  2. Apply a WLOG ordering to remove all three absolute value bars simultaneously and verify the identity.
Hint

Since \(|x - y| = |y - x|\), permuting \(a, b, c\) merely permutes the three terms on the left and leaves \(\max(a,b,c)\) and \(\min(a,b,c)\) unchanged. Assume WLOG \(a \ge b \ge c\).

Worked answer

1. Permutation invariance. Because \(|u - v| = |v - u|\), any permutation of \((a, b, c)\) simply permutes the three summands \(\{|a-b|, |b-c|, |c-a|\}\) without changing their sum, and \(\max(a,b,c)\) and \(\min(a,b,c)\) depend only on the unordered set \(\{a, b, c\}\).

2. One-line proof in the canonical chamber. Without loss of generality, relabel the three numbers so that \(a \ge b \ge c\). Then \(\max(a,b,c) = a\), \(\min(a,b,c) = c\), and \(a - b \ge 0,\; b - c \ge 0,\; a - c \ge 0\), so

\[ |a - b| + |b - c| + |c - a| \;=\; (a - b) + (b - c) + (a - c) \;=\; 2a - 2c \;=\; 2\bigl(\max(a,b,c) - \min(a,b,c)\bigr). \]

Exercise P68.2 (●●○ — Respecting cyclic symmetry \(C_3\) vs. full symmetry \(S_3\)). Let \(a, b, c > 0\) be positive real numbers. Consider the inequality

\[ \frac{a^2}{b} + \frac{b^2}{c} + \frac{c^2}{a} \;\ge\; a + b + c. \]
  1. Check whether the left-hand side is invariant under swapping \(a \leftrightarrow b\) (try \(a = 1, b = 2, c = 4\)). What is the true symmetry group of this inequality, and what WLOG assumption is valid?
  2. Prove the inequality cleanly in both cyclic orbits — either by using \(a = \max(a, b, c)\) across the two cases \(a \ge b \ge c\) and \(a \ge c \ge b\), or by completing the square on each fraction \(\frac{x^2}{y} - 2x + y = \frac{(x - y)^2}{y} \ge 0\).
Hint

At \((1, 2, 4)\), \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{4} + \frac{16}{1} = 17.5\), whereas at \((2, 1, 4)\), \(\frac{4}{1} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{16}{2} = 12.25 \neq 17.5\)! So the symmetry group is \(C_3\), not \(S_3\).

Worked answer

1. Symmetry audit. At \((a, b, c) = (1, 2, 4)\), the left-hand side is \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{4}{4} + \frac{16}{1} = 17.5\); swapping \(a \leftrightarrow b\) to \((2, 1, 4)\) gives \(\frac{4}{1} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{16}{2} = 12.25 \neq 17.5\). Thus the expression is invariant only under the cyclic group \(C_3\) (\((a,b,c) \mapsto (b,c,a)\)), so we may assume WLOG \(a = \max(a, b, c)\), which leaves two cases: \(a \ge b \ge c\) and \(a \ge c \ge b\).

2. Proof across both orbits. Subtracting \(a + b + c\) and writing \(a + b + c = (2a - b) + (2b - c) + (2c - a)\) gives the exact cyclic sum-of-squares identity (P04):

\[ \left(\frac{a^2}{b} + \frac{b^2}{c} + \frac{c^2}{a}\right) - (a + b + c) \;=\; \frac{(a - b)^2}{b} + \frac{(b - c)^2}{c} + \frac{(c - a)^2}{a} \;\ge\; 0, \]

which holds in both cyclic orbits! Alternatively, using WLOG \(a = \max(a, b, c)\), one can verify directly that the difference between the two cyclic orbits is \(\frac{(a-b)(b-c)(a-c)}{abc}\): when \(a \ge c \ge b\), this difference is \(\le 0\), and even in that smaller orbit we have \(\frac{a^2}{b} + \frac{b^2}{c} + \frac{c^2}{a} - (a + b + c) = \frac{(a-c)^2}{c} + \frac{(a-b)(c-b)(a+c)}{bc} \ge 0\).

Exercise P68.3 (●●● — Scaling WLOG \(abc = 1\) in IMO 1995 Problem 2). Prove that for any positive real numbers \(a, b, c > 0\),

\[ \frac{(abc)^{4/3}}{a^3(b + c)} + \frac{(abc)^{4/3}}{b^3(c + a)} + \frac{(abc)^{4/3}}{c^3(a + b)} \;\ge\; \frac{3}{2}. \]
  1. Verify that the left-hand side is homogeneous of degree \(0\) under \((a, b, c) \mapsto (\lambda a, \lambda b, \lambda c)\) for \(\lambda > 0\), and apply a scaling WLOG to set \(abc = 1\).
  2. Substitute \(a = 1/x,\; b = 1/y,\; c = 1/z\) (so \(xyz = 1\)) to simplify the left-hand side into \(\frac{x^2}{y+z} + \frac{y^2}{z+x} + \frac{z^2}{x+y}\), and finish the proof using Cauchy–Schwarz and AM–GM.
Hint

With \(abc = 1\) and \(a = 1/x, b = 1/y, c = 1/z\), notice that \(\frac{1}{a^3(b+c)} = \frac{x^3}{\frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z}} = \frac{x^3 y z}{y + z} = \frac{x^2 (xyz)}{y + z} = \frac{x^2}{y + z}\)!

Worked answer

1. Homogeneity check and \(abc = 1\) normalization. Under \((a, b, c) \mapsto (\lambda a, \lambda b, \lambda c)\), the numerator \((abc)^{4/3}\) scales by \((\lambda^3)^{4/3} = \lambda^4\) and the denominator \(a^3(b+c)\) scales by \(\lambda^3 \cdot \lambda = \lambda^4\). Since the inequality is degree-\(0\) homogeneous, choosing \(\lambda = (abc)^{-1/3} > 0\) allows us to assume without loss of generality that \(abc = 1\), reducing the target to

\[ \frac{1}{a^3(b + c)} + \frac{1}{b^3(c + a)} + \frac{1}{c^3(a + b)} \;\ge\; \frac{3}{2}. \]

2. Reciprocal substitution and Cauchy–Schwarz. Set \(x = 1/a,\; y = 1/b,\; z = 1/c\); then \(x, y, z > 0\) and \(xyz = 1\). Each summand transforms cleanly:

\[ \frac{1}{a^3(b + c)} \;=\; \frac{x^3}{\frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z}} \;=\; \frac{x^3 y z}{y + z} \;=\; \frac{x^2 (xyz)}{y + z} \;=\; \frac{x^2}{y + z}. \]

By Cauchy–Schwarz in Engel form (Titu's lemma) followed by AM–GM with \(xyz = 1\):

\[ \frac{x^2}{y + z} + \frac{y^2}{z + x} + \frac{z^2}{x + y} \;\ge\; \frac{(x + y + z)^2}{2(x + y + z)} \;=\; \frac{x + y + z}{2} \;\ge\; \frac{3\,(xyz)^{1/3}}{2} \;=\; \frac{3}{2}. \]

Exercise P68.4 (◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis). Find all ordered triples of positive integers \((a, b, c) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^3\) such that

\[ \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} \;=\; 1. \]
Worked answer

1. Pattern diagnosis (P68 · Without loss of generality + P15 · Squeeze it). An equation in \(3\) unbounded positive integers appears to have infinitely many candidates, because if \(a\) is small, \(b\) and \(c\) could be huge. However, both the domain \((a, b, c) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1}^3\) and the equation \(\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} = 1\) are invariant under all \(3! = 6\) permutations in \(S_3\). Therefore, without loss of generality, sort the denominators so that \(1 \le a \le b \le c\), and reconstruct all permutations at the end.

2. Bounding the smallest denominator \(a\). Since \(a \le b \le c\), we have \(\frac{1}{a} \ge \frac{1}{b} \ge \frac{1}{c}\), so

\[ 1 \;=\; \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} \;\le\; \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{a} \;=\; \frac{3}{a} \quad\implies\quad a \le 3. \]

Since \(\frac{1}{a} < 1\), we also have \(a \ge 2\). Thus \(a \in \{2, 3\}\) has only two possibilities!

  • Case 1 (\(a = 2\)): Then \(\frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\). Since \(b \le c\), \(\frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} \le \frac{2}{b} \implies b \le 4\), and \(\frac{1}{b} < \frac{1}{2} \implies b \ge 3\).
    • \(b = 3 \implies \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6} \implies (a,b,c) = (2, 3, 6)\).
    • \(b = 4 \implies \frac{1}{c} = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{4} \implies (a,b,c) = (2, 4, 4)\).
  • Case 2 (\(a = 3\)): Then \(\frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{c} = \frac{2}{3} \le \frac{2}{b} \implies b \le 3\). Since \(b \ge a = 3\), this forces \(b = 3\) and \(c = 3\), giving \((a,b,c) = (3, 3, 3)\).

3. Unfolding the \(S_3\) orbits to recover all ordered solutions.

  • The sorted triple \((2, 3, 6)\) has \(3\) distinct entries, generating \(3! = 6\) ordered triples: \((2,3,6), (2,6,3), (3,2,6), (3,6,2), (6,2,3), (6,3,2)\).
  • The sorted triple \((2, 4, 4)\) has \(2\) equal entries, generating \(\frac{3!}{2!} = 3\) ordered triples: \((2,4,4), (4,2,4), (4,4,2)\).
  • The sorted triple \((3, 3, 3)\) has all \(3\) entries equal, generating \(1\) ordered triple: \((3,3,3)\).

In total, there are \(6 + 3 + 1 = 10\) ordered solutions.

Exercise P68.5 (◐ — Exploration: Three-point WLOG via Möbius transformations and the cross-ratio). On the Riemann sphere \(\widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}\), the group \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\) of Möbius transformations

\[ T(z) \;=\; \frac{\alpha z + \beta}{\gamma z + \delta} \qquad (\alpha\delta - \beta\gamma = 1) \]

preserves circles, lines, and angles (P28). Notice that \((\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta)\) has \(4\) complex parameters minus \(1\) overall scaling, leaving \(3\) complex degrees of freedom.

  1. Given any three distinct points \(z_1, z_2, z_3 \in \mathbb{C}\), verify that the explicit Möbius transformation \[ T(z) \;=\; \frac{(z - z_1)(z_2 - z_3)}{(z - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)} \] maps \(z_1 \mapsto 0\), \(z_2 \mapsto 1\), and \(z_3 \mapsto \infty\). Why does this allow any theorem in Möbius geometry involving \(4\) points \((z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4)\) to assume without loss of generality that \((z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 1, \infty)\)?
  2. When \((z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 1, \infty)\), the fourth point \(z_4\) lands at \(w = T(z_4)\) (the cross-ratio \([z_4, z_1; z_2, z_3]\)). Since the real axis \(\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}\) is the unique circle/line through \(0, 1, \infty\), deduce a 1-line criterion for when four points \(z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4 \in \mathbb{C}\) lie on a common circle or line!
Hint

Plug \(z = z_1\), \(z = z_2\), and \(z = z_3\) directly into \(T(z)\). For Part 2, because \(T\) maps circles/lines to circles/lines and sends \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) to \(0, 1, \infty \in \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}\), the circle through \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) maps to the real axis!

Worked answer

1. Three-point transitivity of \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\). Substituting \(z = z_1\) makes the numerator \((z_1 - z_1)(z_2 - z_3) = 0\), so \(T(z_1) = 0\). Substituting \(z = z_2\) makes the numerator and denominator equal to \((z_2 - z_1)(z_2 - z_3) \neq 0\), so \(T(z_2) = 1\). Substituting \(z = z_3\) makes the denominator \(0\) while the numerator is \((z_3 - z_1)(z_2 - z_3) \neq 0\), so \(T(z_3) = \infty\). Because \(\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{C})\) acts simply transitively on triples of distinct points, any Möbius-invariant question about \((z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4)\) can assume WLOG that \((z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 1, \infty)\) and \(z_4 = w\).

2. Concyclicity via the real cross-ratio. Under \(T\), the unique circle or line passing through \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) maps to the unique circle or line through \(0, 1, \infty\), which is the extended real axis \(\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}\). Therefore, \(z_4\) lies on the same circle or line as \(z_1, z_2, z_3\) if and only if \(T(z_4)\) lies on the real axis:

\[ z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4 \text{ are concyclic or collinear} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{(z_4 - z_1)(z_2 - z_3)}{(z_4 - z_3)(z_2 - z_1)} \;\in\; \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}. \]
