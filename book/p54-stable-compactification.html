The Patterns Atlas · P54 · the fourteenth move of tier 5

Compactify by stable degenerations

A family that runs out of room is not a failed family — it is a census that forgot to count its own escapes. The move: design the boundary on purpose, admit the degenerate limits under a stability clause, and the counting machine always has a place to stop.

New word: compactification. Four ideas stand at the door, and each is a careful extension of something this book already built. (1) Compact is the grown-up name for closed and bounded: P21 proved that in such a room every endless family must crowd somewhere — that crowd is the catch. (2) A degeneration is what a family becomes in the limit when it stops being a member of its own kind: two marked points collide, a component shrinks to nothing, a trajectory breaks in the middle, a shape thins out to a segment. (3) Stability is the naming discipline: a degenerate object is admitted only if it is rigid enough to carry only finitely many names — no continuous family of self-symmetries left to blur the count. (4) A moduli room is a space whose points are the objects themselves: one point per configuration, two points close together when the configurations are near-twins. P05 taught the normalization habit that builds such rooms; this page teaches what to do when the room comes out with holes.

1 · Before we begin

P21 caught the limit point: in a closed and bounded room, every endless family crowds, and the crowd is the catch. P21's far door named this page precisely: "Exhibit A taught the repair — close the room, admit the boundary — as an emergency measure. P54 practises it deliberately: a family of objects that refuses to have a limit point is completed on purpose, the boundary points are added with ceremony, and what was a bug in a record becomes a compact room in which every endless family must crowd." This page is that deliberate practice.

P33 left the waiting-room line in its own words — "P52, P54, and P65 wait behind these, each a customer of the same cochain bookkeeping" — and this page is the second name on that list: the census below is read by the same cochain ledger that reads rise tables and strips. P53 sharpened the errand from the other side: "A family's walls are cleanest at its boundary — P54 adds the boundary; P65 counts strips where the walls are the analysis." And P53's last exploration honestly handed over the baton: which reals give the same flat torus "is a moduli question, the deeper bookkeeping this page only points at." This page is that deeper bookkeeping.

P65, published ahead of this page in tier 5, already spent the pattern once: its boundary door read "P54's move is to make the naming systematic — add the degenerate limits to the space itself, so that families which 'want' to escape are already standing in the census when they arrive. Distance: near — it is Exhibit A's discipline made into a general method. What it carries: the habit of building the boundary before counting what runs into it." This page builds that method from its first small case to the boundary P65 counted strips against.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P21's machine closes the trap only when the room is already closed and bounded. But the rooms where counting actually happens — rooms of configurations, of curves, of flow lines — are born with holes: exactly at the degenerations the objects are running toward. A counting machine that needs limits (and P65 needed them for \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\), and P64 will need them for every count it makes) cannot operate over a room with holes. The move of this page: do not repair the room by accident, as P21's Exhibit A did — design the boundary on purpose, admit the degenerate limits as first-class named points under a stability clause, and verify that the enlarged room is compact. Then every family crowds somewhere in it, and the census can begin.

2 · Meet the problem

Four labeled points on a line, considered up to stretch-and-slide. Stretch the line, slide it, turn it over — the four labels ride along. What survives? P05's move: normalize. There is exactly one stretch-and-slide carrying the first, second, and fourth labeled points to the positions \(0\), \(1\), and \(\infty\), namely

\[ M(z) = \frac{(z - z_1)(z_2 - z_4)}{(z_2 - z_1)(z - z_4)}, \]

and it parks the third point at a single number \(t = M(z_3)\). That number — the cross-ratio in canonical form — is the entire room: one point per configuration, so the room of all four-point configurations is the \(t\)-line. Almost. The normalization demands the four points distinct, and distinctness punctures the line three times:

  • \(z_3\) colliding with \(z_1\) forces \(t \to 0\);
  • \(z_3\) colliding with \(z_2\) forces \(t \to 1\);
  • \(z_3\) escaping toward \(z_4\)'s parking spot forces \(t \to \infty\).

The room of four-point configurations is the \(t\)-line with three holes: \(t \in \mathbb{R}\) minus \(\{0, 1\}\), with the third hole waiting at \(\infty\). Now the failure, in one sentence: take the family of configurations with \(z_3\) sliding toward \(z_1\) — the family is perfectly decent, each member a configuration of four distinct points, but its canonical coordinate marches \(t \to 0\), and the limit it wants to have is not in the room. The family escapes through a hole. P21's machine is useless here: the room is not compact, so no crowd is forced, so no catch is guaranteed. Counting anything over this room — averaging over it, integrating over it, taking limits in it — is unsafe while the exits are open. And the exits are exactly the degenerations: the collisions.

The second artifact is P21's own Exhibit A, grown one dimension. On the square with corners \((1,1)\), \((1,-1)\), \((-1,1)\), \((-1,-1)\), run the downhill flow of \(F(x,y) = (x - x^3/3) + (y - y^3/3)\) — the very function P65 used for its diamond census. The trajectories from the top corner \(P\) to the bottom corner \(B\) that pass near the saddle \(S_a = (1,-1)\) have a dwell that grows without bound: the nearer the approach, the longer the family lingers at the saddle, and in the limit the single trajectory breaks into the pair \(P \to S_a \to B\) — a concatenation, not a trajectory. The room of trajectories has a hole shaped like the broken pair. Same disease, different body: the family wants a limit, and the room refuses to hold it.

3 · Name the move

Compactify by stable degenerations. When the objects you count live in a room that is not compact, enlarge the room by adjoining the degenerate limits themselves — each admitted under a stability clause that keeps its naming finite — and work in the enlarged room, where every endless family must crowd.

In words: do not treat the escape as a failure of the family; treat it as a missing point of the room. Walk the boundary on purpose: list the ways the family can degenerate, check each degeneration for stability (does it carry a continuous family of self-symmetries that would make one object wear infinitely many names? then it is not yet honestly named — refine or exclude), add the stable ones as new points of the room, and verify the enlarged room is closed and bounded. What was a bug in a record — a family that "wants" to escape — becomes a point of the census.

Three-key diagnosis. Claim type: an existence-of-limits claim in the service of a count — you are not proving the limit for its own sake; you are making a later count possible. Problem shape: a room of configurations whose families run toward degenerate positions the room does not contain — collisions, collapses, breaks, escapes to infinity. Tools at hand: P21's closed-and-bounded (the enlarged room must be compact or the whole design fails), P05's canonical form (to name the room's points and spot the holes), and a stability filter — the one genuinely new tool, and the design work of the move: the clause that decides which degenerations are admitted.

4 · The move at work

Exemplar 1 · The line's two doors to infinity — designing the boundary is choosing the names

The simplest room with an escape: the line itself. The family \(x_n = n\) wants a limit. The move, in miniature: add the escape as a point. But there is more than one way to do it, and the choice is the design.

Door one: add a single point \(\infty\). Wrap the line onto the unit circle by stereographic projection, \(x \mapsto \left( \tfrac{2x}{1+x^2},\, \tfrac{x^2-1}{1+x^2} \right)\) — the wrap is onto, and the north pole is unoccupied. Declare it the point \(\infty\), and measure with the chordal distance it inherits from the circle:

\[ d(x, y) = \frac{2|x - y|}{\sqrt{(1+x^2)(1+y^2)}}, \qquad d(x, \infty) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}. \]

Then \(x_n = n\) converges: \(d(n, \infty) = 2/\sqrt{1+n^2}\), and indeed \(n \cdot d(n,\infty) \to 2\) — the convergence is honest and its rate is exactly \(1/n\) (at \(n = 10, 100, 1000\) the product reads \(1.9901\), \(1.99990\), \(1.9999990\)). The enlarged room is a closed bounded circle: compact, by design. But notice what the single door did to the naming: the family \(x_n = n\) and the family \(y_n = -n\) now have the same limit — the two escapes share one name, and indeed the chordal distance between the two families' members vanishes, \(d(n, -n) = \tfrac{4n}{1+n^2} \to 0\) (reading \(0.396\), \(0.040\), \(0.0040\) at \(n = 10, 100, 1000\)). The room cannot tell the two escapes apart.

Door two: add two points \(+\infty\) and \(-\infty\), and measure with the arctangent metric \(d(x,y) = |\arctan x - \arctan y|\), extended by \(\arctan(\pm\infty) = \pm\tfrac{\pi}{2}\). The enlarged room is a closed interval — compact again — but now \(x_n = n\) and \(y_n = -n\) have different limits, and their distance stays bounded away from zero: \(d(n, -n) = 2\arctan n \to \pi\) (reading \(2.942\), \(3.122\), \(3.1396\) at \(n = 10, 100, 1000\), against \(\pi = 3.1416\)).

Both rooms are compact; both satisfy P21; the difference is pure design — which degenerations get names, and how many. This is the move's first lesson, and it is the one P21's Exhibit A skipped in the emergency: the boundary is not found, it is designed, and the design space is real. The rest of this page is the design discipline at work.

Exemplar 2 · Four points, three holes, three names — the census that matches

Back to the room of \(\S 2\): the \(t\)-line with three holes at \(\{0, 1, \infty\}\). The move says: the holes are degenerations; the degenerations are collisions; so name the collisions and add them.

The naming must be honest, so run the full census. There are six ways a pair among \(\{z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4\}\) can collide, and the machine census of this page's lab pins each one's escape: colliding \(z_1\) with \(z_2\) drives \(t \to \infty\) — and so does colliding \(z_3\) with \(z_4\). Colliding \(z_1\) with \(z_3\) drives \(t \to 0\) — and so does colliding \(z_2\) with \(z_4\). Colliding \(z_1\) with \(z_4\) drives \(t \to 1\) — and so does colliding \(z_2\) with \(z_3\). The six arrivals pair off perfectly: two collisions per hole, and the pairing is the partition of the four labels into two pairs:

HoleArriving collisionsPartition name
\(t = \infty\)\(z_1 \to z_2\) and \(z_3 \to z_4\)\(\{12 \mid 34\}\)
\(t = 0\)\(z_1 \to z_3\) and \(z_2 \to z_4\)\(\{13 \mid 24\}\)
\(t = 1\)\(z_1 \to z_4\) and \(z_2 \to z_3\)\(\{14 \mid 23\}\)

And the count of unordered two-pair partitions of four labels is exactly three — \(12\mid34\), \(13\mid24\), \(14\mid23\) — matching the count of holes. Three holes, three names, a systematic dictionary: this is the "naming made systematic" that P65's boundary door asked for, in its first honest case.

Now the stability clause, which decides what the added points are. The degenerate configuration behind the hole \(t = \infty\) is not "four points with two merged": it is better read as two components — one carrying the colliding pair \(\{z_1, z_2\}\), one carrying the pair \(\{z_3, z_4\}\) — joined at a single node where the original line pinched. The clause demands each component carry at least three special points (marks or nodes): here each component carries two marks plus the node, three exactly — the configuration is stable, it has no room for a continuous family of self-symmetries, and it is admitted with a finite name. The clause is not decoration: with only two special points a component could slide its marks symmetrically forever, wearing infinitely many names — the naming would blur, the census would lie. Admitting exactly the stable degenerations, the room of four-point configurations closes to the entire \(t\)-line plus its three holes — the full circle: compact. The open room \(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0,1\}\) has become the compact room \(\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}\), with every hole holding a named, stable degeneration.

Exemplar 3 · The broken pair — the census P65 already spent

The flow-line artifact of \(\S 2\), made honest by machine. On the square with \(F(x,y) = (x - x^3/3) + (y - y^3/3)\): the critical points are \(P = (1,1)\) with \(F = \tfrac43\), the saddles \(S_a = (1,-1)\) and \(S_b = (-1,1)\) with \(F = 0\), and \(B = (-1,-1)\) with \(F = -\tfrac43\) — P65's diamond, and its corner drops are exactly \(F(P) - F(S_a) = \tfrac43\), \(F(S_a) - F(B) = \tfrac43\), total \(F(P) - F(B) = \tfrac83\). Two edges of the square are flow lines, and they are exact: the edge \(x = 1\) carries the trajectory \((1, -\tanh s)\) from \(P\) down to \(S_a\), and the edge \(y = -1\) carries \((-\tanh s, -1)\) from \(S_a\) to \(B\) — both verified as solutions to machine precision in this page's lab.

Now the family: trajectories from \(P\) to \(B\) that leave \(P\) a hair's breadth inside the \(S_a\) corner, started at \((1 - \varepsilon^2,\; 1 - \varepsilon)\). The lab integrates the flow for \(\varepsilon = 10^{-3}, 10^{-4}, 10^{-5}\) and the census reads:

  • Convergence to the broken pair. The greatest distance from the trajectory to the two edges — \(\min(|x - 1|, |y + 1|)\), the honest measure of "how close to the concatenation" — reads \(0.0606\), \(0.0195\), \(0.0063\): shrinking with \(\varepsilon\), so on every fixed window the family converges to the pair \(P \to S_a \to B\). The limit exists — as a broken trajectory.
  • The dwell grows logarithmically. Time spent within a fixed ball of the saddle: \(0.93\), \(2.09\), \(3.24\) — growth \(1.159\) per decade of \(\varepsilon\), against the theoretical \(\ln(10)/2 = 1.151\): the trajectory's escape from the saddle is governed by the linear rate \(2\) there, and the dwell diverges as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). The family does not pass the saddle; it lingers, longer and longer, and in the limit it rests there forever — which is precisely what "broken" means.
  • The books balance at every \(\varepsilon\). The total drop \(F(P) - F(B) = \tfrac83\) is carried by every family member, and in the limit it splits exactly as \(\tfrac43 + \tfrac43\) across the two legs. Nothing is lost in the breaking — the account P65's ledger needed.

The move's verdict: the room of \(P \to B\) trajectories — an open one-dimensional family, quotiented by time-shift — acquires its boundary by adjoining the broken pairs. How many? The index-1 critical points standing between \(P\) and \(B\): exactly two, \(S_a\) and \(S_b\). So the compactified room is an open interval plus its two endpoints — a closed interval: compact, with the boundary named. This is the one-dimensional census whose boundary pairing, in the full theory, is the mechanism behind \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\): the broken configurations are the boundary of the census, and the boundary of a boundary is empty. P65's strip census ran this machine at the research scale; this page has now built the machine itself.

Exemplar 4 · Five points: the census one dimension up — walls, corners, and the graph that matches

Four points made a line with three holes. Five points make a room of dimension \(n - 3 = 2\) — and for the first time the boundary is too big to name point by point; it must be censused and drawn. Park \((z_1, z_2, z_3)\) at \((0, 1, \infty)\) by P05's normalization: the room is the set of positions for the pair \((z_4, z_5)\), each free label required to stay clear of the parked three and of the other free one. Its natural home is the plane of pairs \((\mathbb{P}^1)^2\) with the collision loci removed — and the removed loci are the design material.

The seven visible walls. \(z_4\) can collide with each parked label: the loci \(z_4 = 0\), \(z_4 = 1\), \(z_4 = \infty\) — three walls, named by the partitions \(D_{14}\), \(D_{24}\), \(D_{34}\). The same for \(z_5\): three more walls, \(D_{15}\), \(D_{25}\), \(D_{35}\). And \(z_4\) can collide with \(z_5\): the diagonal \(z_4 = z_5\), the seventh wall, \(D_{45}\). Each wall is a copy of the four-point room of Exemplar 2 — \(D_{ij} \cong \overline{M}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1\), because freezing one collision of five labels leaves four labels to name — so the census nests: the five-point boundary is stitched from copies of the four-point room.

The three hidden divisors. Exercise 3 counted ten boundary divisors; seven walls are only seven, and the missing three are the move's second lesson. Walk toward the corner \((z_4, z_5) \to (0, 0)\): both free labels collide with \(z_1\) — a triple collision that no crossing of walls can name. Its stable name is the partition \(\{1,4,5\} \mid \{2,3\}\), the divisor \(D_{23}\): a whole exceptional divisor the corner acquires when the room is enlarged, because the bubble carrying \(\{1,4,5\}\) has room for internal bookkeeping of its own — how \(z_4\) and \(z_5\) made their approach — and that room is the new divisor. The same at \((1,1)\): the divisor \(D_{13}\). The same at the \((\infty, \infty)\) corner: \(D_{12}\). Seven walls plus three exceptional divisors: ten — the census that matches, with its arithmetic in the open: \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\), one divisor per pair of labels, wall or corner alike.

The corners, and the graph. A corner is where two divisors meet, and two divisors \(D_{ij}\), \(D_{kl}\) meet exactly when their label-pairs share no label: \(\{1,4\}\) can coexist with \(\{2,3\}\) on one degenerate configuration, but \(\{1,4\}\) with \(\{1,5\}\) cannot — the label \(1\) cannot sit on two bubbles at once. The count: choose the four labels a corner engages, \(\binom{5}{4} = 5\) ways, and split them into two pairs, 3 ways: fifteen corners. Ten divisors as vertices, fifteen corners as edges, adjacency by disjointness — the boundary census is a graph, and the graph is the Petersen graph (Fig P54.3). The machine audit of this page's lab regenerates every vertex of the figure from exact trigonometry and certifies all fifteen edges: disjoint, unique, an exact cover of the fifteen pairs. What was a count of holes in Exemplar 2 has become, one dimension up, a drawn object with structure — a census the rest of the book will keep meeting.

Exemplar 5 · Stable maps: the count the compactification buys

Curves that carry a map are where the move pays rent. A degree-\(d\) rational plane curve is the image of a map \(u: \mathbb{P}^1 \to \mathbb{P}^2\) of degree \(d\), counted up to reparametrizing the domain, and the oldest census in the business asks: how many pass through \(3d - 1\) general points? The number \(3d - 1\) is not a mystery but a dimension count — P53's audit, verbatim: deformations minus obstructions. The room of degree-\(d\) maps with \(n\) marked domain points has dimension \(3d + n - 1\); a marked point pinned to a fixed target point imposes two conditions; and at \(n = 3d - 1\) the books balance exactly: room dimension \(6d - 2\), conditions \(2(3d - 1) = 6d - 2\), a zero-dimensional, finite census. The base cases are the schoolroom facts: \(d = 1\), lines through \(2\) points — one line, \(N_1 = 1\); \(d = 2\), conics through \(5\) — one conic, \(N_2 = 1\).

But the room of maps has the same disease as every room in this book: families escape at the degenerations, and for maps the degenerations are splittings — the domain breaks at a node, the degree divides \(d = d_1 + d_2\), and the two components' images must agree at the node's image. The compactification is Kontsevich's: admit the split domains as nodal curves carrying their maps — stable maps — under a stability clause with two halves. A component the map collapses to a point needs Deligne–Mumford's clause verbatim: at least three special points. A component the map covers names itself: a reparametrization commuting with a nonconstant map is finite in number — the parabola \(z \mapsto (1 : z : z^2)\) admits none at all, its double-covered line \(z \mapsto (1 : z^2 : 0)\) exactly one — and finite naming is all the clause asks. The marks ride along and remember where they land; the node carries the ledger's matching condition; the enlarged room is closed, and the count can begin.

The count is a recursion, and the recursion is a boundary census — the move's pattern, worn openly. On the compactified room, look at the boundary where a degree-\(d\) map splits into degrees \(d_1 + d_2 = d\). The census equates two boundary divisors, and the equation is Exemplar 2's fact read as divisors: \(\overline{M}_{0,4} \cong \mathbb{P}^1\), and on \(\mathbb{P}^1\) any two points are linearly equivalent — divisors of equal degree cut the same class — so the three boundary points of the four-point room can be equated in the ledger. Pull that relation back along the forgetful map (drop the map, keep the marks, stabilize by the clause) and read it against the point constraints: the two sides of the equation count the two-component maps in two different ways, and equating them gives Kontsevich's recursion:

\[ N_d \;=\; \sum_{d_1 + d_2 = d} N_{d_1}\, N_{d_2} \left( d_1^2 d_2^2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-2} \;-\; d_1^3 d_2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-1} \right), \qquad N_1 = 1, \]

where the binomials are the census of how the remaining fixed points distribute across the two components, and the two weight terms are the two splittings' multiplicities — whose exact provenance is the derivation's business (Kontsevich 1995, this page's §7), not a sketch's. What this page verifies is the verdict, and the verdict is famous: the recursion returns \(N_1 = 1\), \(N_2 = 1\), \(N_3 = 12\) — twelve rational cubics through eight general points — then \(N_4 = 620\), Zeuthen's 1873 hand count reproduced in one machine pass, then \(N_5 = 87{,}304\). Every value is exact-integer machine-checked in this page's lab, term by term at \(d = 4\): \(-144 + 224 + 540 = 620\).

Two honest notes, one per clause. For the plane, these are honest counts — the compactified room is so well-behaved that its zero-dimensional census needs no repair; for general targets the same census runs on rooms whose books do not quite balance, and repairing them is P64's virtual audit, the near door of \(\S 6\) — the reason the stable-map room is its stage. And the compactification itself is Gromov's wall from \(\S 5\), in action: a family of maps can bubble — degree escaping onto ever smaller spheres — but the area budget bounds the bubbling, the stability clause names each bubble, and the boundary tree is finite. Compactify by stable degenerations, then count: the move, end to end.

5 · When the move fails

The move has three honest failure modes, and each is a real design hazard, not a footnote.

Names can refuse to separate. Build a room by adding two points \(0_+\) and \(0_-\) to the line at the origin, declaring sequences approaching from above to converge to \(0_+\) and from below to \(0_-\). The intent is honest — two names for two one-sided escapes — but the room fails the separation audit: every neighborhood of \(0_+\) contains points arbitrarily close to \(0_-\), and no design of the neighborhoods repairs it, because the line itself cannot tell the two names apart. A room whose points cannot be separated cannot carry honest counts — limits would be ambiguous, and the census would report crowds that cannot be pinned. The research literature's answer — assign the ambiguity a room of its own and count it — is a deeper pattern, and \(\S 6\)'s near door names it.

The stability clause can admit nothing. Take three labeled points on the line, up to stretch-and-slide. The canonical form parks \((z_1, z_2) \to (0, 1)\) and leaves no free coordinate: the room is a single point, \(M_{0,3}\), dimension \(n - 3 = 0\). It is already compact — but try to name its degenerations: a collision of two of the three marks gives a component carrying two marks and no node — two special points, one short of the stability clause. Every degeneration of the three-point configuration is unstable; the clause excludes them all; the compactification adds nothing. That is the honest verdict here (nothing needed adding), but the same clause, run on rooms where the objects themselves shrink, can come up empty-handed even when limits are wanted — the classical hazard of quotient rooms, where the stability audit can reject every candidate boundary point. The clause is not a formality; it is the load-bearing design decision, and it can refuse to carry any load.

Without a budget, degeneration is uncontrolled. Consider the family of bumps \(u_\sigma(s) = \operatorname{sech}(s/\sigma)\) as \(\sigma \to 0\): each member is a perfectly decent bell, but the family's Dirichlet energy \(\int (u_\sigma')^2\,ds\) — the quantity a flow's trajectories must budget, and the exact currency of P65's strip energy — reads \(\tfrac{2}{3\sigma}\): \(0.667\), \(6.667\), \(66.67\) at \(\sigma = 1, 0.1, 0.01\). The budget explodes, and with it the number of places the family could degenerate: nothing stops the bump from splitting into ever more, ever smaller copies, and no naming scheme can census an unlimited tree. Contrast the budgeted family \(v_\sigma(s) = \sigma^{-1/2}\operatorname{sech}(s/\sigma)\): its total \(\int v_\sigma^2\,ds = 2\) exactly, for every \(\sigma\) — the books are forced to balance — while its mass still concentrates: the fraction inside \(|s| \leq W\sigma\) is exactly \(\tanh W\), so \(76.2\%\) within one half-width, \(99.5\%\) within three, \(99.99\%\) within five, and the full \(99\%\) at \(W = 2.647\). Concentration with a budget has finitely many names: with total budget \(E\) and each visible bubble costing at least \(\varepsilon\), there are at most \(\lfloor E/\varepsilon \rfloor\) of them — with \(E = 2\pi\), at most \(12\) bubbles of size \(\tfrac12\), at most \(62\) of size \(0.1\). This is Gromov's wall in miniature: the area budget is what makes the degenerations nameable at all. Without it, the move fails not by mislabeling the boundary but by never finishing the naming.

Scope, honestly. This page built the design discipline: the census that matches holes to degenerations, the stability clause as the naming filter, the budget as the finiteness engine, and worked compactifications in geometry (four points, five points), in flows (the broken pair), and in enumeration (stable maps). It did not build the analytic walls the research machine stands on: the elliptic estimates behind Gromov's compactness, the energy identity's error terms, the transversularity that cuts the census cleanly, the gluing that runs the broken-pair picture in reverse. Those are the literature's work; the lineage section names them, and no sketch in \(\S 4\) should be read as more than the census the walls protect.

6 · What to try next

The doors this page opens. The nearest door: P64 · Virtual classes from obstruction theory. Once the room is compact, count with signs — and P64's whole trade is integrating over rooms that do not quite exist, certified by exactly the obstruction bookkeeping of P53. P53's \(H^2\) is P64's starting ledger; this page's boundary is P64's stage: every virtual count is a count over a compactified census whose degenerations were named first. Exemplar 5's stable-map rooms are that stage in miniature: for the plane the counts are honest; for a general target the same census needs P64's virtual weights to balance. The waiting-room line from P33 ends here and begins again.

The middle door: P65 · Floer homology from holomorphic strips, reread from this side of the boundary. Its \(\partial \circ \partial = 0\) is this page's Exemplar 3 at research scale — the broken strips were the named degenerations all along, and its bubble trees were the budgeted census of \(\S 5\). One page later, the machine reads as the move's largest customer.

The far door: P56 · Rescale for asymptotic geometry. Bubbles are rescalings: the concentrated bump of \(\S 5\) only shows its face after the zoom — and after the substitution \(w = s/\sigma\) the family is the same shape again, which is precisely why the naming could stay finite. Where this page adjoins the degenerate limits, P56 asks what the limits look like scale after scale, and the two reads combine: the boundary tree, level by level.

Default habit. When a count needs limits — an average over configurations, a boundary of a census, a limit of trajectories — name the escapes before counting: list the degenerations the family can run toward, audit each with the stability clause (would it wear infinitely many names? refine or exclude), check the budget (what quantity bounds the degeneration, and does it force finitely many names?), and only then close the room and let P21's machine run. The price, paid up front: the boundary can refuse to separate, the clause can come up empty, and the budget can be missing — each failure is a finding about the problem, not about the method.

7 · Lineage

The census that needed a boundary is older than the boundary itself. Bernhard Riemann, 1857, counting the shapes a surface of genus \(g\) can wear, found the number \(3g - 3\) — a count of a room of structures, one of the first times geometry counted configurations rather than objects, and a count whose families degenerate exactly at the surfaces that pinch, the boundary this page's pattern was born to name.

David Mumford, 1965, in Geometric Invariant Theory, faced the quotient version of the naming problem — when a room is divided by a symmetry group, which orbits deserve to be points? — and answered with the stability clause: admitted are the points whose self-symmetry group is finite, the exact "finitely many names" filter of \(\S 3\), here first written as a general principle: the boundary is not found, it is earned by an audit.

Pierre Deligne and David Mumford, 1969, compactified Riemann's room: adjoining the stable degenerations — surfaces allowed to pinch into nodes, each component carrying at least three special points — they proved the enlarged room compact and irreducible. The three-point clause of this page's Exemplar 2 is their clause, verbatim; the pair-partition dictionary of the four-point room is their boundary, in its smallest honest case.

Mikhail Gromov, 1985, carried the pattern into symplectic geometry: pseudo-holomorphic curves with bounded area form families whose only escape is bubbling — and the budget makes the bubble tree finite, the degenerations stable, and the compactified census closed. This is the wall P65 stood on; this page's \(\S 5\) is its miniature, budget and all.

Maxim Kontsevich, 1995, fused the two censuses: stable maps — curves plus maps to a target, compactified by their degenerations (the idea proposed around 1992, published in 1995) — gave enumeration the compact room it integrates over. The counts that followed (and the virtual classes certifying them) are the near door of \(\S 6\): P64's stage, built from this page's move.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Two distances that agree. On the one-point-compactified line of Exemplar 1: (a) compute \(d(1,2)\) exactly, by the chordal formula. (b) Compute \(d(3, \infty)\). (c) The two answers agree — explain, without computation, why some symmetry of the room had to make them agree.

Hint

(a), (b): substitute into \(d(x,y) = 2|x - y|/\sqrt{(1+x^2)(1+y^2)}\) and \(d(x,\infty) = 2/\sqrt{1+x^2}\). (c): the room is a circle; distances on a circle are chords.

Worked answer

(a) \(d(1,2) = \frac{2 \cdot 1}{\sqrt{(1+1)(1+4)}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.6325\). (b) \(d(3, \infty) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{1+9}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.6325\) — identical. (c) The enlarged room is the unit circle under stereographic projection, and the chordal distance is the circle's own chord length: \(d = 2\sin(\Delta\theta/2)\) where \(\Delta\theta\) is the central angle. The points \(1, 2\) sit at central angles \(2\arctan x - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) reading \(0^\circ\) and \(36.87^\circ\); the points \(3, \infty\) sit at \(53.13^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\). Both pairs subtend exactly \(36.87^\circ\), and on a circle any two chords of equal length are related by a rotation — a symmetry of the room carrying one pair to the other. The agreement was forced by the room's symmetry, exactly as \(\S 3\)'s diagnosis predicts: the boundary design (a circle) determined the distance book. Machine-verified in this page's lab.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The six values of one configuration. Take \((z_1, z_2, z_3, z_4) = (0, 1, 2, 4)\). (a) Compute the canonical coordinate \(t = M(z_3)\). (b) Re-order the same four points — there are \(24\) orderings — and show the canonical coordinates take exactly six distinct values; list them. (c) Show each value is hit exactly four times, and explain the count via the room's symmetries.

Hint

(a) Substitute into \(M\). (b) The symmetries of the four-point room are the stretch-slide maps fixing the set of holes \(\{0, 1, \infty\}\); there are six of them — write down what each does to \(t\).

Worked answer

(a) \(t = \frac{(2-0)(1-4)}{(1-0)(2-4)} = \frac{-6}{-2} = 3\). (b) The six orderings of the hole-set \(\{0,1,\infty\}\) give the six maps \(t \mapsto \{t,\; 1/t,\; 1 - t,\; 1/(1-t),\; (t-1)/t,\; t/(t-1)\}\); at \(t = 3\) these read \(3,\; \tfrac13,\; -2,\; -\tfrac12,\; \tfrac23,\; \tfrac32\) — six distinct values. (c) The machine census of this page's lab runs all \(24\) orderings: each of the six values is hit exactly \(4\) times, because the four configurations sharing a value differ only by which labeled point the normalization parked at \(\infty\) — four labels, four orderings per value. Six values, four hits each, twenty-four orderings: the books balance. Machine-verified.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The next census: five points. The room of five labeled points on the line up to stretch-and-slide has dimension \(n - 3 = 2\). Its compactification adjoins the stable degenerations. (a) List the degenerations where exactly one collision happens — the room's boundary divisors — by naming them as partitions of the five labels. (b) Count them and verify against the lab census. (c) Check each against the stability clause: which component could ever fail it, and why does that failure never occur here?

Hint

(a) One collision splits the five labels into two groups — but the node counts as a special point for each side. (b) Count unordered splits. (c) The smallest possible group carries two labels plus the node.

Worked answer

(a) A single collision degenerates the line into two components joined at a node, splitting the labels into a group of \(k\) and a group of \(5 - k\), each group inheriting the node as an extra special point. The list, unordered: \(12\mid345,\; 13\mid245,\; 14\mid235,\; 15\mid234,\; 23\mid145,\; 24\mid135,\; 25\mid134,\; 34\mid125,\; 35\mid124,\; 45\mid123\). (b) Ten — the count of unordered splits of five labels into groups of \(2\) and \(3\): \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\), and the machine census of this page's lab confirms exactly these ten — and Exemplar 4, below, draws this census as a graph. (c) The stability clause demands three special points per component: a group of \(k\) labels carries \(k\) marks plus the node, so the smallest possible component carries \(2 + 1 = 3\) — exactly stable, never short. The clause is what rules out the \(1 \mid 4\) splits: a lone label plus the node is only two special points, unstable, excluded — and indeed a one-label group cannot collide with anything without merging into a neighbor's collision, which the census already named. Machine-verified.

Exercise 4 ●●● — The two-scale cascade. Park \((z_1, z_2, z_3)\) at \((0, 1, \infty)\) and send \(z_4 = \varepsilon\), \(z_5 = \varepsilon^2\) — a perfectly decent family of five-point configurations for every \(\varepsilon > 0\). (a) Under the microscope \(w = z/\varepsilon\): compute the five limits and name the collisions the \(w\)-room sees. (b) Under the second microscope \(u = z/\varepsilon^2\): compute the five limits again — and notice the two microscopes disagree about who collides with whom. (c) Write the stable tree the family converges to (three components), audit each component against the stability clause, and name the corner divisor of \(\overline{M}_{0,5}\) the limit lies in; verify the corner is an edge of Fig P54.3's graph.

Hint

(a) \(1/\varepsilon \to \infty\). (b) \(\varepsilon/\varepsilon^2 = 1/\varepsilon\). (c) The tree must record every collision either microscope sees; a corner is a pair of divisors with disjoint label-pairs.

Worked answer

(a) \(w = (0,\; 1/\varepsilon,\; \infty,\; 1,\; \varepsilon) \to (0, \infty, \infty, 1, 0)\): the \(w\)-room sees \(z_5\) colliding with \(z_1\) (both at \(0\)) and \(z_2\) sliding toward \(z_3\)'s home (\(\infty\)); \(z_4\) rides along at \(1\), colliding with nothing. (b) \(u = (0,\; 1/\varepsilon^2,\; \infty,\; 1/\varepsilon,\; 1) \to (0, \infty, \infty, \infty, 1)\): the \(u\)-room sees \(z_4\) joining the \(\infty\)-crowd with \(z_2, z_3\), and \(z_5\) not colliding with anything — a unit from \(z_1\). The two microscopes disagree — at the \(\varepsilon\)-scale \(\{1,5\}\) collide, at the \(\varepsilon^2\)-scale they are far apart — and neither lies: each sees one scale, and the family's limit carries both readings at once. (c) The stable tree: three components in a chain \(\{2,3\} - \{4\} - \{1,5\}\). The \(\{2,3\}\) and \(\{1,5\}\) components record the two microscopes' collisions; the middle \(\{4\}\) component is the honest broker between the scales. Stability audit: \(\{2,3\}\) carries two marks plus a node, \(\{4\}\) one mark plus two nodes, \(\{1,5\}\) two marks plus a node — three special points each, all stable. The two splits \(\{2,3\} \mid \{1,4,5\}\) and \(\{1,5\} \mid \{2,3,4\}\) name the corner \(D_{23} \cap D_{15}\); the pairs \(\{2,3\}\) and \(\{1,5\}\) are disjoint, so the corner is an edge of Fig P54.3's Petersen graph — an edge of its outer pentagon. Machine-verified: both microscopes' limits, the component audit, and the corner census run in this page's lab.

Exercise 5 ◆ — The consultant's rectangles.

Worked answer

A family of axis-parallel rectangles of area \(1\), considered up to translation: the room is the width \(w \in (0, \infty)\) — one-dimensional, open at both ends, with the two escapes \(w \to 0\) and \(w \to \infty\). The move's audit: the degenerations are the infinitely thin, infinitely long limits — and these are genuinely new kinds of objects, not rectangles; the room \((0,\infty)\) compactifies to the closed interval \([0, \infty]\) with two added boundary points, each naming a degenerate object with a marked direction (horizontal at \(w \to 0\), vertical at \(w \to \infty\), if orientation is tracked — otherwise one point, by the same design choice as Exemplar 1's two doors). The stability clause passes: a degenerate segment carries no continuous family of self-symmetries (its aspect is frozen), so each escape has finitely many names. The census stands: two boundary points, both named. The honest price, and the finding: the boundary objects live outside the original category — the compactified room counts them anyway, and any count over the family (an average over shapes, say) must decide, as part of its design, whether the boundary carries weight. That decision is not the pattern's to make; it is the problem's — and naming it is exactly what the pattern is for.

Exercise 6 ◐ — Concentration, scale by scale. The budgeted family \(v_\sigma(s) = \sigma^{-1/2}\operatorname{sech}(s/\sigma)\) of \(\S 5\) concentrates: the fraction of its total \(2\) inside \(|s| \leq W\sigma\) is exactly \(\tanh W\). (a) Find the half-width \(W\) that captures \(90\%\), \(99\%\), \(99.9\%\) — exactly, and verify each. (b) Show the law is honest to the family's shape: substitute \(w = s/\sigma\) and read the total and the fraction as one and the same integral. (c) Open end: build the same census for the Gaussian family \(g_\sigma(s) = \sigma^{-1/2} e^{-s^2/2\sigma^2}\) — which fraction law replaces \(\tanh\), and which family concentrates faster, in units of its own half-width? What does the comparison say about "half-width" as a unit?

Hint

(a) Invert \(\tanh\): the half-width for fraction \(f\) is \(\operatorname{arctanh}(f) = \tfrac12 \ln\frac{1+f}{1-f}\). (b) \(\int \operatorname{sech}^2 w \, dw = \tanh w\). (c) The Gaussian's integral is the error function.

Worked answer

(a) \(W = \operatorname{arctanh}(f) = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+f}{1-f}\): for \(f = 0.90\), \(W = 1.4722\); for \(f = 0.99\), \(W = 2.6467\); for \(f = 0.999\), \(W = 3.8002\). Each verified by the lab: the machine-computed fractions at those half-widths read \(0.900000\), \(0.990000\), \(0.999000\). (b) The substitution \(w = s/\sigma\) carries \(\int v_\sigma^2\,ds\) to \(\int \operatorname{sech}^2 w\,dw\) — total \(2\) for every \(\sigma\) — and the windowed fraction to \(\frac{1}{2}\int_{-W}^{W}\operatorname{sech}^2 w\,dw = \tanh W\): total and fraction are literally the same integral, windowed or not. This is the self-similarity of \(\S 6\)'s far door, in one line: after the rescaling the family is the same shape, which is why one number, \(\tanh W\), carries the whole concentration story. (c) For the Gaussian, the same substitution gives total \(\sqrt{\pi}\) and fraction \(\operatorname{erf}(W)\): the half-widths for \(90\%\), \(99\%\), \(99.9\%\) read approximately \(1.163\), \(1.821\), \(2.327\) — smaller than the sech's \(1.472\), \(2.647\), \(3.800\) at every level: the Gaussian concentrates faster in units of its own half-width, at all three depths. The open finding stands: "half-width" is not a neutral unit — each family measures concentration in its own coin, and the comparison only becomes honest after both are renormalized to the same budget, exactly as \(\S 5\) renormalized the bump. Machine-verified where closed; the tail trade-offs are the exploration's open end.

Exercise 7 ◐ — The real room: chambers, walls, and the associahedron. Keep every coordinate real: five labeled points on the circle \(\mathbb{RP}^1\), parked at \((z_1, z_2, z_3) = (0, 1, \infty)\), and the chamber \(0 < z_4 < z_5 < 1\) — one of the \(\tfrac{(5-1)!}{2} = 12\) chambers, one per cyclic ordering. (a) The chamber's closure in \(\overline{M}_{0,5}(\mathbb{R})\) meets exactly five boundary divisors — list them, and explain why the list is the census of adjacent pairs in one cyclic order. (b) The chamber in coordinates is the open triangle \(0 < z_4 < z_5 < 1\), and its compactified closure is a pentagon: three edges from the triangle's walls, two from blown-up corners. Name the two corners that blow up, name the corner that does not, and check \(3 + 2 = 5\) against the Catalan number \(C_3 = \tfrac{1}{4}\binom{6}{3}\). (c) Open end: the closure of one chamber of the \((n+1)\)-point real room \(\overline{M}_{0,n+1}(\mathbb{R})\) is the Stasheff associahedron \(K_n\) — dimension \(n - 2\), vertices the Catalan number \(C_{n-1}\). Audit the claim at \(n = 4\) (this exercise's own room), and say what \(K_3\) and \(K_5\) are.

Hint

(a) A degeneration reachable from inside the chamber is a collision of adjacent labels in the cyclic order \((1, 4, 5, 2, 3)\). (b) Which corners of the triangle are triple collisions, and which are two separate ones? (c) \(C_{n-1} = \tfrac{1}{n}\binom{2n-2}{n-1}\).

Worked answer

(a) The chamber fixes the cyclic order \((1, 4, 5, 2, 3)\) around the circle, and a degeneration reachable from inside it is a collision of adjacent labels in that order: \(\{1,4\}\), \(\{4,5\}\), \(\{5,2\}\), \(\{2,3\}\), \(\{3,1\}\) — divisors \(D_{14}\), \(D_{45}\), \(D_{25}\), \(D_{23}\), \(D_{13}\), five, and the lab's census confirms the list. (b) The triangle's three sides are the walls \(D_{14}\) (\(z_4 \to 0\)), \(D_{45}\) (\(z_5 \to z_4\)), \(D_{25}\) (\(z_5 \to 1\)). Of the corners: \((0, 1)\) is two separate collisions — \(z_4 \to z_1\), \(z_5 \to z_2\) — with no internal freedom left to name, so it stays a single vertex where \(D_{14}\) meets \(D_{25}\). But \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\) are triple collisions — Exemplar 4's corners — and each is blown up: the \((0,0)\) corner becomes a new edge, a piece of \(D_{23}\), carrying the bubble's internal bookkeeping (how \(z_4\) and \(z_5\) made their approach to \(z_1\)); the \((1,1)\) corner becomes a piece of \(D_{13}\). Three walls plus two blowup edges: \(3 + 2 = 5\), and the pentagon's five vertices — the chamber's five maximal degenerations — are counted by \(C_3 = \tfrac{1}{4}\binom{6}{3} = 5\). (c) At \(n = 4\) (five points): dimension \(n - 2 = 2\), vertices \(C_3 = 5\) — the pentagon, this exercise's own answer, so \(K_4\) is the honest face of the five-point room. \(K_3\) (four points): dimension 1, \(C_2 = \tfrac{1}{3}\binom{4}{2} = 2\) vertices — an interval; indeed each chamber of the real four-point room is an open interval whose closure adds two boundary points, Exemplar 2's three holes taken one chamber at a time. \(K_5\) (six points): dimension 3, \(C_4 = \tfrac{1}{5}\binom{8}{4} = 14\) vertices — the first associahedron this book's rooms cannot draw flat. Machine-verified: the adjacent-pair census, the wall list, the blowup count, and all three Catalan numbers.
