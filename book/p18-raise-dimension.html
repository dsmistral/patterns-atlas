The Patterns Atlas · P18 · the first move of tier 2

Lift to a higher dimension

Some problems are flat by accident. The plane you are working in may be a slice of a bigger room; the numbers you are allowed to use may be the shadow of a room with more numbers in it; the one-dimensional integral you are stuck on may be one factor of a two-dimensional area that polar coordinates measure in three lines. A 2D problem may be a shadow of an easy 3D one — the shadow has the difficulty, not the object. Intersections that refuse to exist downstairs meet comfortably upstairs; solutions the small room forbids, the big one supplies; coincidences that look miraculous in the plane are the meeting line of two ordinary planes in space. The eighteenth move is the lift, done honestly: notice the bigger room, name the map that casts the shadow, prove the easy version upstairs, and then pay for the trip back down — because the descent is where all the fine print lives. Some answers do not survive the return trip. Some rooms cannot be descended from at all. And some rooms charge rent the problem cannot pay. This page builds the move on a theorem the plane cannot prove about itself from incidence alone, repairs the census of 625 machines that B07 left broken, and evaluates the most famous integral in mathematics by first refusing to do it.

New word: lift. A lift of a problem is a bigger problem that contains it: the same objects, embedded in a room with more room — a plane inside space, the real numbers inside the complex ones, one integral standing beside a second copy of itself. Lifting is licensed only when you can say what the bigger room is and how the original sits inside it; "think bigger" is not a lift, it is a mood. New word: projection. A projection is the map that casts the shadow: a function from the bigger room onto the smaller one — for example, the map that collapses a solid onto a plane by drawing straight lines from a fixed center. Projections are honest when they are named, because they forget things on purpose, and what they forget is exactly the fine print this page keeps auditing. New word: perspectivity. Two figures are in perspective from a point when the lines through their corresponding points all pass through one fixed point, and in perspective from a line when the meets of their corresponding sides all lie on one fixed line. P17 met these two relations as trading partners; this page proves the theorem that says they always trade — Desargues's theorem, which the plane cannot prove from its incidence facts alone, and which the lift explains.

1 · Before we begin

Tier 1 is complete — the spine's seventeen moves, one breath. P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on, P13 convicted whole families by manufacturing ever-smaller criminals, P14 built the one object no list can contain, P15 trapped the unwritable between two computable walls, P16 licensed whole problems to change clothes, and P17 flipped every problem to its twin, half of geometry proving the other half for free. Tier 2 begins here — the same ladder, gentler grades, university-ward — and the first climb leans on three tools the spine already issued. P07, because the lift is the auxiliary construction at its largest scale: the object nobody handed us is sometimes a whole extra dimension, built on purpose, used a handful of times, and then folded away. P16, because a lift is licensed only when the shadow map is a dictionary P16 would certify — explicit, one-for-one on the objects that matter, and checked for exactly what it carries and what it forgets. And P17, because this page's flagship theorem is P17's own nearest door: Desargues's theorem is self-dual, the one mirror that gave nothing back, and its natural proof is the lift. P17's repair gets explained too — the points at infinity that healed the parallel lines turn out to be a shadow fact: the horizon of a plane is what you see when you stand outside it.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Some jams are not stubbornness; they are architecture. Lines that refuse to meet, equations with no real roots, an integral with no elementary antiderivative, a coincidence whose cause cannot be found anywhere inside the plane it lives in — each of these is the small room's report that the complete object lives somewhere bigger, and each has a standard repair that quietly is the lift: add a point at infinity (P17's repair, a shadow of the horizon), allow a number whose square is \(-1\), stand a second copy of the integral beside the first and multiply. The good news: once the room above is named, the proofs upstairs are routinely trivial — three sentences of incidence where the plane demanded a page of algebra, a diagonalization where the reals offered nothing, a polar change of coordinates where the line was stuck. The bad news, and this page spends three exhibits on it: the room above hands back more than you asked for (§5, Exhibit A); some rooms are not attached to your floor at all (Exhibit B); and some rooms solve other people's problems, not yours (Exhibit C). The lift is the most expensive habit the book has taught so far — it builds furniture in a room the problem never mentioned — and the only way to spend that much honestly is to know, before the proof starts, exactly what the trip back down is going to cost.

2 · The coincidence that will not explain itself

Two triangles, cold, in the ordinary plane. The first has corners \(A = (1,0)\), \(B = (0,1)\), \(C = (2,3)\). The second has corners \(A' = (4,0)\), \(B' = (0,2)\), \(C' = (-4,-6)\). Draw the three lines \(AA'\), \(BB'\), \(CC'\) and check where they lead: the line through \(A\) and \(A'\) is the \(x\)-axis, the line through \(B\) and \(B'\) is the \(y\)-axis, and the line through \(C\) and \(C'\) is \(y = \frac{3}{2}x\) — all three pass through the origin \(O = (0,0)\). The triangles are in perspective from \(O\): the onramp's word, live. So far, a drawing.

Now the strange part. Extend corresponding sides and let them meet — three two-line solves, the kind P02 taught you in the first weeks. \(AB\) is \(x + y = 1\); \(A'B'\) is \(x + 2y = 4\); solving, \(P = (-2, 3)\). \(BC\) is \(y = x + 1\); \(B'C'\) is \(y = 2x + 2\); solving, \(Q = (-1, 0)\). \(CA\) is \(y = 3x - 3\); \(C'A'\) is \(y = \frac{3}{4}x - 3\); solving, \(R = (0, -3)\). Now plot \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\). They lie on one line — the line \(y = -3x - 3\): check all three, \(3 = 6 - 3\), \(0 = 3 - 3\), \(-3 = 0 - 3\). Run it again with different triangles in perspective from a different point, and the meets are collinear again. Every time, for every pair of perspective triangles in the plane. This is Desargues's theorem, and the claim is now pinned the P01 way: if two triangles are in perspective from a point, then the three meets of their corresponding sides are collinear.

Try to prove it. In the plane, with coordinates, a proof exists — grind the algebra for a general pair of triangles and the collinearity falls out after a page. But the grind verifies the way a fingerprint verifies: it confirms and explains nothing. Why should three intersection points, computed from six arbitrary-looking corners, refuse to form a triangle? The meet points do what they do for a reason, and the reason is not anywhere in the plane. And the jam is deeper than a long grind: the plane's own incidence facts — points, lines, throughness, the axioms that fit on a card — cannot prove Desargues at all, and that is a theorem, not a complaint. David Hilbert, in lectures of 1898–99, built a plane that satisfies every incidence axiom and in which Desargues fails (§5, Exhibit B). Whatever proves Desargues in your plane, it is more than incidence. The missing ingredient is the one this page is about.

A skeptic should now object, and the objection is the right one. What exactly is "the room above" — who chooses it, and what if the choice is a trick? What does the shadow map carry and what does it forget? And why does a proof written upstairs license any conclusion downstairs, in a world the proof never mentioned? The next three sections answer in order: name the move and its promises (\S3), watch it work — the spatial proof, a census repaired, the famous integral (\S4) — and then study the wreckage where it fails (\S5). The pattern to hold onto is the one the whole spine has taught: a machine is only as good as its checks, and the lift's checks are called the descent.

3 · Name the move

P18 · Lift to a higher dimension — prove the easy shadow-free problem upstairs

When a problem jams in a way that smells like missing room — intersections that do not exist, solutions the setting forbids, coincidences with no visible cause — do not fight it where it stands. Name the bigger room in which the given objects sit (a plane inside space, the reals inside the complexes, one integral beside a second copy of itself) and name the shadow map that projects the big room onto the small one. Then prove upstairs, where the extra room turns the jam into a straight line, and descend honestly: read the answer back through the shadow map, check it against the question actually asked, and accept — and use — the possibility that no descent exists. The lift gives room, not truth: everything gained upstairs is provisional until the return trip is paid for.

In words: some problems are hard because they are shadows, and a shadow's difficulty is not the object's difficulty. The move has three working parts. First, the room must be nameable — a specific bigger structure the given objects embed into, with the embedding written down. "It would be easier in higher dimensions" is not a lift; it is a sigh. Second, the proof upstairs must use the extra room on purpose — the new dimension has to be where the work happens (two planes meeting in a line; a complex root that is genuinely complex; a polar factor \(r\) the line never had). If the upstairs proof never touches the extra coordinate, the room was decoration. Third, the descent must be audited: what comes back down is checked against the original question, and what the projection forgot is priced. The lift is the only move in the book so far that can fail at the last step after succeeding at the first two, which is exactly why its failure exhibits (\S5) are the best classrooms it has.

Box P18.A · The three promises of a lift

These are P16's certification checks, restated for rooms instead of dictionaries — a lift licenses nothing until all three pass.

Promise 1 — the original problem lives upstairs intact. There is an embedding of the given objects into the bigger room, and it carries the problem's relations: the plane's points, lines, and incidences all sit inside space; the real matrices sit inside the complex ones unchanged; the integral's values are unchanged by being written as one factor of a pair. If the problem does not survive the trip up intact, the room is not bigger — it is different, and different rooms solve different problems, not this one.

Promise 2 — the extra room is bigger exactly where the jam was. The obstruction must dissolve upstairs: lines that would not meet now lie in a configuration where meeting is forced by incidence; numbers that had no roots have them; the extra coordinate turns an unexplained coincidence into a visible incidence. The test is honest and local: does the upstairs proof use the new room at the exact spot where the downstairs proof jammed? A lift that does not pass this test is a change of subject.

Promise 3 — the descent is checked, never assumed. Answers read back down must answer the original question, and the checking is not a formality: projections forget (Exhibit A's junk room hands back answers nobody asked for); some floors admit no room above at all (Exhibit B's non-Desarguesian planes); and a room's tools can simply not fit the problem (Exhibit C's integral, which the polar room cannot help). The descent is where the lift's honesty lives — the same place P16 put a dictionary's honesty: in the checks, not the vibes.

Box P18.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Geometry: Desargues's theorem, proved in space in three sentences this page (\S4); Dandelin's spheres (1822) — the foci of a sliced cone appearing as the touching points of spheres inscribed in it (\S7). Algebra: the reals inside the complexes, Hamilton's 1837 "algebraic couples" — repairing an eigenvalue census wholesale in \S4, industrialized as a whole later page (P27). Calculus: the Gaussian integral (\S4) — one dimension lifted to two, where the extra room is polar coordinates; the same lift is how Stirling's formula is honestly proved. Combinatorics: an indeterminate is a dimension — lift an integer identity into \(\mathbb{Z}[t]\), prove it where factoring is honest, specialize back down: P20's entire engine, and the cheapest lift in the book.

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the jam smells like missing room: a meeting that refuses to happen, a solution the setting forbids, a symmetric object with broken symmetry, "parallel" standing in the way. Second, the room and the shadow map can be named explicitly — a lift without a named projection is a metaphor, and metaphors prove nothing (P16's discipline, restated one level up). Third, the bigger room owns a tool the small one lacks — incidence of planes, complex arithmetic, radial coordinates — and you can say in advance what the descent will cost. One key alone is a guess; two keys, a hypothesis; all three, a plan.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · Desargues — the proof the plane cannot give itself

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim), P07 (the auxiliary construction — an entire extra dimension), P16 (the shadow map, certified as a dictionary).

Build the room. Take three-dimensional space with coordinates \((x,y,z)\), and in it two planes that cross: \(\Pi_1\), the plane \(z = 1\), and \(\Pi_2\), the plane \(x = 1\). Two non-parallel planes meet in exactly one line, and here that line is \(\ell = \{(1, t, 1) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}\) — the points with \(x = 1\) and \(z = 1\), and any \(y\) at all. Hold onto \(\ell\); it is the entire engine of the proof. Now put one triangle in each plane, in perspective from the origin \(O^* = (0,0,0)\):

Triangle 1, in \(\Pi_1\) (\(z=1\))Triangle 2, in \(\Pi_2\) (\(x=1\))Scalar on the ray from \(O^*\)
\(A = (2,1,1)\)\(A' = (1, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2})\)\(A' = \frac{1}{2}A\)
\(B = (3,2,1)\)\(B' = (1, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3})\)\(B' = \frac{1}{3}B\)
\(C = (-2,1,1)\)\(C' = (1, -\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2})\)\(C' = -\frac{1}{2}C\)

Each primed vertex sits on its ray from \(O^*\) — the first two between \(O^*\) and their partners, the third on the opposite ray — so the lines \(AA'\), \(BB'\), \(CC'\) all pass through \(O^*\). Two triangles, in two different planes, in perspective from a point: Desargues's setup, one dimension up.

The proof is three sentences. The plane through \(O^*\), \(A\), and \(B\) contains \(A'\) and \(B'\) too (they sit on the rays \(O^*A\) and \(O^*B\)), so it contains both lines \(AB\) and \(A'B'\); call it \(\Sigma_{AB}\). The line \(AB\) also lies in \(\Pi_1\), and the line \(A'B'\) also lies in \(\Pi_2\), so their meet \(P = AB \cap A'B'\) lies in \(\Pi_1\), in \(\Pi_2\), and hence on \(\ell\). The same two sentences with \(B, C\) in place of \(A, B\) put \(Q = BC \cap B'C'\) on \(\ell\), and with \(C, A\) they put \(R = CA \cap C'A'\) on \(\ell\). Three meets, one line — Desargues, proved. And the meets can be watched: computing them, \(P = (1, 0, 1)\), \(Q = (1, \frac{8}{5}, 1)\), \(R = (1, 1, 1)\), and each one indeed has \(x = 1\), \(z = 1\) — this page's lab verifies every meet by substitution on both of its lines, both ways.

The descent, watched. The three sentences just proved Desargues for triangles in different planes; §2 asked about two triangles in the same plane. The gap between them is the return trip, and the honest way down is a construction — which means it can be run, checked, and audited. Run it on the very triangles of §2. Choose a center off the plane: \(S = (0,0,5)\), straight above the origin, and on the line from \(S\) down to the planar center \(O = (0,0,0)\) choose the lifted center \(O_1 = (0,0,2)\). Now walk each unprimed vertex of §2 partway up its ray from \(S\), and build each primed vertex as a two-line meet in a plane that contains \(S\): the line through \(O_1\) and the lifted vertex, met with the ray from \(S\) through the primed vertex. Concretely:

\(A = (1,0,0)\)\(A_1 = (\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{5}{2})\)\(A' = (4,0,0)\)\(A_2 = O_1A_1 \cap SA' = (\frac{4}{3}, 0, \frac{10}{3})\)
\(B = (0,1,0)\)\(B_1 = (0, \frac{3}{5}, 2)\)\(B' = (0,2,0)\)\(B_2 = O_1B_1 \cap SB' = (0, \frac{6}{5}, 2)\)
\(C = (2,3,0)\)\(C_1 = (\frac{4}{3}, 2, \frac{5}{3})\)\(C' = (-4,-6,0)\)\(C_2 = O_1C_1 \cap SC' = (-2, -3, \frac{5}{2})\)

Why the two lines always meet — the one geometric sentence the construction needs: the line \(O_1A_1\) and the ray \(SA'\) both lie in the plane through \(S\), \(O\), and \(A\) (the first because \(O_1\) sits on \(SO\) and \(A_1\) on \(SA\); the second because \(A'\) sits on the line \(OA\), which is in that plane), and two lines in one plane meet unless parallel — the parallel case is a measure-zero accident this construction's choices avoid, and the lab checks all three meets by substitution. Now audit the structure: the two lifted triangles lie in two different planes (both different from \(z = 0\)); the lines \(A_1A_2\), \(B_1B_2\), \(C_1C_2\) all pass through \(O_1\) — \(C_2\) sits on the ray opposite \(C_1\), which perspectivity permits; and the center \(S\) lies on none of the lines involved. So the spatial theorem applies: the three meets of the lifted triangles' corresponding sides are collinear, on the line where the two lifted planes cross. Finally project the whole structure from \(S\) back onto the plane \(z = 0\). Projection from a point carries lines to lines and preserves meets; the six lifted vertices project exactly onto §2's six vertices — check one in your head: the ray from \(S = (0,0,5)\) through \(A_1 = (\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{5}{2})\) continues past \(A_1\) to \((1, 0, 0) = A\), and the same ray hits the plane at \(A\); the lab verifies all six — so the lifted configuration's side-line meets project exactly onto \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\), and the lifted axis projects to a line through them. The line \(y = -3x - 3\) is the shadow of the axis upstairs. §2's coincidence is explained — not verified, explained: the three meets are collinear because they are shadows of three points on one line, in a room the plane could not see.

What the construction teaches. The descent consumed three things the plane cannot supply by itself: a point \(S\) off the plane, a lifted center \(O_1\), and the freedom to leave and come back. A proof of planar Desargues from planar incidence alone would consume none of these — and Exhibit B will show why waiting for one is a category error: there are planes for which the whole construction is impossible, because there is no \(S\). Your plane keeps Desargues because it is a shadow, and the shadow has a cause one dimension up. That is the pattern's signature sentence, and every example on this page is a variation on it.

Example 2 · The census repaired — six hundred twenty-five machines, one bigger room

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim, B07's deferred verdict), P16 (the embedding, certified), B07 (the hunt, run upstairs).

B07 ended a census and left a hole in it, named and deferred. The census: all \(625\) machines with entries from \(-2\) to \(2\), verdict by discriminant — \(416\) two-rail, \(61\) with a doubled factor (five pure stretches that own every direction, fifty-six shears that own one), and \(148\) empty-handed: no real eigenvalue, no rail, nothing the machine leaves alone. The quarter-turn \(Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\) was the exhibit: \(\lambda^2 + 1\) has no real roots, and B07 said so and stopped — "the completion that gives it rails lives over the complex numbers, is named here, and is deferred." This page pays that debt, and the paying is a lift in full dress.

Name the room. The bigger room is the complex numbers, and the nameability discipline — the lift's first promise — is not a formality here: it is a theorem about how the room was built. William Rowan Hamilton, in 1837, constructed the complex numbers out of real ones as algebraic couples: a complex number is a pair \((a,b)\) of reals, added componentwise, multiplied by the rule \((a,b)(c,d) = (ac - bd,\; ad + bc)\). Inside that room sits a copy of the old floor, the embedding \(a \mapsto (a,0)\) — addition and multiplication carry over untouched, a P16 dictionary in the smallest possible room. And one computation tells you what the new room is: \((0,1)(0,1) = (-1,0)\), so the pair \((0,1)\) — call it \(i\) — is a manufactured square root of \(-1\); and multiplying any couple by \(i\) sends \((c,d)\) to \((-d,c)\) — a quarter-turn. The census's own broken machine is the room's coordinate arithmetic: multiplication by \(i\) is the turn \(Q\). The lab checks every step of this paragraph by substitution, the B07 way.

Prove upstairs. The lift of B07's hunt is one instruction: run the same two steps, but let \(\lambda\) live in the couples. The quarter-turn: \(\lambda^2 + 1 = 0\) now has solutions \(\lambda = i\) and \(\lambda = -i\), and step two finds rails for both — the vector \((1, i)\) with factor \(-i\), since \(Q(1, i) = (-i, 1) = -i \times (1, i)\), and the vector \((1, -i)\) with factor \(i\), since \(Q(1, -i) = (i, 1) = i \times (1, -i)\). Feed them: both hold by substitution. The empty-handed verdict is repaired one machine at a time and then wholesale: over the couples, every one of the \(148\) empty-handed machines owns a pair \(\alpha \pm \beta i\), conjugate twins with \(\beta \ne 0\), and B07's two Vieta readings survive the trip — the twins sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant, exactly as the polynomial's coefficients promised; the lab runs both readings on all \(148\). The census closes at \(625 \times 2 = 1250\): every machine owns exactly two eigenvalues upstairs, counting multiplicity, and none is missing. What was "empty-handed" was a census of the floor, not of the machine.

A concrete repair, watched. Take \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\) from the \(148\): trace \(2\), determinant \(5\), discriminant \(4 - 20 = -16 < 0\). Upstairs the hunt returns \(1 + 2i\) and \(1 - 2i\) — sum \(2\), product \(5\), both readings exact — and the rails are real to find: \(M(1, -i) = (1 + 2i,\; 2 - i) = (1 + 2i) \times (1, -i)\), and the conjugate rail \(M(1, i) = (1 - 2i) \times (1, i)\) just as conjugately. The room did exactly what the three keys promised: the jam (no real roots) dissolved at the exact spot where the floor jammed (the discriminant), and the new room was used on purpose — the eigenvector's second coordinate is the new coordinate.

Descend honestly — twice, once wrong. Here is the lift's whole tuition in one contrast. The wrong descent is the easy one: drop the imaginary parts and read the rail \((1, -i)\) as the arrow \((1, 0)\). The lab kills it in one feed: \(M(1,0) = (1,2)\), not a multiple of \((1,0)\) — the projection handed back an answer nobody asked for, an arrow that is nobody's eigenvector. Forgetting is what projections do; what the shadow map forgets is the fine print. The honest descent reads the pair \(\alpha \pm \beta i\) as one real statement: over the reals, \(M\) cannot be a pure stretch, but it is the next uniform in P05's wardrobe — a scaled turn, \(\begin{bmatrix} \alpha & -\beta \\ \beta & \alpha \end{bmatrix}\), stretching every arrow by \(\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2} = \sqrt{\det M}\) while turning it through the angle the pair names. For \(M\): \(\alpha = 1\), \(\beta = 2\), scale \(\sqrt{5}\); for the quarter-turn, \(\alpha = 0\), \(\beta = 1\), scale \(1\), angle a quarter — the census's verdict, recovered as one real sentence. Nothing complex comes back down; what comes down is the shape the complex pair certifies. That is what a checked descent looks like: the answer to the question actually asked, which was never "does the plane contain a rail?" — B07 answered that, honestly, no — but "what does the machine own?" — and the census, repaired, says: a conjugate pair, Vieta-true, and down here a scaled turn.

Example 3 · The famous integral — refused, then done in two dimensions

Moves used: P01 (the pinned claim: a value, exact), P09 (the invariant — rotation — that polar coordinates cash), P15 (the walls that trap a value but cannot produce one), P16 (the pairing, certified as a dictionary).

The pinned problem is one line: evaluate \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\, dx\). The jam is a theorem, not a mood. The antiderivative of \(e^{-x^2}\) is not an elementary function — not stubbornness, not a missing trick: Joseph Liouville, in work of the 1830s, built the theory that proves some elementary functions have no elementary antiderivative, and this one is the canonical case. P15's discipline still applies — the value can be trapped between ever-tighter computable walls — but walls trap; they do not evaluate. And yet the value is famously clean: \(\sqrt{\pi}\). No amount of grinding on the line produces it. The history is honest about where it came from: Abraham de Moivre met the shape's normalizing constant in 1733, in the normal curve, with Stirling's formula pinning the constant as \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\); Pierre-Simon Laplace extracted the value itself in the 1770s — Gauss credited him explicitly in 1809 — and the two-dimensional trick that evaluates it is usually credited to Siméon-Denis Poisson (the integral's older name, the Euler–Poisson integral, keeps the record). The trick is this page's third lift.

Refuse, then lift. The move is to decline the one-dimensional problem — no antiderivative exists, and pretending otherwise is decoration — and stand beside the integral a second copy of itself, in a new variable: \(I^2 = I \times I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx \times \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2} dy\). Name the room: the plane, with coordinates \((x,y)\). Name the shadow map, in reverse: the double integral \(\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\, dA\) over the whole plane, projected to a product of two one-dimensional integrals — a legal exchange exactly because the integrand is positive and both factors converge; this is fine print to read aloud, not bury, and the page returns to it at the audit. The one-dimensional problem is now a slice of a two-dimensional one, and the slice was the hard way round.

The room's tool. The line had no tool for \(e^{-x^2}\); the plane owns one immediately, because \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\) is P09's invariant in person: the integrand \(e^{-r^2}\) does not care about direction, and direction is what polar coordinates sell. Change coordinates — the area element carries the Jacobian \(r\), itself half of the answer's work — and the room pays out:

\[I^2 \;=\; \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\, r\, dr\, d\theta \;=\; 2\pi \int_0^\infty e^{-r^2} r\, dr \;=\; 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \;=\; \pi,\]

because the substitution \(u = r^2\) turns the radial integral into \(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\, du = \frac{1}{2}\) — elementary, first-week calculus, the one integral the line could do all along. The extra dimension was used on purpose: the work happens entirely in the new coordinate \(r\) that the line never had, exactly where the line jammed.

Descend, audited. Upstairs handed back \(I^2 = \pi\), and the descent has exactly one decision in it, so watch it close. \(I^2 = \pi\) allows \(I = \sqrt{\pi}\) and \(I = -\sqrt{\pi}\); the audit that chooses is the integrand's positivity — \(e^{-x^2} > 0\) on the whole line, so \(I > 0\), so \(I = \sqrt{\pi} \approx 1.7724538509\). The lab checks the full chain numerically — the line integral to ten decimal places against \(\sqrt{\pi}\), the double integral against \(\pi\), the radial integral's \(\frac{1}{2}\), and the substitution \(u = r^2\) both ways — and every step feeds. Then the last audit, the one that separates a descent from a wish: did the answer answer the question? The question was a value on the line, and a value came back, in closed form, checkable to any precision. What did not come back down: an antiderivative — \(e^{-x^2}\) still has no elementary one, and the honest page says the lift evaluated the number, not the primitive. The room hands back what was asked; what it cannot hand back, it says so. And the receipt has a counterpart warning that §5 spends Exhibit C on: the same trick, run on \(e^{-x^4}\), stalls — the pairing is still legal, but the pair integrand is not radial, and the room's one tool does not bite.

Three lifts, one ledger. Space explained the plane's coincidence (Example 1); the couples repaired the machine census (Example 2); the plane-beside-the-line evaluated the famous integral (Example 3). Three different jams — a coincidence with no cause in the room, a census with a hole in it, a value with no elementary route — and one repeated shape of solution: name the room, name the shadow map, work where the new room is doing the work, and pay for the trip home with checks, not vibes. The pattern-box promises passed every time, and the passing was never free: each descent had a decision in it (the projection from \(S\), the sign of the pair's meaning, the sign of \(\sqrt{\pi}\)), and the §5 wreckage is where those decisions go wrong.

5 · When it fails

The lift is the first move of the book that can succeed twice — room named, proof done — and still fail on the way home. Its wreckage is therefore the best classroom it has, and this page promised three exhibits: the room that hands back what nobody asked for, the floor with no room above at all, and the room that solves other people's problems. Each is verified, not sketched; wreckage deserves the same audits as treasure.

Exhibit A · The junk room — a projection is honest only relative to a question. Example 2 already caught the cheap version: drop the imaginary parts of the rail \((1, -i)\) and the "answer" \((1, 0)\) dies by one feed, \(M(1,0) = (1,2)\), nobody's eigenvector. Here is the subtler merchandise. Take Example 1's lift and swap the shadow map: instead of projecting from \(S\), collapse the room along the \(z\)-axis — drop the third coordinate and keep the first two. The map is honest-looking, simple, and wrong for this question. The lab runs it. The collapsed triangles even stay in perspective from \(O\) — an accident of the construction's geometry — and their side meets land at \((-2, 3)\), \((-\frac{4}{7}, 0)\), \((0, -\frac{6}{5})\): true points, computable, checkable, and not \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) — one of the three happens to coincide with \(P\), which is exactly the kind of luck that makes junk convincing. And those three meets are collinear — the plane obeys Desargues, so the junk is even true — but on the line \(21x + 10y + 12 = 0\), not the axis \(y = -3x - 3\) the question asked about. That is the junk room's inventory: true facts about triangles nobody drew. The lesson is not "collapse maps are bad"; it is that no projection is good or bad in itself — the shadow map is part of the lift's identity, certified P16-style for the specific question, and a map that forgets different things answers a different question.

Exhibit B · Floors with no room above — the plane that breaks Desargues. §2 claimed the plane cannot prove Desargues from its incidence facts alone, and named Hilbert's lectures of 1898–99 as the proof. Here is the exhibit behind the claim, finite and checkable to the last arithmetic fact. Build nine numbers: the field \(\mathbb{F}_9\), the three digits \(0, 1, 2\) adjoined a new symbol \(t\) with \(t^2 = -1\) — complex-style arithmetic mod 3. Now twist the multiplication: \(a \circ b = ab\) when \(a\) is a square, \(a \circ b = a \cdot b^3\) when \(a\) is a nonsquare. One line shows the twist biting: \((1+t) \circ t = 1 + 2t\), while \(1 \circ t + t \circ t = t + 2\) — not equal; distributing over a sum from the right is gone. The lab verifies what survives: \(\circ\) is associative with identity and inverses, and it distributes from the left — a nearfield — Dickson's twist (1905, the first proper nearfield), the construction Veblen and Wedderburn used for their plane. Its affine plane — points the pairs, lines \(y = m \circ x + b\) plus the verticals — is an honest plane: the lab checks all 81 points and 90 lines, nine points on every line, one line through every pair, at most one meet for every pair of lines; completed projectively, \(9^2 + 9 + 1 = 91\) points and 91 lines: the order-nine plane Veblen and Wedderburn published in 1907, the first finite non-Desarguesian plane. And Desargues fails in it, on small coordinates. Two triangles in perspective from the origin — \(A = (t,t)\), \(A' = (2t, 2t)\) on the line \(y = x\); \(B = (t, 2)\), \(B' = (1, t)\) on \(y = t \circ x\); \(C = (0, t)\), \(C' = (0, 1+t)\) on \(x = 0\) — the lab checks all three lines through the origin, then computes the side meets: \(P = (t,\; 2+t)\), \(Q = (2t,\; 1+2t)\), \(R = (1+t,\; t)\), each verified to lie on both of its lines. And they are not collinear. Desargues's conclusion is false here; no lift will rescue it, because — and this is the exhibit's whole point — a projective plane embeds in three-space exactly when Desargues holds in it (Veblen–Young), so this floor admits no room above at all: Example 1's construction has no first step, no \(S\) to choose, no horizon to borrow. The lift's precondition is not a formality; it is a theorem with counterexamples, and waiting for this plane to prove Desargues from incidence is not patience but a category error. Your plane keeps Desargues because your plane is embeddable — §4's room was never optional furniture; it was your plane's own certificate.

Exhibit C · The room that solves other people's problems — \(\int e^{-x^4}\), stalled. Walk the Gaussian recipe one door down. The jam is real: \(J = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^4} dx\) has no elementary antiderivative (Liouville again, same theorem). The room is nameable: the same plane, the same pairing, \(J^2 = \iint e^{-(x^4+y^4)}\, dA\), the same shadow map run in reverse. The lift is legal. And upstairs, nothing happens: the room's one tool does not bite, because \(x^4 + y^4 = r^4(\cos^4\theta + \sin^4\theta)\), and \(\cos^4\theta + \sin^4\theta\) is not a constant — it is \(1\) at \(0°\) and \(\frac{1}{2}\) at \(45°\) (lab-checked), so the pair integrand is not radial, the angular integral refuses to separate, and the evaluation stalls with both promises technically kept. The toll is visible if you ignore the stall and compute radially anyway: \(2\pi \int_0^\infty e^{-r^4} r\, dr = \pi^{3/2}/2 \approx 2.7841\), while the true \(J^2 \approx 0.8216\) — a gap of nearly two, the price of pretending the tool fit. Run the three keys honestly and the failure is diagnosed before the climb: key one holds (the jam is real), key two holds (room and shadow map named), key three fails — the polar room owns exactly one tool, and the problem's pair integrand cannot use it. \(J\)'s value comes from a different room entirely, the Gamma integral: the substitution \(u = x^4\) sends \(J = \Gamma(5/4) \approx 0.9064024771\), and the lab verifies the whole smooth chain numerically. The Gaussian worked because its pair integrand is genuinely radial — P09's invariant, present in one problem and absent in the other. The lesson: a lift is a tool-fit question, not a size question. Before climbing, ask what the room owns, not how big it is.

Scope and price of P18

Three boundaries, stated while the exhibits are still warm. (i) The room and the shadow map are both load-bearing. "It would be easier in higher dimensions" is a sigh, not a lift; Exhibit A shows that even a correct room with a swapped map hands back junk, and Box P18's first sentence licenses nothing until both are named. (ii) The descent is a separate theorem from the lift. Examples 1–3 succeeded because their descents were audited decisions; Exhibit C stalls with the lift half-succeeding, and Exhibit B never leaves the ground floor. The lift is the only move so far whose final step can fail after every earlier step passes — which is why its cost is paid in checks, never in confidence. (iii) What this page defers, it names. The complex room returns wholesale as its own pattern, P27, with Hamilton's couples as its foundation; the polynomial lift — an indeterminate is a dimension — is P20's entire engine, the cheapest lift in the book; Stirling's formula honestly proved by the same two-dimensional trick is a later page's debt; and the finite nearfield planes stay exhibits, not tools — Wedderburn's theorem (a finite division ring is a field) is the boundary that keeps them from ever being coordinates, and it is a story for another book. The price tag, said once: the lift is P07 at full scale — a whole dimension built on purpose, used a handful of times, folded away — and the audit trail is what separates that expense from furniture nobody sits on.

6 · What comes next

Doors this opens

Three doors, near to far. The nearest is P19, the next move of the spine, which trades rooms sideways instead of up: when you cannot exhibit an object, step into the room of every candidate at once and count — if the failures cannot fill the room, a survivor exists. That is a lift in this page's exact sense, with the names changed: the room is the space of all configurations, the shadow map is the count of the bad ones, the upstairs work is an average, and the descent is the sentence "fewer bad than total, so a good one exists." Erdős made that trade a policy, and the room it opens is the biggest one in the book — every outcome at once. The second door is P20: the polynomial lift, §5's deferred promise cashed. An indeterminate standing next to the numbers is a new coordinate — the cheapest room this page ever builds, built every time anyone writes \(x\) — and two polynomials that agree at enough points agree everywhere, because the room's own bookkeeping (a degree-\(d\) object has at most \(d\) roots) certifies the identity wholesale. The farthest is P27: Hamilton's couples return as the foundation of a whole subject, where the room is the complex plane and the descents — contour integrals that come home carrying integer facts — are the machinery itself. Example 2 was that subject's front door; P27 is the house.

The default habit

When a problem jams with the shape of a missing reason — a coincidence with no cause in the room, a solution the room forbids, an intersection that refuses to exist — ask whether the problem is a shadow. Then, in order: name the bigger room, one sentence, before doing any work upstairs; name the shadow map that connects the floors, and say what it forgets; prove the easy version where the new room is doing the work; and pay for the trip home in checks — what exactly does the descent claim, and what certifies it? If the descent cannot be paid, keep the upstairs truth as upstairs truth and say so out loud; and if you cannot name the room or the map, stop, because "think bigger" is a mood, not a lift. The audit is always the same three questions, and §5 priced them one exhibit apiece: what room (Exhibit B's floor had none), what map (Exhibit A's answered a question nobody asked), what descent (Exhibit C's never came home). A lift that can answer all three is a machine; one that cannot is furniture — and the difference, as ever, is the checks.

7 · Where it came from

The lift is as old as drawing, because projection is the oldest machine for making three dimensions fit on two. Girard Desargues — the Lyon architect of P17's history, points at infinity and all — proved his theorem in space and never printed it under his own covers; it reached print in 1648, appended to La manière universelle de pratiquer la perspective, a perspective manual by his Paris student the engraver Abraham Bosse: the theorem about shadows first published inside a book about drawing them, its three-dimensional proof standing where the draftsmen could see it. Germinal Dandelin in 1822 dropped spheres into the cone — each one tangent to the cone and to the cutting plane, touching the plane exactly at a focus — and the focal property of the ellipse, a stubborn fact of the flat section, became obvious in the room the cone supplies; Adolphe Quetelet shares the credit, and the spheres still head the geometry course. Joseph Liouville, working through the 1830s and publishing in 1835, proved that some antiderivatives are nonelementary — \(e^{-x^2}\) the canonical case: this page's most famous integral was first proved impossible by every means the school room owns, which is why the two-dimensional lift is not a convenience but the only door. William Rowan Hamilton in 1837 built the room Example 2 moved into: his Theory of Conjugate Functions, or Algebraic Couples (Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy 17, 293–422) constructed the complex numbers as ordered pairs of reals, added componentwise and multiplied by \((a,b)(c,d) = (ac - bd,\; ad + bc)\) — no "imaginary" quantity anywhere, a bigger room built out of two copies of the old one. The Gaussian integral's own history is a chain of shadows: Abraham de Moivre met the constant \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) in 1733 in his approximation to the binomial; James Stirling pinned the same scale; Pierre-Simon Laplace extracted the integral's value in the 1770s; Carl Friedrich Gauss's 1809 Theoria motus credited Laplace for it; and the polar trick of Example 3 is usually credited to Siméon-Denis Poisson — the integral's traditional name, the Euler–Poisson integral, records the room it is evaluated in. The boundary has its own century. David Hilbert, in lectures of 1898–99, built the first plane in which Desargues fails — proof that this page's §2 theorem is not a consequence of the plane's incidence facts alone; Leonard Dickson in 1905 constructed the first finite nearfield, the twisted arithmetic of Exhibit B; Oswald Veblen and Joseph Wedderburn used one in 1907 to build the first finite non-Desarguesian planes, nine points each (Transactions of the American Mathematical Society 8, 379–388); and the Veblen–Young embedding theorem — a projective plane sits inside three-space exactly when Desargues holds in it — closed the circle this page keeps quoting: the plane's own confession that the theorem is about the room, not the plane.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 (●○○). (a) Take the triangle \(A = (1,0)\), \(B = (0,1)\), \(C = (2,1)\) — §2's triangle with the corner \(C\) moved from \((2,3)\) to \((2,1)\) — and apply §2's own scalars: \(A' = 4A\), \(B' = 2B\), \(C' = -2C\). Check the two triangles are in perspective from the origin, compute the three meets of corresponding sides, and find the axis. Which of §2's \(P\), \(Q\), \(R\) did not move, and why? (b) Run §4's construction on the A-pair of this new configuration: with \(S = (0,0,5)\) and \(O_1 = (0,0,2)\), walk \(A\) halfway up its ray from \(S\) to get \(A_1\), build \(A_2\) as the meet of the line \(O_1A_1\) with the ray from \(S\) through \(A'\), and check that the projection from \(S\) through \(A_2\) lands exactly on \(A'\).

Hint

(a) Three two-line solves, all small integers. For the "why", ask which of the three pairs \((A,A')\), \((B,B')\), \((C,C')\) the move of \(C\) left untouched — and which of the three meets is built without \(C\). (b) Everything happens inside the plane through \(S\), \(O\), and \(A\); parametrize both lines there, solve, and then push the ray from \(S\) through your \(A_2\) until \(z = 0\).

Worked

(a) \(A' = (4,0)\), \(B' = (0,2)\), \(C' = (-4,-2)\); the three vertex-joins still pass through the origin — \(AA'\) is the \(x\)-axis, \(BB'\) the \(y\)-axis, \(CC'\) is \(y = \frac{1}{2}x\) — so the triangles are in perspective from \(O\). The meets: \(AB\) is \(x + y = 1\), \(A'B'\) is \(x + 2y = 4\), so \(P = (-2,3)\); \(BC\) is \(y = 1\), \(B'C'\) is \(y = x + 2\), so \(Q = (-1,1)\); \(CA\) is \(y = x - 1\), \(C'A'\) is \(y = \frac{x - 4}{4}\), so \(R = (0,-1)\). All three lie on the axis \(2x + y + 1 = 0\): \(-4+3+1 = 0\), \(-2+1+1 = 0\), \(0-1+1 = 0\). \(P\) did not move — §2's \(P\) was \((-2,3)\) — because \(P\) is built from the A-pair and the B-pair alone, and the move of \(C\) left those four points untouched; \(Q\) and \(R\) each consume \(C\), so they moved, and the axis tilted from \(y = -3x - 3\) to \(2x + y + 1 = 0\) to meet them. (b) \(A_1\) is the midpoint of \(S\) and \(A\): \((\frac{1}{2}, 0, \frac{5}{2})\). The line \(O_1A_1\) is \((\frac{t}{2},\, 0,\, 2 + \frac{t}{2})\); the ray from \(S\) through \(A' = (4,0,0)\) is \((4u,\, 0,\, 5 - 5u)\). Meet: \(\frac{t}{2} = 4u\) and \(2 + \frac{t}{2} = 5 - 5u\) give \(u = \frac{1}{3}\), so \(A_2 = (\frac{4}{3}, 0, \frac{10}{3})\) — on both lines by substitution. Projection from \(S\): the ray \(S + v(A_2 - S) = (\frac{4v}{3},\, 0,\, 5 - \frac{5v}{3})\) hits \(z = 0\) at \(v = 3\), where \(x = 4\): exactly \(A' = (4,0)\), the landing the construction promised and §4 audited.

Exercise 2 (●●○). Let \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), one of the census's \(148\) empty-handed machines. (a) Hunt it over Hamilton's couples: show the vector \((1, -i)\) is a rail with stretch factor \(2 + i\), and name the partner rail and factor. (b) Check Vieta's two readings upstairs: the factors sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant. (c) Descend the honest way: read \(M\) as multiplication by the couple \((2,1)\) — a stretch by \(\sqrt{5}\) combined with a turn through the angle with \(\tan\theta = \frac{1}{2}\) — and say why the same lift cannot repair the shear \(\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\), in any room of numbers whatever.

Hint

(a) Feed \((1,-i)\) through the machine — first component \(2x - y\), second \(x + 2y\) — and check both components come out as \((2+i)\) times what went in. The partner comes from conjugation. (b) \((2+i) + (2-i)\) and \((2+i)(2-i)\), against \(\operatorname{tr} M\) and \(\det M\). (c) For the shear, compute the discriminant first: is the shear's problem missing numbers, or something else?

Worked

(a) \(M(1,-i) = (2 \cdot 1 - (-i),\; 1 + 2 \cdot (-i)) = (2+i,\; 1 - 2i)\); and \((2+i) \cdot 1 = 2+i\), \((2+i) \cdot (-i) = -2i - i^2 = 1 - 2i\): both components match, so \(M(1,-i) = (2+i)(1,-i)\) — a rail with factor \(2+i\). Conjugation swaps \(i\) for \(-i\) everywhere, so \((1, i)\) carries \(2 - i\): feed it, \(M(1,i) = (2-i,\; 1+2i)\) and \((2-i) \cdot i = 2i - i^2 = 1 + 2i\) ✓. (b) \((2+i) + (2-i) = 4 = \operatorname{tr} M\); \((2+i)(2-i) = 4 + 1 = 5 = \det M\). Both readings survive the trip upstairs, exactly as §4 promised. (c) As a couple map, \(M(x,y) = (2x - y,\; x + 2y)\) is multiplication by \(2 + i\): every arrow stretches by \(|2+i| = \sqrt{5}\) and turns by the angle with \(\tan\theta = 1/2\) — the scaled-turn verdict, \(\alpha = 2\), \(\beta = 1\), one real sentence with nothing complex in it. The shear is beyond every room of numbers: its discriminant is \(0\), the factor is doubled with no roots missing anywhere, and what it lacks is a second direction, which no supply of new numbers can manufacture. The lift buys missing numbers — the \(148\), repaired; it never buys missing directions — the \(56\), honestly broken in every room.

Exercise 3 (●●●). The integral family, one floor up. Take Example 3's value \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\) as given. (a) Run the two-dimensional trick on \(I_2 = \int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-x^2}\,dx\): compute the plane integral \(\iint x^2 e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA\) over the whole plane two ways — as a product of one-dimensional integrals, and in polar coordinates — and solve for \(I_2\). (b) Compute \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx\) from Example 3's value by a change of variable. (c) Compute \(\int_0^\infty x e^{-x^2}\,dx\), and say why this one never needed the lift.

Hint

(a) On the polar side \(x^2 = r^2\cos^2\theta\) and \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\), so the integral splits: \(\int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta\) times \(\int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-r^2}\,dr\). For the second, the substitution of Example 3's polar step, \(u = r^2\), leaves \(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty u e^{-u}\,du\) — and \(\int_0^\infty u e^{-u}\,du = 1\) by parts. (b) Let \(x = \sqrt{2}\,u\). (c) The same substitution, with nothing attached to it.

Worked

(a) Product side: \(\iint x^2 e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x^2 e^{-x^2}dx\right)\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2}dy\right) = 2I_2 \cdot \sqrt{\pi}\). Polar side: \(\left(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\right)\left(\int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-r^2}dr\right) = \pi \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\), since \(\int_0^\infty r^3 e^{-r^2}dr = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty u e^{-u}du = \frac{1}{2}\). Equate: \(2I_2\sqrt{\pi} = \frac{\pi}{2}\), so \(I_2 = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\) — one lift, one new constant. (b) \(x = \sqrt{2}u\) turns \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}dx\) into \(\sqrt{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-u^2}du = \sqrt{2}\cdot\sqrt{\pi} = \sqrt{2\pi}\): the normal curve's constant, one substitution away. (c) \(u = x^2\): \(\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^\infty e^{-u}du = \frac{1}{2}\), no lift needed. The reason is visible inside Example 3's polar step: the \(r\) in \(r\,dr\) was doing this very substitution all along — the one-dimensional integrand \(xe^{-x^2}\) is a radial integrand wearing the ring's own bookkeeping, so the bigger room's work was already done in the smaller one.

Exercise 4 (◆). Diagnose a proposed lift. The Dirichlet integral \(\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}\) is a famous value, and its integrand has no elementary antiderivative — Liouville again, §7. A student proposes: "Square it: \(\left(\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}dx\right)^2 = \iint_{[0,\infty)^2} \frac{\sin x\,\sin y}{xy}\,dx\,dy\). Now switch to polar coordinates, like Example 3, and compute the double integral." Diagnose. (a) Which step is honest, and which step is not even licensed to start? (b) Show the polar switch stalls anyway, with a witness at a single radius. (c) A repair that works is the damping machine: \(I(\varepsilon) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon x}\frac{\sin x}{x}dx\) for \(\varepsilon > 0\). Show that \(I'(\varepsilon) = -\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon^2}\), deduce \(I(\varepsilon) = \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan\varepsilon\), and read off the value. Then say, honestly, what the final descent \(\varepsilon \to 0\) costs — and why brute force cannot pay it.

Hint

(a) Example 3's polar switch was licensed by two facts: the integrand depended on \(r\) alone, and the absolute integral converged. Test both here; for the second, look at \(\int_0^X |\sin x/x|\,dx\) at \(X = 10\), \(40\), \(100\). (b) Fix \(r = 5\) and evaluate the integrand at two different angles. (c) For \(\varepsilon > 0\) the derivative passes under the integral sign, because the differentiated integrand \(e^{-\varepsilon x}\sin x\) is absolutely integrable; \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon x}\sin x\,dx\) is a two-integrations-by-parts exercise. For the last part: try summing \(e^{-0.001x}\sin x/x\) by machine out to \(x = 200\) and compare with the closed form.

Worked

(a) The squaring is honest as an iterated integral: slice the quadrant at fixed \(y\) and the inner integral converges for every \(y\), so the iterated value is the square. But the polar switch is not even licensed to start, on two counts. First, absolute convergence fails: \(\int_0^X \left|\frac{\sin x}{x}\right|dx\) grows like \(\frac{2}{\pi}\) times a logarithm — about \(2.56\) at \(X=10\), \(3.47\) at \(40\), \(4.05\) at \(100\) — so the double integral is only conditionally convergent, and rearranging conditionally convergent integrals can change the answer. Second, and independently: (b) the integrand is not radial anyway. At radius \(r = 5\), the slice at angle \(0.3\) (\(x \approx 4.78\), \(y \approx 1.48\)) gives \(\approx -0.141\); the slice at angle \(1.2\) (\(x \approx 1.81\), \(y \approx 4.66\)) gives \(\approx -0.115\). Same radius, different values: polar coordinates do no work here even where licensed. The student named a room, but the map does nothing in it — Exhibit A's first audit question. (c) For \(\varepsilon > 0\), differentiating under the integral is licensed (the differentiated integrand is absolutely integrable), and \(I'(\varepsilon) = -\int_0^\infty e^{-\varepsilon x}\sin x\,dx = -\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon^2}\) — the last integral by two integrations by parts. With \(I(\varepsilon) \to 0\) as \(\varepsilon \to \infty\), integrate back: \(I(\varepsilon) = \int_\varepsilon^\infty \frac{dt}{1+t^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan\varepsilon\). Check at \(\varepsilon = 1\): \(I(1) = \frac{\pi}{4}\), and the damped integral converges fast enough for a machine to confirm it to many digits; at \(\varepsilon = 2\), \(\frac{\pi}{2} - \arctan 2 \approx 0.4636\). The closed form now reads off the limit: \(I(\varepsilon) \to \frac{\pi}{2}\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0\). The cost, honestly: that last step moves a limit under an integral whose absolute convergence fails — a second descent, licensed in the grown-up world by Abel's theorem on damped limits, named here and not proved here. And brute force cannot pay it: at \(\varepsilon = 0.001\) the closed form says \(I \approx 1.5698\), but the damping is so weak that a machine sum out to \(x = 200\) — sixty-plus oscillations in — still lands about \(0.002\) short. The limit is visible only through the closed form, which is what closed forms are for. The diagnosis, one sentence: the student's lift named a room and forgot the map; the repair names a different room — the damped line, with differentiation in \(\varepsilon\) as the upstairs work — and its descent is a limit the closed form licenses, priced and not free.

Exercise 5 (◐). Close the census in both rooms. Take the \(625\) machines with entries in \(\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\). (a) Count the real census yourself, by discriminant sign: how many have \(D > 0\), \(D = 0\), \(D < 0\); and among the \(D = 0\) machines, how many are multiples of the identity table. (b) Over Hamilton's couples, count how many machines own a scheme — two distinct factors, or the every-direction ownership of a pure stretch — and say exactly which machines stay broken, and why no room of numbers can repair them. (c) Take \(M = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\), one of the \(148\): hunt it upstairs (factors, rails, Vieta's two readings), descend the honest way to the scaled-turn verdict with scale and angle, and write the one-sentence moral of Example 2's census — what the lift buys and what it never buys.

Hint

(a) Sweep all four entries over the five values and sort by the sign of \((a+d)^2 - 4(ad - bc)\); the counts are three numbers that sum to \(625\). (b) Over the couples every \(D \ne 0\) machine owns two distinct factors; add the pure stretches. For the residue, ask what a doubled factor lacks — numbers, or directions? (c) \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 8\); feed \((1,-i)\) the way Exercise 2 did; the scaled turn is \(\begin{bmatrix} \alpha & -\beta \\ \beta & \alpha \end{bmatrix}\).

Worked

(a) \(D > 0\): \(416\) two-rail machines. \(D = 0\): \(61\) — of which \(5\) are the multiples of the identity table \(\lambda I\) (\(\lambda \in \{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\), the zero machine included), owning every direction, and \(56\) shear-types owning exactly one. \(D < 0\): \(148\) empty-handed. \(416 + 61 + 148 = 625\). (b) Upstairs, every \(D \ne 0\) machine owns two distinct factors — a real pair when \(D > 0\), a conjugate pair when \(D < 0\) — and the five pure stretches still own every direction: \(416 + 148 + 5 = 569\) of the \(625\) own a scheme over the couples. The residue is exactly the \(56\) shear-types, and no room of numbers repairs them: their doubled factor is present in every room — nothing is missing to supply — and what they lack is a second direction, a fact about the machine itself. The census closes both ways: \(421\) diagonalizable over the reals (\(416 + 5\)), \(569\) over the couples, and the difference is exactly the \(148\) the lift bought. (c) \(\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 8\), so \(D = 16 - 32 = -16 < 0\): \(M\) is one of the \(148\). Factors \(2 \pm 2i\). Rails: \(M(1,-i) = (2 + 2i,\; 2 - 2i)\), and \((2+2i)(-i) = -2i - 2i^2 = 2 - 2i\) ✓, so \((1,-i)\) carries \(2+2i\); conjugation gives \((1,i)\) with \(2-2i\). Vieta: \((2+2i) + (2-2i) = 4 = \operatorname{tr} M\); \((2+2i)(2-2i) = 4 + 4 = 8 = \det M\). Downstairs the pair is one real sentence: \(M\) multiplies every couple by \(2 + 2i\), stretching by \(|2+2i| = 2\sqrt{2}\) while turning through \(45^\circ\) — the scaled-turn verdict, \(\alpha = 2\), \(\beta = 2\), nothing complex in it. The moral: the lift repairs the census precisely where the old room was too small to hold the answers — and leaves intact what is broken for reasons no room can fix.
