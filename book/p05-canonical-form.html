The Patterns Atlas · P05 · the fifth move of the spine

Reduce to canonical form

Every class of messy objects has one standard representative. Put your object in its uniform first — most questions answer themselves in uniform.

New word: canonical form. You have reduced things all your school life — fractions to lowest terms, surds to "simplified" form, remainders to the honest leftover — without anyone naming the profession these drills belong to. The name is canonical form: the one standard shape, fixed in advance, that every object of a class can be brought to. "Canonical" means appointed by the theory, not "nicest looking": the form is part of the class's definition, chosen once, and the same for everyone. This page is not about any one reduction you already own. It is about the move itself: when to reduce, what the reduction must guarantee, and what it may never be allowed to forget.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned claims, P02 built tables, P03 attacked them with hostile cases, and P04 paid for corners and read off what the new shape knew. Then two bridge pages taught the arithmetic of leftovers: B01 gave every division exactly one honest remainder, and B02 proved that same-remainder numbers are interchangeable for every leftover question.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P04's completed square was quietly more than a trick: (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 25 is a standard form — every circle has exactly one, and the form answered questions (center? radius?) the scattered equation could not. And B02's leftover is a standard form too: among all numbers of one residue team, the leftover in {0, …, n − 1} is the appointed representative. Both pages used the same unnamed move, each in one corner of mathematics. The move deserves its own page — and it is, by the evidence, one of the heaviest techniques in the entire registry (block 9 quotes the numbers).

2 · Meet the problem

Two problems, one continuous and one discrete. No names yet — just watch what solves them.

Problem one. Add the surds: √50 − √18 + √8. School training says "simplify each one first" — but watch what the simplification actually is. Each number under a root hides a square factor: 50 = 2 · 5², 18 = 2 · 3², 8 = 2 · 2². So √50 = 5√2, √18 = 3√2, √8 = 2√2 — all three are whole multiples of the same standard surd √2. The sum is now transparent: (5 − 3 + 2)√2 = 4√2. The check is exact and needs no decimals: squaring both sides, (√50 − √18 + √8)² = 50 + 18 + 8 − 2·30 + 2·20 − 2·12 = 32, and (4√2)² = 32 — integers only, agreement. The "simplification" was a reduction to a common standard form: each surd to its canonical shape a√d with d square-free, after which the arithmetic happened in canonical coordinates, coefficient by coefficient.

Problem two. Is 2026⁷⁷ − 2026 divisible by 7? The number 2026⁷⁷ has 255 digits (the lab confirms the count from the logarithm: 77 · log₁₀ 2026 ≈ 254.6). Nobody sensible multiplies it out. B01 computed the leftover for you: 2026 = 7 · 289 + 3, so 2026 is on team 3. By B02's working rule (reduce first), 2026⁷⁷ is on the team of 3⁷⁷. The powers of 3 walk the teams mod 7 in a cycle of length 6: 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, back to 3. Since 77 = 6 · 12 + 5, the 77th step lands where the 5th step lands: 3⁷⁷ ≡ 3⁵ ≡ 5 (mod 7). So 2026⁷⁷ − 2026 ≡ 5 − 3 = 2 (mod 7): not divisible — leftover 2. A 255-digit question, answered in single digits, and the answer is no, which no amount of confident multiplying would have made obvious.

Both problems were solved by the same shape of move: replace each object by the appointed representative of its class — surds by a√d, numbers by their leftover — do the work among the representatives, and read the answer off. Neither problem mentioned the move; the move is what the solutions had in common. It has a name, and it is the fifth pattern of the spine.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P05 — Reduce to canonical form. When the objects of a class carry an appointed standard representative — the canonical form — transform your object to it before doing anything else. The transformation must meet three demands: it is legal (defined for every object of the class, and reversible, or class-preserving, exactly when the answer must be translated back); it is class-preserving (two objects get the same canonical form precisely when they are equivalent for the question being asked); and it is unique (the form is well-defined — no arbitrary choices survive the reduction). Then: answer the question in canonical coordinates, where every object looks like every other of its class, and translate back if the answer is an object rather than a verdict.

Or, in words: don't argue with the object as you found it; put it in its uniform first — the one shape every equivalent object shares — and the question usually dissolves. But check the uniform's contract: it must exist for every object (legality), fit the class the question actually cares about (same uniform ⇔ same class), and be forced, not chosen (uniqueness). A uniform that fails any of the three is a disguise, not a reduction.

Box P05.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. Legality. The reduction must be defined on the whole class, and when the answer must return to the original world it must run both ways. Squaring both sides of an equation fails this demand as a "reduction" — it is defined everywhere but not reversible, and P01's ghost roots (x = −1 walking into √(x + 2) = x) are the standard wreckage: a transform that merges classes can create impostors that translate back as false answers. The cure is P01's Box P01.C: carry the side condition, or check candidates back.
  2. Class-preservation. Same canonical form ⇔ same class for this question. The leftover mod 7 treats 2026 and 3 as the same object — which is exactly right for divisibility-by-7 questions and exactly wrong for "which number is bigger". A reduction that preserves more equivalence than the question licenses destroys information (see block 5); one that preserves less never gets off the ground.
  3. Uniqueness. The form must be the form. √(7 + 4√3) has two algebraic square roots, 2 + √3 and −2 − √3; the radical sign's convention — √ means the non-negative root — is what makes "the" canonical value a function at all. Conventions that pin choices are not bureaucracy; they are demand three being enforced. When a reduction seems to give "two canonical forms", P01's discipline applies: a pinning is missing, and finding it is the work.

Three-key diagnosis. The claim type: an identification, comparison, divisibility, or extremum question about an object from a class with a known standard form. The problem shape: a messy object whose mess is irrelevant to the question — different-looking objects that must be compared, or one object that must be interrogated. The tools: the class's reduction, plus its license. The catalogue grows as the book climbs — matrices, quadratic forms, and whole problems get their canonical forms at B05–B08 and P16.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. Each names every pattern used.

Example 1 (numbers) — the surd system, licensed

The encounter's Problem One, now with the license on the table. Every whole number n ≥ 1 factors as n = a²d with d square-free (strip the square part out of the prime factorization; the lab verifies this reduction exists and is unique for every n up to 500, with zero exceptions and zero collisions). So √n = a√d is the canonical form of a surd, and "like terms" stops being a schoolteacher's intuition and becomes a theorem: two surds are like terms exactly when their canonical d matches. The arithmetic of the encounter — 5√2 − 3√2 + 2√2 = 4√2 — is then nothing but the arithmetic of the coefficients, 5 − 3 + 2, in front of the shared canonical surd: the same reason x-terms collect. And the honest boundary, named now: uniqueness of the reduction rests on unique prime factorization, which this book has not proved and will not smuggle — B03–B04 build the step it stands on (Euclid's lemma), and until then the lab's exhaustive check is a detector over 500 cases, while the proof is a named debt. Moves used: P05, with P02's detector-honesty on the uniqueness claim.

Example 2 (counting) — the mandated application: mod n, the leftover is the canonical form

The bridge pages taught the parts; this is the assembled move, and it is the page's mandated application. Fix a modulus n. The class: all whole numbers. The equivalence: B02's teams (same leftover on division by n). The canonical form: the leftover rem(a, n) in {0, 1, …, n − 1}.

Check the three demands of Box P05.A against the bridges. Legality: B01's division algorithm assigns every whole number exactly one legal leftover — the transform is defined everywhere. Class-preservation: B02 proved same leftover ⇔ same team, and B02's working rule (Box B02.A) licenses computing with representatives — reduce first, at every step. Uniqueness: B01's uniqueness proof (the size argument) is precisely the statement that the representative is forced, not chosen. All three demands are already theorems on standing pages — the canonical form mod n is fully licensed, which is why the book built the bridge before this page.

Now the encounter's Problem Two, formally. The question is a divisibility verdict — a team question, so class-preservation holds for exactly this question. Reduce first: 2026 ≡ 3 (mod 7). Compute in canonical coordinates: the powers of 3 cycle 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 with period 6, and 77 = 6 · 12 + 5, so 3⁷⁷ ≡ 3⁵ ≡ 5. Translate the verdict back: 2026⁷⁷ − 2026 ≡ 5 − 3 = 2 (mod 7) — not divisible, remainder 2. The lab runs a second, road-grinding route — reduce first at every one of 77 steps, r → rem(3r, 7) — and a third guard: the 255-digit count, from 77 · log₁₀ 2026 ≈ 254.6. All routes agree. Note what the canonical form did not do: it never claimed 2026⁷⁷ = 3⁷⁷ — that would be a category error (a size question!). It claimed exactly what the license covers: same team. Moves used: P05, on B01's uniqueness and B02's Box B02.A; the cycle-hunt is P02's small-case discipline.

Example 3 (geometry) — parabolas: one canonical form, three curves, one invariant

By Box P05.B, every parabola y = ax² + bx + c (a ≠ 0) has exactly one canonical form a(x − h)² + k, with h = −b⁄2a and k = c − b²⁄4a. Uniqueness is a two-line proof, complete: expanding a(x − h)² + k gives ax² − 2ah·x + (ah² + k), and since a ≠ 0, matching the x-coefficient forces h = −b⁄2a, after which the constant term forces k. (P04 paid the corner; this page cashes the uniqueness.)

Now classification. Two parabolas deserve to be called "the same up to sliding" when a translation of the plane carries one onto the other: shifting the graph of f(x) by t horizontally and s vertically produces g(x) = f(x − t) − s. Compute the canonical form of g from the canonical form of f: f(x − t) − s = a(x − t − h)² + k − s. Same a. So a is translation-invariant — the canonical form's leading coefficient is an invariant of the sliding class (P09 will make invariants a whole pattern; here one falls out of the canonical form for free).

Three parabolas, reduced: f₁: x² − 2x + 3 reduces to (x − 1)² + 2; f₂: 2x² − 4x + 4 reduces to 2(x − 1)² + 2; f₃: x² + 4x + 7 reduces to (x + 2)² + 3. Same a for f₁ and f₃, so a translation may connect them — and it does: t = 3, s = −1, since f₃(x − 3) − 1 = ((x − 3) + 2)² + 3 − 1 = (x − 1)² + 2 = f₁(x), an identity the lab verifies exactly over the grid x = −30…30. For f₂ the invariant blocks the door before any search begins: a = 2 ≠ 1, so no translation whatsoever carries f₂ onto f₁ — and the lab's small (t, s)-search agrees, finding zero matches where the algebra said none could exist. The algebra is the proof; the search is the detector. This is the canonical form's second gift beyond tidiness: classification — same form, same class; different invariant, different class, certified. (And for the calculus-reader: the vertex (h, k) that the canonical form displays is exactly the extremum your class-12 calculus hunt locates by f′(x) = 2a(x − h) = 0 — the canonical form is that answer, pre-computed.) Moves used: P05 on P04's corner; P03's near-miss discipline on the sign of h; P02's detector-honesty on the (t, s)-search.

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

The move is powerful and its failure modes are worth cataloguing more than most — because here the failures are mostly category errors: reductions used on questions they were never licensed for.

  • The reduction forgets — and the question needed what was forgotten. The leftover mod 7 treats 2026 and 3 as the same object. For "is 2026⁷⁷ − 2026 divisible by 7", that collapse is the whole method; for "is 2026⁷⁷ bigger than 2026⁶⁰?", it is sabotage — the canonical form burned the magnitude, and no translation back can restore it. Mitigation: read the question first (P01) and match the reduction to the question's equivalence. Price: every reduction forgets something; that is what makes it canonical. Default: leftovers for divisibility and team questions; exact arithmetic for magnitude and order questions; canonical vertex form for shape questions about a single curve.
  • The canonical form inherits the licenses of its equivalence — no more. B02's scope box showed division is not licensed mod n: 2 · 3 ≡ 2 · 8 (mod 10), yet cancelling the 2 to conclude 3 ≡ 8 is false. Reducing to canonical representatives and then dividing is the same category error wearing a uniform. The demand is Box P05.A's class-preservation, enforced on operations, not just on objects: a canonical form licenses exactly the operations its equivalence preserves. Which operations those are, mod n, is B03–B04's to settle.
  • "Simplest" is not a category — canonical is. P01's discipline applies with force here: "simplify √32" has no meaning until the class and its standard form are pinned. 4√2 (canonical: d = 2 square-free) and √32 are the same number; the canonical one is not shorter, and "shorter" was never the point — comparability was. Conversely √(7 + 4√3) looks irreducible but is exactly (2 + √3)² in disguise (Exercise 4 makes you reduce it) — and its reduction pins a sign, because −2 − √3 squares to the same thing: the radical's non-negative convention is demand three doing real work, the same sign wall P01's Box P01.C met in √(x + 2) = x.
  • Canonical forms can exist and still be out of reach. Two drawings of the same graph (dots and joining lines, laid out differently) have a canonical form: relabel to the lexicographically smallest encoding. It exists — demand three can be met in principle — but no known fast algorithm computes it, and for big graphs, deciding "same graph?" is a famous computational wall. Honest scope: a canonical form being well-defined does not make computing it easy. The move's price tag can be paid in computation, not just in forgotten information.
  • P04's deferred debt, paid to the extent this page can certify. P04 completed x² + 4xy + 5y² into a sum of two squares for positivity, but honestly declined to classify two-variable quadratics (ellipse? hyperbola?), deferring to this page. The full classification rotates the axes — change-of-basis machinery, owned by the coordinate bridges B05–B08, still ahead — and this page will not pretend otherwise. What P05 can certify now, completely, is the diagonal-free core, on the family x² + Bxy + y² = 1: for |B| < 2 the curve is bounded, and the proof needs no rotation: x² + Bxy + y² ≥ (1 − |B|⁄2)(x² + y²), because the difference is (|B|⁄2)(|x| − |y|)² ≥ 0 — a completed square in disguise (P04 again). At |B| = 2 the form collapses: (x + y)² = 1 or (x − y)² = 1 — two parallel lines, unbounded but not a hyperbola. For |B| > 2 the curve is unbounded, witnessed by an infinite machine-verified family of integer points: for B = 3, every solution (s, y) of the Pell-type equation s² − 5y² = 4 manufactures curve points x = (−3y + s)⁄2, and the family (3, 1), (7, 3), (18, 8), (47, 21), (123, 55), … never stops (the lab verifies both the recurrence step and every witness exactly; the "never stops" step is an induction — P66's formality, named and deferred exactly as B02 deferred it). The quantity B² − 4 predicts the class before any machine runs — a shadow of the discriminant P04 watched emerge; its full story is the bridges' rotation. The debt is hereby reduced from "classification" to "one honest deferral, named".
Scope note. This page owns the move — the three demands and the licensed catalogue of Box P05.B — not the hundreds of canonical forms mathematics has appointed. Each new form arrives with its class, its equivalence, and its license, on the page that needs it. The catalogue rows are receipts, not territory.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. Reduction is older than the word for it. Euclid's Book VII treats numbers by their common measure — the same division algorithm B01 proved — and lowest-terms fractions are its direct descendants. The name arrives with Gauss: his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) — the same book that gave B02 its triple bar — reduces binary quadratic forms, appointing finitely many "reduced forms" per class so that questions about infinitely many forms become questions about finitely many representatives (this page's Pell family is a distant echo of exactly that reduction theory). The linear-algebra descendants — Smith's normal form (1861) and Jordan's canonical form (1870) — are what the coordinate bridges B05–B08 will build toward. None of this history is load-bearing; it says the uniform you just learned to hand out has been standard issue for two centuries.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Reduce √147 + √75 − √27 to canonical form, and verify your answer by an exact check that uses no decimals at all.

Hint

Strip the square part from each: 147 = 3 · 7², 75 = 3 · 5², 27 = 3 · 3². All three share which canonical surd?

Worked answer

All three carry square-free part 3: √147 = 7√3, √75 = 5√3, √27 = 3√3, so the sum is (7 + 5 − 3)√3 = 9√3. Exact check by squaring (all integers): (√147 + √75 − √27)² = 147 + 75 + 27 + 2√(147 · 75) − 2√(147 · 27) − 2√(75 · 27) = 249 + 2·105 − 2·63 − 2·45 = 243, and (9√3)² = 81 · 3 = 243. Agreement, zero decimals. The lab runs this identity check exactly.

Exercise 2 ●●○

(a) Find every x in {0, 1, …, 12} with x² ≡ 3 (mod 13), and prove your list is complete. (b) Show x² ≡ 7 (mod 13) has no solution at all — by a method that proves impossibility, not exhaustion in the wrong sense.

Hint

By B02's working rule only the class of x matters — so squaring all thirteen classes once is a complete census of infinitely many integers, not a sample.

Worked answer

(a) Census: 0² → 0, 1 → 1, 2 → 4, 3 → 9, 4 → 16 ≡ 3, 5 → 25 ≡ 12, 6 → 36 ≡ 10, and squares are even in ±x, so 7 through 12 repeat 6 through 1. The square classes mod 13 are exactly {0, 1, 3, 4, 9, 10, 12}; x² ≡ 3 holds at x = 4 and x = 9 (checks: 16 = 13 + 3; 81 = 78 + 3). Completeness: every integer is on one of the thirteen teams, and the census visited each team once — this is the canonical form making "check everything" finite. (b) 7 is not among the seven square classes, and the census covers all classes — so no integer squares to 7 mod 13: a proved impossibility, not a failed search. (The lab re-runs the census independently and confirms both lists.)

Exercise 3 ●●●

Reduce the fraction 2026⁄4782 to lowest terms. Then prove the general uniqueness claim — a fraction has exactly one lowest-terms form p⁄q with q > 0 — or, if you cannot complete the proof, say exactly which lemma is missing and where this book will prove it.

Hint

For the reduction: the greatest common divisor. For uniqueness: write p⁄q = p′⁄q′ cross-multiplied, and watch yourself need the fact that if p divides a product and shares nothing with one factor, it must divide the other.

Worked answer

Reduction: gcd(2026, 4782) = 2 (the subtraction chain runs 4782 → 2·2026 + 730 → … → 2; the lab runs the full chain), so 2026⁄4782 = 1013⁄2391, and both parts resist further reduction — the lab trial-divides to confirm 1013 and 2391 = 3 · 797 have no small prime factors (2391's factorization 3 · 797 with 797 prime; 1013 prime). Uniqueness: suppose p⁄q = p′⁄q′, both lowest terms, q, q′ > 0. Cross-multiply: pq′ = p′q. Then p divides p′q. Since gcd(p, q) = 1, the missing step says p must divide p′; running it symmetrically, p′ divides p; with both positive, p = p′ and then q = q′. The missing step is Euclid's lemma (p | p′q and gcd(p, q) = 1 ⇒ p | p′), and this book has not proved it: it is exactly the theorem B04 builds via Bézout's identity, with B03 as the engine. Until then, the lab's uniqueness check (every equal pair of reduced fractions in a wide grid coincides — thousands of cases, zero exceptions) is an honest detector, and the debt is named, not hidden.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the reduction

Reduce √(7 + 4√3) to the form a + b√3 with a, b non-negative, prove your values are the only rational ones possible, and name the move from this page's shelf that licenses the whole procedure — including why the answer's sign is forced and not chosen. No hint: the diagnosis is the exercise.

Worked answer

Look for (a + b√3)² = a² + 3b² + 2ab√3 = 7 + 4√3: match parts, 2ab = 4 and a² + 3b² = 7. From b = 2⁄a substitute: a⁴ − 7a² + 12 = 0, which factors as (a² − 3)(a² − 4) = 0 (the lab verifies the quartic identity), so a = 2 (a = √3 is irrational and rejected — the form demands rational a, b), giving b = 1: √(7 + 4√3) = 2 + √3. The engine was P04 — 7 + 4√3 is a completed square in disguise, (2 + √3)² — and the framing move is P05: nested radicals reduce to the canonical two-term form a + b√3, which is what "simplify" meant all along. Uniqueness: the quartic has only a = 2 in non-negative rationals. And the sign: −2 − √3 also squares to 7 + 4√3, so the radical's convention — √ denotes the non-negative root — is what forces the answer 2 + √3; the canonical form pins the sign, exactly the sign wall of P01's Box P01.C, now doing service inside a reduction. The lab checks (2 + √3)² = 7 + 4√3 exactly, in integer pair arithmetic (u + v√3 multiplied as pairs): (2, 1)² = (4 + 3, 2·2·1) = (7, 4) ✓.

Exercise 5 ◐ The family x² + Bxy + y² = 1

Sweep B through −3, −2.5, …, 2.5, 3 and describe what the curve x² + Bxy + y² = 1 is, class by class. What you should find: three regimes — bounded (an ellipse-shaped oval) for |B| < 2, a collapse at |B| = 2 into two parallel lines (which lines?), and unbounded branches for |B| > 2. Find the single quantity in B that predicts the class before any machine runs; find the boundedness proof that needs no rotation (block 5 sketches it — make it yours); and say exactly what remains unproved about the full classification, and which bridge pages own it. The lab's Pell-family witnesses for B = 3 are the ground truth for the unbounded regime: (−3, 1), (−1, 3), (−8, 3), (−3, 8), (−21, 8), … — check them all on the curve by hand, exactly.
