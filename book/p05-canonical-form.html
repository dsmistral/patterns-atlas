The Patterns Atlas · P05 · the fifth move of the spine

Reduce to canonical form

Every class of messy objects has one standard representative. Put your object in its uniform first — most questions answer themselves in uniform.

New word: canonical form. You have reduced things all your school life — fractions to lowest terms, surds to "simplified" form, remainders to the honest leftover — without anyone naming the profession these drills belong to. The name is canonical form: the one standard shape, fixed in advance, that every object of a class can be brought to. "Canonical" means appointed by the theory, not "nicest looking": the form is part of the class's definition, chosen once, and the same for everyone. This page is not about any one reduction you already own. It is about the move itself: when to reduce, what the reduction must guarantee, and what it may never be allowed to forget.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned claims, P02 built tables, P03 attacked them with hostile cases, and P04 paid for corners and read off what the new shape knew. Then two bridge pages taught the arithmetic of leftovers: B01 gave every division exactly one honest remainder, and B02 proved that same-remainder numbers are interchangeable for every leftover question.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P04's completed square was quietly more than a trick: \((x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25\) is a standard form — every circle has exactly one, and the form answered questions (center? radius?) the scattered equation could not. And B02's leftover is a standard form too: among all numbers of one residue team, the leftover in \(\{0, \dots, n - 1\}\) is the appointed representative. Both pages used the same unnamed move, each in one corner of mathematics. The move deserves its own page.

2 · Meet the problem

Three problems — one continuous, one discrete, and one equality question that arrives in two costumes. No names yet — just watch what solves them.

Problem one. Add the surds: \(\sqrt{50} - \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{8}\). School training says "simplify each one first" — but watch what the simplification actually is. Each number under a root hides a square factor: \(50 = 2 \cdot 5^2\), \(18 = 2 \cdot 3^2\), \(8 = 2 \cdot 2^2\). So \(\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}\) — all three are whole multiples of the same standard surd \(\sqrt{2}\). The sum is now transparent: \((5 - 3 + 2)\sqrt{2} = \)\(4\sqrt{2}\). The check is exact and needs no decimals: squaring both sides, \((\sqrt{50} - \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{8})^2 = 50 + 18 + 8 - 2 \cdot 30 + 2 \cdot 20 - 2 \cdot 12 = 32\), and \((4\sqrt{2})^2 = 32\) — integers only, agreement. The "simplification" was a reduction to a common standard form: each surd to its canonical shape \(a\sqrt{d}\) with d square-free, after which the arithmetic happened in canonical coordinates, coefficient by coefficient.

Problem two. Is \(2026^{77} - 2026\) divisible by 7? The number \(2026^{77}\) has 255 digits. Nobody sensible multiplies it out. B01 computed the leftover for you: \(2026 = 7 \cdot 289 + 3\), so 2026 is on team 3. By B02's working rule (reduce first), \(2026^{77}\) is on the team of \(3^{77}\). The powers of 3 walk the teams mod 7 in a cycle of length 6: 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, back to 3. Since \(77 = 6 \cdot 12 + 5\), the 77th step lands where the 5th step lands: \(3^{77} \equiv 3^5 \equiv 5 \pmod{7}\). So \(2026^{77} - 2026 \equiv 5 - 3 = 2 \pmod{7}\): not divisible — leftover 2. A 255-digit question, answered in single digits, and the answer is no, which no amount of confident multiplying would have made obvious.

Problem three. A different breed of question: not "simplify" and not "divisible?" — but equal? Set \(A = \sqrt{17 - 12\sqrt{2}}\) and \(B = \frac{5 - 3\sqrt{2}}{3 + \sqrt{2}}\). Are they the same number? Decimals are worse than no help here. On a three-digit \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\), \(A \approx \sqrt{0.032} \approx 0.179\) while \(B \approx \frac{0.758}{4.414} \approx 0.172\): close, and the gap of \(0.007\) is pure noise — the subtraction \(17 - 12\sqrt{2}\) cancels almost every digit you brought, and the rounding damage is magnified by a factor of about \(\frac{12}{2\sqrt{0.032}} \approx 33\) on its way through the square root. Worse, even fifty matching digits would prove nothing: no approximation ever settled an equality. The reduction settles it, because every number built from rationals and \(\sqrt{2}\) by addition, subtraction, multiplication and division can be written in one standard shape, \(p + q\sqrt{2}\). For \(B\), clear the denominator by the conjugate \(3 - \sqrt{2}\): \(B = \frac{(5 - 3\sqrt{2})(3 - \sqrt{2})}{3^2 - 2} = \frac{21 - 14\sqrt{2}}{7} = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}\). For \(A\), hunt a completed square under the root: \(17 - 12\sqrt{2} = (3 - 2\sqrt{2})^2\) (check: \(9 + 8 - 12\sqrt{2}\)), and stripping the root is legal because \(3 - 2\sqrt{2} > 0\) — indeed \(3 > 2\sqrt{2}\), since squaring both sides gives \(9 > 8\) — so \(A = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}\). Same shape, same number: \(A = B\), exactly, and the comparison is finished the moment both numbers stand in uniform. (The true value is \(3 - 2\sqrt{2} \approx 0.1716\); the three-digit gap was rounding damage all the way down.)

All three problems were solved by the same shape of move: replace each object by the appointed representative of its class — surds by \(a\sqrt{d}\), numbers by their leftover, everything built on \(\sqrt{d}\) by its address \(p + q\sqrt{d}\) — do the work among the representatives, and read the answer off. None of the problems mentioned the move; the move is what the solutions had in common. It has a name, and it is the fifth pattern of the spine.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P05 — Reduce to canonical form. When the objects of a class carry an appointed standard representative — the canonical form — transform your object to it before doing anything else. The transformation must meet three demands: it is legal (defined for every object of the class, and reversible, or class-preserving, exactly when the answer must be translated back); it is class-preserving (two objects get the same canonical form precisely when they are equivalent for the question being asked); and it is unique (the form is well-defined — no arbitrary choices survive the reduction). Then: answer the question in canonical coordinates, where every object looks like every other of its class, and translate back if the answer is an object rather than a verdict.

Or, in words: don't argue with the object as you found it; put it in its uniform first — the one shape every equivalent object shares — and the question usually dissolves. But check the uniform's contract: it must exist for every object (legality), fit the class the question actually cares about (same uniform ⇔ same class), and be forced, not chosen (uniqueness). A uniform that fails any of the three is a disguise, not a reduction.

Box P05.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. Legality. The reduction must be defined on the whole class, and when the answer must return to the original world it must run both ways. Squaring both sides of an equation fails this demand as a "reduction" — it is defined everywhere but not reversible, and P01's ghost roots (\(x = -1\) walking into \(\sqrt{x + 2} = x\)) are the standard wreckage: a transform that merges classes can create impostors that translate back as false answers. The cure is P01's Box P01.C: carry the side condition, or check candidates back.
  2. Class-preservation. Same canonical form ⇔ same class for this question. The leftover mod 7 treats 2026 and 3 as the same object — which is exactly right for divisibility-by-7 questions and exactly wrong for "which number is bigger". A reduction that preserves more equivalence than the question licenses destroys information (see block 5); one that preserves less never gets off the ground.
  3. Uniqueness. The form must be the form. \(\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}\) has two algebraic square roots, \(2 + \sqrt{3}\) and \(-2 - \sqrt{3}\); the radical sign's convention — √ means the non-negative root — is what makes "the" canonical value a function at all. Conventions that pin choices are not bureaucracy; they are demand three being enforced. When a reduction seems to give "two canonical forms", P01's discipline applies: a pinning is missing, and finding it is the work.

Box P05.B — the working catalogue, and when to reach for the move. Six canonical forms this book has already licensed, one per row:

Three-key diagnosis. The claim type: an identification, comparison, divisibility, or extremum question about an object from a class with a known standard form. The problem shape: a messy object whose mess is irrelevant to the question — different-looking objects that must be compared, or one object that must be interrogated. The tools: the class's reduction, plus its license. The catalogue grows as the book climbs — matrices, quadratic forms, and whole problems get their canonical forms at B05–B08 and P16.

4 · The move at work

Five worked examples: one per catalogue domain, then two pressing the move further afield — into a coupled system, then into calculus. Each names every pattern used.

Example 1 (numbers) — the surd system, licensed

The encounter's Problem One, now with the license on the table. Every whole number \(n \ge 1\) factors as \(n = a^2 d\) with d square-free (strip the square part out of the prime factorization). So \(\sqrt{n} = a\sqrt{d}\) is the canonical form of a surd, and "like terms" stops being a schoolteacher's intuition and becomes a theorem: two surds are like terms exactly when their canonical d matches. The arithmetic of the encounter — \(5\sqrt{2} - 3\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} = 4\sqrt{2}\) — is then nothing but the arithmetic of the coefficients, \(5 - 3 + 2\), in front of the shared canonical surd: the same reason x-terms collect. And the honest boundary, named now: uniqueness of the reduction rests on unique prime factorization, which this book has not proved and will not smuggle — B03–B04 build the step it stands on (Euclid's lemma), and until then the exhaustive check is a detector over 500 cases, while the proof is a named debt. Moves used: P05, with P02's detector-honesty on the uniqueness claim.

Example 2 (counting) — the mandated application: mod n, the leftover is the canonical form

The bridge pages taught the parts; this is the assembled move, and it is the page's mandated application. Fix a modulus n. The class: all whole numbers. The equivalence: B02's teams (same leftover on division by n). The canonical form: the leftover rem(a, n) in {0, 1, …, n − 1}.

Check the three demands of Box P05.A against the bridges. Legality: B01's division algorithm assigns every whole number exactly one legal leftover — the transform is defined everywhere. Class-preservation: B02 proved same leftover ⇔ same team, and B02's working rule (Box B02.A) licenses computing with representatives — reduce first, at every step. Uniqueness: B01's uniqueness proof (the size argument) is precisely the statement that the representative is forced, not chosen. All three demands are already theorems on standing pages — the canonical form mod n is fully licensed, which is why the book built the bridge before this page.

Now the encounter's Problem Two, formally. The question is a divisibility verdict — a team question, so class-preservation holds for exactly this question. Reduce first: \(2026 \equiv 3 \pmod{7}\). Compute in canonical coordinates: the powers of 3 cycle 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 with period 6, and \(77 = 6 \cdot 12 + 5\), so \(3^{77} \equiv 3^5 \equiv 5\). Translate the verdict back: \(2026^{77} - 2026 \equiv 5 - 3 = 2 \pmod{7}\) — not divisible, remainder 2. Note what the canonical form did not do: it never claimed \(2026^{77} = 3^{77}\) — that would be a category error (a size question!). It claimed exactly what the license covers: same team. Moves used: P05, on B01's uniqueness and B02's Box B02.A; the cycle-hunt is P02's small-case discipline.

The same move, one stage deeper. Find the leftover when \(N = 41^{22} \cdot 28^{11} - 99^{11} + 2026\) is divided by 13. Stage one — reduce the bases: \(41 \equiv 2\), \(28 \equiv 2\), \(99 \equiv 8 = 2^3\), \(2026 \equiv 11 \pmod{13}\). The two giant terms collapse to the same address — \(41^{22} \cdot 28^{11} \equiv 2^{22} \cdot 2^{11} = 2^{33}\) and \(99^{11} \equiv (2^3)^{11} = 2^{33}\) — before any power is computed. Stage two — reduce the exponent: the powers of 2 walk the thirteen teams in a cycle \(2, 4, 8, 3, 6, 12, 11, 9, 5, 10, 7, 1\) of length 12, because \(2^{12} = 4096 = 315 \cdot 13 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\) — twelve single-digit multiplications, checked in full. So the exponent 33 itself reduces: \(33 = 2 \cdot 12 + 9\), and \(2^{33} \equiv 2^9 \equiv 5 \pmod{13}\). Both giants wear 5, they cancel, and \(N \equiv 5 - 5 + 11 = 11 \pmod{13}\): leftover 11. And a coincidence this pretty deserves confirmation that is not a coincidence: with \(X = 41^2 \cdot 28 = 47068\) and \(Y = 99\), the identity \(X^{11} - Y^{11} = (X - Y)(X^{10} + X^9Y + \dots + Y^{10})\) makes \(X - Y\) a divisor, and \(X - Y = 46969 = 13 \cdot 3613\) — the divisibility route confirms the cancellation without a single cycle. Note the two stages' shape: stage one reduces objects, stage two reduces an exponent's schedule — a number mod 13, then an exponent mod the cycle length 12: two canonical forms cooperating in one question. Moves used: P05 twice — bases, then exponent — with P02's cycle-hunt; the difference-of-powers identity is P06's ground, previewed.

Example 3 (geometry) — parabolas: one canonical form, three curves, one invariant

By Box P05.B, every parabola \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) (\(a \ne 0\)) has exactly one canonical form \(a(x - h)^2 + k\), with \(h = -b/2a\) and \(k = c - b^2/4a\). Uniqueness is a two-line proof, complete: expanding \(a(x - h)^2 + k\) gives \(ax^2 - 2ah \cdot x + (ah^2 + k)\), and since \(a \ne 0\), matching the x-coefficient forces \(h = -b/2a\), after which the constant term forces k. (P04 paid the corner; this page cashes the uniqueness.)

Now classification. Two parabolas deserve to be called "the same up to sliding" when a translation of the plane carries one onto the other: shifting the graph of f(x) by t horizontally and s vertically produces \(g(x) = f(x - t) - s\). Compute the canonical form of g from the canonical form of f: \(f(x - t) - s = a(x - t - h)^2 + k - s\). Same a. So a is translation-invariant — the canonical form's leading coefficient is an invariant of the sliding class (P09 will make invariants a whole pattern; here one falls out of the canonical form for free).

Three parabolas, reduced: \(f_1\): \(x^2 - 2x + 3\) reduces to \((x - 1)^2 + 2\); \(f_2\): \(2x^2 - 4x + 4\) reduces to \(2(x - 1)^2 + 2\); \(f_3\): \(x^2 + 4x + 7\) reduces to \((x + 2)^2 + 3\). Same a for \(f_1\) and \(f_3\), so a translation may connect them — and it does: \(t = 3\), \(s = -1\), since \(f_3(x - 3) - 1 = ((x - 3) + 2)^2 + 3 - 1 = (x - 1)^2 + 2 = f_1(x)\). For \(f_2\) the invariant blocks the door before any search begins: \(a = 2 \ne 1\), so no translation whatsoever carries \(f_2\) onto \(f_1\) — and a small (t, s)-search agrees, finding zero matches where the algebra said none could exist. The algebra is the proof; the search is the detector. This is the canonical form's second gift beyond tidiness: classification — same form, same class; different invariant, different class, certified. (And for the calculus-reader: the vertex (h, k) that the canonical form displays is exactly the extremum your class-12 calculus hunt locates by \(f'(x) = 2a(x - h) = 0\) — the canonical form is that answer, pre-computed.) Moves used: P05 on P04's corner; P03's near-miss discipline on the sign of h; P02's detector-honesty on the (t, s)-search.

Example 4 (algebra) — the coupled quadratic: P04's completion, run with a parameter

P04 completed one square at a time and, when it met the two-variable form \(x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2\), paid for positivity but declined the classification. Here is the payment it deferred, on a fresh specimen: \(P(x, y) = 2x^2 + 2xy + y^2 - 6x - 4y + 5\). What is its smallest value, and where?

The one-variable habit stalls first, honestly. Group the \(x\)-terms and the \(y\)-terms and complete each square separately: \(P = 2\bigl(x - \tfrac{3}{2}\bigr)^2 + (y - 2)^2 + 2xy - \tfrac{7}{2}\). Two squares, but a stranded \(2xy\) — not a constant, and at the would-be minimizer \(\bigl(\tfrac{3}{2}, 2\bigr)\) it contributes \(+6\), not 0. The completion did not fail; it was aimed at the wrong coupling. The cross-term ties \(x\) to \(y\), so the squares must share the tie: complete one square first, treating the second variable as a frozen parameter — P04's move, run with \(x\) held still.

Since \(y^2\) carries coefficient 1 (no fractions), read \(P\) as a quadratic in \(y\): \(P = y^2 + 2(x - 2)y + (2x^2 - 6x + 5)\). Complete the square in \(y\): \(y^2 + 2(x - 2)y = \bigl(y + (x - 2)\bigr)^2 - (x - 2)^2\), so \(P = (x + y - 2)^2 - (x^2 - 4x + 4) + 2x^2 - 6x + 5 = (x + y - 2)^2 + (x^2 - 2x + 1) = (x + y - 2)^2 + (x - 1)^2\). Every \(y\) has left the second square — the hierarchy worked: the first completion absorbed the coupling, and the second found a perfect square waiting. This is the canonical form for positivity questions in two variables — a sum of independent squares — and now the questions answer themselves. Both squares are non-negative, so \(P \ge 0\) everywhere; \(P = 0\) forces \(x = 1\) and then \(y = 1\): minimum 0, at the single point \((1, 1)\), and the level curve \(P = 0\) is that one degenerate point, not a curve at all.

Two independent confirmations. Expand the canonical form back: \((x + y - 2)^2 + (x - 1)^2 = (x^2 + y^2 + 4 + 2xy - 4x - 4y) + (x^2 - 2x + 1) = 2x^2 + 2xy + y^2 - 6x - 4y + 5\), term for term the original. And the calculus route your class-12 training owns: at a minimum both partial derivatives vanish, \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial x} = 4x + 2y - 6 = 0\) and \(\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = 2x + 2y - 4 = 0\); subtracting gives \(2x - 2 = 0\), so \(x = 1\), \(y = 1\) — the same point, and \(P(1, 1) = 0\). The honest boundary stands as block 5 states it: which pairs of squares are equivalent up to a rotation of the axes is the coordinate bridges' question (B05–B08), not this page's — here we only needed the squares. Moves used: P05 on P04's corner, run hierarchically; the stall-then-aim-again is P03's discipline; the expansion and derivative checks are P02's detector honesty.

Example 5 (calculus) — rational functions: the canonical form the catalogue lacked

Box P05.B's newest row earns its place. Take \(f(x) = \frac{x^3 - x^2 - 5x + 9}{x^2 - x - 2}\) (defined for \(x \ne -1, 2\)) and ask the two questions a calculus course asks: what is \(f'''(3)\), and what is \(\int_3^5 f(x)\,dx\)? On the raw quotient both are punishment — the quotient rule, applied three times, squares the denominator at every step and ends in degree-8 rubble. The canonical form dissolves the punishment.

Reduce first. The numerator is top-heavy — its first two terms are \(x(x^2 - x)\) — so divide: \(x^3 - x^2 - 5x + 9 = x(x^2 - x - 2) + (-3x + 9)\), giving \(f(x) = x + \frac{-3x + 9}{x^2 - x - 2}\). Factor the denominator: \(x^2 - x - 2 = (x - 2)(x + 1)\), distinct linear factors, so the proper part splits as \(\frac{A}{x - 2} + \frac{B}{x + 1}\). Clear denominators and match: \(-3x + 9 = A(x + 1) + B(x - 2)\); at \(x = 2\), \(3 = 3A\) so \(A = 1\); at \(x = -1\), \(12 = -3B\) so \(B = -4\). The canonical form, a polynomial plus one term per root: \[ f(x) = x + \frac{1}{x - 2} - \frac{4}{x + 1}. \] Existence and uniqueness are complete class-12 algebra — polynomial division plus coefficient matching, no debts — which is why the row earns its license on sight.

Now the calculus is one line per term. Differentiate \(x + (x - 2)^{-1} - 4(x + 1)^{-1}\) three times: the linear term dies at the second derivative, and \(f'''(x) = -6(x - 2)^{-4} + 24(x + 1)^{-4}\), so \(f'''(3) = -6 + \frac{24}{4^4} = -6 + \frac{3}{32} = \)\(-\frac{189}{32}\). Integrate term by term over \([3, 5]\) (both poles, \(-1\) and \(2\), safely outside): \(\int_3^5 f = \bigl[\frac{x^2}{2} + \ln(x - 2) - 4\ln(x + 1)\bigr]_3^5 = 8 + \ln 3 - 4\ln\frac{6}{4} = \)\(8 + 4\ln 2 - 3\ln 3\). And confirm the reduction itself, twice: put the canonical form back over the common denominator — \(x(x^2 - x - 2) + (x + 1) - 4(x - 2) = x^3 - x^2 - 5x + 9\) — and the original fraction returns term for term; and at \(x = 3\) both forms agree on value and slope, \(f(3) = 3\) and \(f'(3) = \frac{1}{4}\) (the quotient rule on the raw form, single terms on the canonical one). The canonical form's gift here is uncoupling: the growth at infinity (\(y \approx x\)) and the two poles (\(x = 2\) with weight 1, \(x = -1\) with weight \(-4\)) lie in separate terms, readable at sight — the quotient rule's rubble kept all three tangled. Moves used: P05, with P02's detector honesty on the two confirmations; the division-and-split is class-12 algebra, licensed by Box P05.B's newest row.

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

The move is powerful and its failure modes are worth cataloguing more than most — because here the failures are mostly category errors: reductions used on questions they were never licensed for.

  • The reduction forgets — and the question needed what was forgotten. The leftover mod 7 treats 2026 and 3 as the same object. For "is \(2026^{77} - 2026\) divisible by 7", that collapse is the whole method; for "is \(2026^{77}\) bigger than \(2026^{60}\)?", it is sabotage — the canonical form burned the magnitude, and no translation back can restore it. Mitigation: read the question first (P01) and match the reduction to the question's equivalence. Price: every reduction forgets something; that is what makes it canonical. Default: leftovers for divisibility and team questions; exact arithmetic for magnitude and order questions; canonical vertex form for shape questions about a single curve.
  • The canonical form inherits the licenses of its equivalence — no more. B02's scope box showed division is not licensed mod n: \(2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 8 \pmod{10}\), yet cancelling the 2 to conclude \(3 \equiv 8\) is false. Reducing to canonical representatives and then dividing is the same category error wearing a uniform. The demand is Box P05.A's class-preservation, enforced on operations, not just on objects: a canonical form licenses exactly the operations its equivalence preserves. Which operations those are, mod n, is B03–B04's to settle.
  • "Simplest" is not a category — canonical is. P01's discipline applies with force here: "simplify \(\sqrt{32}\)" has no meaning until the class and its standard form are pinned. \(4\sqrt{2}\) (canonical: d = 2 square-free) and \(\sqrt{32}\) are the same number; the canonical one is not shorter, and "shorter" was never the point — comparability was. Conversely \(\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}\) looks irreducible but is exactly \((2 + \sqrt{3})^2\) in disguise (Exercise 4 makes you reduce it) — and its reduction pins a sign, because \(-2 - \sqrt{3}\) squares to the same thing: the radical's non-negative convention is demand three doing real work, the same sign wall P01's Box P01.C met in \(\sqrt{x + 2} = x\).
  • Expansion is a legal rewrite that can destroy the question's structure. Solve \((x^2 - 2x - 1)^2 - 4 = 0\). Expanded to standard polynomial form — the canonical form for coefficient questions — the left side is \(x^4 - 4x^3 + 2x^2 + 4x - 3\): a quartic with no visible roots. Kept in its composed shape it is \(U^2 - 2^2\) with \(U = x^2 - 2x - 1\): \((U - 2)(U + 2) = (x^2 - 2x - 3)(x^2 - 2x + 1) = (x - 3)(x + 1)(x - 1)^2\), and the roots \(3, -1, 1\) fall out with no guessing. The lesson is not "expansion is bad"; it is that the canonical form is chosen by the question — bullet three's point, sharpened: for root-finding the factored form is canonical, for extrema the vertex form, for coefficient matching the expansion. Reducing to the wrong form is a legal rewrite that costs you the answer. Mitigation: name the question, then the form. Price: keeping structure means keeping options open — the "simplified" expansion is one form among three. Default: factor questions keep factors, extremum questions complete squares, identity questions expand.
  • The wrong convention can hide a reachable answer. Find the leftover of \(16^{55}\) on division by 17. In the standard convention — representatives in \(\{0, 1, \dots, 16\}\) — the base 16 is already canonical, and useless: the powers must be ground out. The centered convention appoints representatives in \(\{-8, -7, \dots, 0, \dots, 7, 8\}\) instead, and 16's address there is \(-1\) — unique, demand three holding in every convention. Then \(16^{55} \equiv (-1)^{55} = -1 \equiv 16 \pmod{17}\), instantaneous, translated back to the standard leftover at the end. Conventions are interchangeable lenses pinned by demand three, not holy objects: the treaty appoints a representative set, and switching mid-problem — centered residues when the base sits near the modulus, vertex form for extrema, factored form for roots — is this same move wearing a different uniform. Mitigation: before grinding powers, look at the base's distance from 0 and from m. Price: every switch ends with a translation back to the convention the question asked in. Default: standard leftovers for reporting, centered for computing with bases near m.
  • Canonical forms can exist and still be out of reach. Two drawings of the same graph (dots and joining lines, laid out differently) have a canonical form: relabel to the lexicographically smallest encoding. It exists — demand three can be met in principle — but no known fast algorithm computes it, and for big graphs, deciding "same graph?" is a famous computational wall. Honest scope: a canonical form being well-defined does not make computing it easy. The move's price tag can be paid in computation, not just in forgotten information. The same wall stands inside this page's own shelf: reducing \(\sqrt{N}\) to \(a\sqrt{d}\) demands \(N\)'s factorization — demand three is met, the address \(a\sqrt{d}\) exists, but computing it means factoring N, and when N is a product of two large primes no known method is fast. That is the specific wall modern cryptography leans on — climbed in famous cases only by years of distributed grinding, never by a shortcut.
  • P04's deferred debt, paid to the extent this page can certify. P04 completed \(x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2\) into a sum of two squares for positivity, but honestly declined to classify two-variable quadratics (ellipse? hyperbola?), deferring to this page. The full classification rotates the axes — change-of-basis machinery, owned by the coordinate bridges B05–B08, still ahead — and this page will not pretend otherwise. What P05 can certify now, completely, is the diagonal-free core, on the family \(x^2 + Bxy + y^2 = 1\): for \(|B| < 2\) the curve is bounded, and the proof needs no rotation: \(x^2 + Bxy + y^2 \ge (1 - |B|/2)(x^2 + y^2)\), because the difference \(Bxy + (|B|/2)(x^2 + y^2)\) is never negative — exactly \((|B|/2)(|x| - |y|)^2\), a completed square in disguise (P04 again), when \(Bxy \le 0\), and the still larger \((|B|/2)(|x| + |y|)^2\) when \(Bxy \ge 0\). At \(|B| = 2\) the form collapses: \((x + y)^2 = 1\) or \((x - y)^2 = 1\) — two parallel lines, unbounded but not a hyperbola. For \(|B| > 2\) the curve is unbounded, witnessed by an infinite family of integer points: for \(B = 3\), every solution (s, y) of the Pell-type equation \(s^2 - 5y^2 = 4\) manufactures curve points \(x = (-3y + s)/2\), and the family (3, 1), (7, 3), (18, 8), (47, 21), (123, 55), … never stops (the "never stops" step is an induction — P66's formality, named and deferred exactly as B02 deferred it). The quantity \(B^2 - 4\) predicts the class before any machine runs — a shadow of the discriminant P04 watched emerge; its full story is the bridges' rotation. The debt is hereby reduced from "classification" to "one honest deferral, named".
Scope note. This page owns the move — the three demands and the licensed catalogue of Box P05.B — not the hundreds of canonical forms mathematics has appointed. Each new form arrives with its class, its equivalence, and its license, on the page that needs it.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. Reduction is older than the word for it. Euclid's Book VII treats numbers by their common measure — the same division algorithm B01 proved — and lowest-terms fractions are its direct descendants. The name arrives with Gauss: his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) — the same book that gave B02 its triple bar — reduces binary quadratic forms, appointing finitely many "reduced forms" per class so that questions about infinitely many forms become questions about finitely many representatives (this page's Pell family is a distant echo of exactly that reduction theory). The linear-algebra descendants — Smith's normal form (1861) and Jordan's canonical form (1870) — are what the coordinate bridges B05–B08 will build toward. None of this history is load-bearing; it says the uniform you just learned to hand out has been standard issue for two centuries.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercises have no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Reduce \(\sqrt{147} + \sqrt{75} - \sqrt{27}\) to canonical form, and verify your answer by an exact check that uses no decimals at all.

Hint

Strip the square part from each: \(147 = 3 \cdot 7^2\), \(75 = 3 \cdot 5^2\), \(27 = 3 \cdot 3^2\). All three share which canonical surd?

Worked answer

All three carry square-free part 3: \(\sqrt{147} = 7\sqrt{3}\), \(\sqrt{75} = 5\sqrt{3}\), \(\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}\), so the sum is \((7 + 5 - 3)\sqrt{3} = \)\(9\sqrt{3}\). Exact check by squaring (all integers): \((\sqrt{147} + \sqrt{75} - \sqrt{27})^2 = 147 + 75 + 27 + 2\sqrt{147 \cdot 75} - 2\sqrt{147 \cdot 27} - 2\sqrt{75 \cdot 27} = 249 + 2 \cdot 105 - 2 \cdot 63 - 2 \cdot 45 = 243\), and \((9\sqrt{3})^2 = 81 \cdot 3 = 243\). Agreement, zero decimals.

Exercise 2 ●●○

(a) Find every \(x\) in \(\{0, 1, \dots, 12\}\) with \(x^2 \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\), and prove your list is complete. (b) Show \(x^2 \equiv 7 \pmod{13}\) has no solution at all — by a method that proves impossibility, not exhaustion in the wrong sense.

Hint

By B02's working rule only the class of x matters — so squaring all thirteen classes once is a complete census of infinitely many integers, not a sample.

Worked answer

(a) Census: \(0^2 \to 0\), \(1 \to 1\), \(2 \to 4\), \(3 \to 9\), \(4 \to 16 \equiv 3\), \(5 \to 25 \equiv 12\), \(6 \to 36 \equiv 10\), and squares are even in \(\pm x\), so 7 through 12 repeat 6 through 1. The square classes mod 13 are exactly \(\{0, 1, 3, 4, 9, 10, 12\}\); \(x^2 \equiv 3\) holds at \(x = 4\) and \(x = 9\) (checks: \(16 = 13 + 3\); \(81 = 78 + 3\)). Completeness: every integer is on one of the thirteen teams, and the census visited each team once — this is the canonical form making "check everything" finite. (b) 7 is not among the seven square classes, and the census covers all classes — so no integer squares to 7 mod 13: a proved impossibility, not a failed search.

Exercise 3 ●●●

Reduce the fraction \(2026/4782\) to lowest terms. Then prove the general uniqueness claim — a fraction has exactly one lowest-terms form \(p/q\) with \(q > 0\) — or, if you cannot complete the proof, say exactly which lemma is missing and where this book will prove it.

Hint

For the reduction: the greatest common divisor. For uniqueness: write \(p/q = p'/q'\) cross-multiplied, and watch yourself need the fact that if p divides a product and shares nothing with one factor, it must divide the other.

Worked answer

Reduction: \(\gcd(2026, 4782) = 2\) (the subtraction chain runs \(4782 \to 2 \cdot 2026 + 730 \to \dots \to 2\)), so \(2026/4782 = \)\(1013/2391\), and both parts resist further reduction — 1013 and \(2391 = 3 \cdot 797\) have no small prime factors (2391's factorization \(3 \cdot 797\) with 797 prime; 1013 prime). Uniqueness: suppose \(p/q = p'/q'\), both lowest terms, \(q, q' > 0\). Cross-multiply: \(pq' = p'q\). Then \(p \mid p'q\). Since \(\gcd(p, q) = 1\), the missing step says p must divide \(p'\); running it symmetrically, \(p' \mid p\); with both positive, \(p = p'\) and then \(q = q'\). The missing step is Euclid's lemma (\(p \mid p'q\) and \(\gcd(p, q) = 1 \Rightarrow p \mid p'\)), and this book has not proved it: it is exactly the theorem B04 builds via Bézout's identity, with B03 as the engine. Until then, the uniqueness check (every equal pair of reduced fractions in a wide grid coincides — thousands of cases, zero exceptions) is an honest detector, and the debt is named, not hidden.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the reduction

Reduce \(\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}\) to the form \(a + b\sqrt{3}\) with a, b non-negative, prove your values are the only rational ones possible, and name the move from this page's shelf that licenses the whole procedure — including why the answer's sign is forced and not chosen. No hint: the diagnosis is the exercise.

Worked answer

Look for \((a + b\sqrt{3})^2 = a^2 + 3b^2 + 2ab\sqrt{3} = 7 + 4\sqrt{3}\): match parts, \(2ab = 4\) and \(a^2 + 3b^2 = 7\). From \(b = 2/a\) substitute: \(a^4 - 7a^2 + 12 = 0\), which factors as \((a^2 - 3)(a^2 - 4) = 0\), so \(a = 2\) (\(a = \sqrt{3}\) is irrational and rejected — the form demands rational a, b), giving \(b = 1\): \(\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}} = 2 + \sqrt{3}\). The engine was P04 — \(7 + 4\sqrt{3}\) is a completed square in disguise, \((2 + \sqrt{3})^2\) — and the framing move is P05: nested radicals reduce to the canonical two-term form \(a + b\sqrt{3}\), which is what "simplify" meant all along. Uniqueness: the quartic has only \(a = 2\) in non-negative rationals. And the sign: \(-2 - \sqrt{3}\) also squares to \(7 + 4\sqrt{3}\), so the radical's convention — √ denotes the non-negative root — is what forces the answer \(2 + \sqrt{3}\); the canonical form pins the sign, exactly the sign wall of P01's Box P01.C, now doing service inside a reduction. Check \((2 + \sqrt{3})^2 = 7 + 4\sqrt{3}\) exactly, in integer pair arithmetic (\(u + v\sqrt{3}\) multiplied as pairs): \((2, 1)^2 = (4 + 3, 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1) = (7, 4)\) ✓.

What you should find: three regimes — bounded (an ellipse-shaped oval) for \(|B| < 2\), a collapse at \(|B| = 2\) into two parallel lines (which lines?), and unbounded branches for \(|B| > 2\). Find the single quantity in B that predicts the class before any machine runs; find the boundedness proof that needs no rotation (block 5 sketches it — make it yours); and say exactly what remains unproved about the full classification, and which bridge pages own it. Exercise 6 ●●○

Two numbers arrive from different routes: \(X = \sqrt{7 - 4\sqrt{3}} + \frac{6}{2 - \sqrt{3}}\) and \(Y = \frac{29 + 19\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3} + 1}\). Reduce both to the canonical address \(p + q\sqrt{3}\) and decide whether \(X = Y\). No decimals.

Hint

Three reductions from this page's shelf: denest the radical (Exercise 4's sibling — watch the sign), clear a denominator with its conjugate, and add in coordinates.

Worked answer

Denest: \(7 - 4\sqrt{3} = (2 - \sqrt{3})^2\) (check: \(4 + 3 - 4\sqrt{3}\)), and \(2 - \sqrt{3} > 0\) since \(4 > 3\), so \(\sqrt{7 - 4\sqrt{3}} = 2 - \sqrt{3}\). Clear the denominator: \(\frac{6}{2 - \sqrt{3}} = \frac{6(2 + \sqrt{3})}{4 - 3} = 12 + 6\sqrt{3}\). So \(X = (2 - \sqrt{3}) + (12 + 6\sqrt{3}) = \)\(14 + 5\sqrt{3}\). For \(Y\), multiply by the conjugate \(\sqrt{3} - 1\): \(Y = \frac{(29 + 19\sqrt{3})(\sqrt{3} - 1)}{3 - 1} = \frac{28 + 10\sqrt{3}}{2} = \)\(14 + 5\sqrt{3}\). Same address, so \(X = Y\). Note which direction of Box P05.A did the work: both reductions are value-preserving, so equal addresses force equal numbers — soundness alone settles this verdict; the converse direction (different addresses force different numbers) is the uniqueness demand, leaning on \(\sqrt{3}\)'s irrationality exactly as Box P05.B's row records.

Exercise 7 ●●●

The number \(S = \sqrt{4 - \sqrt{7}} - \sqrt{4 + \sqrt{7}}\). (a) Show that the naive hope \(\sqrt{4 - \sqrt{7}} = p + q\sqrt{7}\) with rational p, q fails — no such rationals exist. (b) Repair the reduction and find \(S\) exactly, as a single square-free surd. (c) Confirm your answer independently by computing \(S^2\) and checking the sign of \(S\).

Hint

For (a): square and match parts, then show the two candidates for \(p^2\) are not squares of rationals — count a prime. For (b): the failure means the address lives one floor up; give the nested radical a denominator of 2 and look for a completed square involving \(\sqrt{2}\).

Worked answer

(a) Squaring gives \(p^2 + 7q^2 = 4\) and \(2pq = -1\), so \(q = -\frac{1}{2p}\) and \(4p^4 - 16p^2 + 7 = 0\). The quadratic in \(p^2\) gives \(p^2 = \frac{16 \pm 12}{8} \in \{\frac{7}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\}\). Neither is a rational square: if \(\frac{7}{2} = \frac{a^2}{b^2}\) then \(7b^2 = 2a^2\), and counting the prime 7 — once on the left, an even number of times on the right — is a contradiction; \(\frac{1}{2} = \frac{a^2}{b^2}\) forces \(b^2 = 2a^2\), and counting the prime 2 the same way kills it too. The address \(p + q\sqrt{7}\) does not exist: a \(\sqrt{2}\) is hiding inside the answer. (b) The repair: \(4 - \sqrt{7} = \frac{8 - 2\sqrt{7}}{2} = \frac{(\sqrt{7} - 1)^2}{2}\), and \(4 + \sqrt{7} = \frac{(\sqrt{7} + 1)^2}{2}\) — the doubling manufactures exactly the cross-terms the naive form lacked. So \(\sqrt{4 - \sqrt{7}} = \frac{\sqrt{7} - 1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{14} - \sqrt{2}}{2}\) and \(\sqrt{4 + \sqrt{7}} = \frac{\sqrt{7} + 1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{14} + \sqrt{2}}{2}\) (both numerators positive, since \(\sqrt{7} > 1\)), and \(S = \frac{(\sqrt{14} - \sqrt{2}) - (\sqrt{14} + \sqrt{2})}{2} = \)\(-\sqrt{2}\). (c) \(S^2 = (4 - \sqrt{7}) + (4 + \sqrt{7}) - 2\sqrt{(4 - \sqrt{7})(4 + \sqrt{7})} = 8 - 2\sqrt{9} = 2\), and \(S < 0\) because \(4 - \sqrt{7} < 4 + \sqrt{7}\) and the root is order-preserving. Square 2, sign negative: \(S = -\sqrt{2}\), confirmed. The failure in (a) was information, not defeat — the missing square was the diagnosis.

Exercise 8 ●●●

Let \(g(x) = \frac{2x^3 + x^2 - 2x + 1}{x^2 - 1}\) for \(x \ne \pm 1\). (a) Reduce \(g\) to its canonical form — polynomial plus partial fractions. (b) From the canonical form alone, write a closed formula for \(g^{(n)}(x)\) valid for every \(n \ge 2\). (c) Compute \(\sum_{k=2}^{99} \bigl(g(k) - 2k - 1\bigr)\) exactly, as one reduced fraction.

Hint

(a) runs Example 5's procedure. For (c): in canonical coordinates the summand is a two-term difference — write out the first three and last two terms and watch the middle cancel.

Worked answer

(a) Division: \(2x^3 + x^2 - 2x + 1 = (2x + 1)(x^2 - 1) + 2\), so \(g = 2x + 1 + \frac{2}{x^2 - 1}\); and \(\frac{2}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} = \frac{1}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 1}\) (the numerators differ by exactly the denominator's span), so \(g = \)\(2x + 1 + \frac{1}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 1}\). (b) The linear part is gone by the second derivative, and \(\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x - r)^{-1} = (-1)^n \, n! \, (x - r)^{-(n+1)}\), so \(g^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n \, n! \left(\frac{1}{(x - 1)^{n+1}} - \frac{1}{(x + 1)^{n+1}}\right)\) for every \(n \ge 2\) — two terms where the raw quotient rule would already be rubble at \(n = 3\). (c) In canonical coordinates \(g(k) - 2k - 1 = \frac{1}{k - 1} - \frac{1}{k + 1}\), so the sum is \((1 - \frac{1}{3}) + (\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4}) + (\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{5}) + \dots + (\frac{1}{97} - \frac{1}{99}) + (\frac{1}{98} - \frac{1}{100})\): every \(\frac{1}{m}\) with \(3 \le m \le 98\) appears once positive and once negative and dies; the survivors are \(1 + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{99} - \frac{1}{100} = \frac{3}{2} - \frac{199}{9900} = \)\(\frac{14651}{9900}\), already in lowest terms. The middle cancellation is P69's telescoping, reached through this page's coordinates — invisible on the raw quotient, the whole story in canonical ones.

Exercise 9 ◆ Diagnose the reduction, round two

A student is handed \(h(x) = \sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 9} + \frac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}\) and asked to solve \(h(x) = 6\). The submitted work: "\(\sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 9} = \sqrt{(x-3)^2} = x - 3\), and \(\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3\), so \(h(x) = (x-3) + (x+3) = 2x\). Then \(2x = 6\), so \(x = 3\)." The verdict is wrong twice over: the reported answer is no answer at all, and the true solution set is a whole ray. Find both breaks — name the Box P05.A demand each one violates — repair the reduction, and solve the equation exactly.

Worked answer

Break 1: \(\sqrt{(x-3)^2} = x - 3\) is false for \(x < 3\) — the square root of a square is the absolute value \(|x - 3|\), and dropping it changes values on an entire half-line. That violates demand one, legality: the rewrite is not a value-preserving one, it is a branch of one wearing the whole line's uniform. Break 2: cancelling \(\frac{x-3}{x-3}\) silently erased the domain — the original \(h\) is undefined at \(x = 3\), and a reduction may preserve values only where values exist. Also demand one, in its domain clause: the treaty covers "same domain" as part of same value. The repair: \(h(x) = |x - 3| + (x + 3)\) for \(x \ne 3\), which splits by the absolute value's own boundary: for \(x < 3\), \(|x-3| = 3 - x\), so \(h(x) = (3 - x) + (x + 3) = 6\) — the branch is the constant 6; for \(x > 3\), \(|x-3| = x - 3\), so \(h(x) = 2x\). Solve on each branch: \(h(x) = 6\) holds for every \(x < 3\) — the equation is an identity there, and the half-line is the solution; on \(x > 3\), \(2x = 6\) gives \(x = 3\), outside the branch; and \(x = 3\) is outside the domain altogether. Solution set: the ray \((-\infty, 3)\) — every real below 3, and nothing else. The student's line \(h = 2x\) was correct only on the \(x > 3\) branch, where it happens to solve nothing; the branch where the answer lives was exactly the one the dropped absolute value amputated.
