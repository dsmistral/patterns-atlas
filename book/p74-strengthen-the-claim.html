The Patterns Atlas · P74 · the tenth move of tier 2

Strengthen the claim

A true claim, a verified base, an honest step — and a ladder that goes nowhere. That was P66's strangest exhibit: the sum \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2\) is true for every \(n\), yet its step cannot close, because the rung as stated is too weak to hand the step what the step needs — adding \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) to a loan of "at most \(2\)" repays \(2\) plus a toll, and overshoots. The repair this page teaches sounds, the first time you hear it, like a joke: when the ladder will not climb, promise more. Prove the stronger claim \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{1}{n}\) — and the step closes in one line, the slack \(\frac{1}{n}\) paying the toll at every rung, while the original claim rides along free, because \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) is even smaller than \(2\). This page turns the joke into a machine with named obligations: diagnose why the bare rung failed — what, exactly, the step lacked; design a load that pays precisely that deficit; re-verify the base, because a heavier rung presses harder on its bottom rung; re-prove the step, spending every ounce of the load; and collect the original claim as cargo. The runners give the machine its range: the stuck sum from P66, finished properly, with the design of its load \(\frac{1}{n}\) worked backwards from the step's own inequality; Sylvester's Egyptian fractions \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{43} + \cdots\), which stay below \(1\) forever precisely because they equal \(1\) minus an exact remainder; and the Fermat numbers \(3, 5, 17, 257, 65537\), conjectured all prime by Fermat in 1640, vetoed by Euler in 1732 (\(2^{32} + 1 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}297 = 641 \times 6{,}700{,}417\)), and pairwise coprime for a reason no bare ladder can reach: their product identity \(\prod_{iP66 (the ladder, its four obligations, and the loan's strict terms — this page redesigns the rung, so the rung's rules must already be owned). Next: the sums that collapse of their own accord — B11 · The discrete derivative and telescoping.

New word: strengthened induction hypothesis. P66 defined the induction hypothesis as the loan the step works with: the temporary assumption that rung \(k\) holds, granted for one purpose — reaching rung \(k{+}1\). A strengthened hypothesis is a bigger loan: a rung \(P^*(n)\) that says more than the claim asked — more information, a tighter bound, a wider list of cases — chosen so that the step can spend the extra. The rules do not relax: the bigger loan must be repaid at rung \(k{+}1\) in full, every clause of it, and the bottom rung must bear the weight. §5 prices what happens when a loan is bigger than the rungs can carry. New word: the inventor's paradox. Pólya's name, from How to Solve It (1945), for the observed fact that the more ambitious plan — prove something stronger than the question asked — is often easier to carry out. A paradox only in appearance: a stronger hypothesis gives the step more to work with, and the original claim arrives as a free consequence; the danger, which the paradox's name hides, is that the stronger claim must also be true — inventors overshoot. §7 tells where Pólya wrote it down; this page is the paradox with its bills itemized. New word: Egyptian fraction. A sum of distinct unit fractions — fractions with numerator \(1\) and all denominators different, as in \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{6} = 1\). The scribes of ancient Egypt wrote most of their fractions this way, and the representation was a live mathematical question for millennia after; §4's second runner builds the most famous Egyptian fraction of all, one denominator at a time, and §7 credits the mathematician who first asked how quickly such expansions grow.

1 · Before we begin

The reader arrives owning a ladder and its rulebook. From P66: the four obligations — state the rung with one climbing variable, verify the base by direct computation, prove the step for every \(k\) at once as an identity, treat the hypothesis as a loan with strict terms — and the legality account underneath them, well-ordering's minimal criminal, which this page never needs to re-run. The strict terms deserve one more reading, because this page lives inside them. The loan of \(P(k)\) is granted for one purpose, reaching \(P(k{+}1)\); nothing else about \(k\) may be assumed; and when the step needs more than \(P(k)\) gives, P66 named the honest repairs and pointed here: prove a stronger rung. That pointer was the last sentence of P66's Box P66.A, and this page is the promise kept. From P02, one habit is assumed without restatement: every load this page designs goes through the conjecture factory before the ladder runs — a table of small cases first, the climb second, always in that order.

Here is the tension this page resolves. The loan's strict terms read like a warning against ambition: assume only \(P(k)\), prove exactly \(P(k{+}1)\), never more. Read that way, a rung too weak to climb looks like a life sentence — the claim is true, the base is honest, the step is honest, and the ladder still fails, so the defect must be somewhere deep. But the terms cut the other way too, and this is the whole lever: the rung is ours to draft. \(P(n)\) is not handed down by the problem; it is written by the prover, and nothing in the rulebook says it must be the claim verbatim. It must only do two things: be true at the base, and imply the claim at the top. Between those two anchors there is room — room to add slack, add exactness, add coverage — and the added material becomes income the step can spend. The catch, and the reason this page needs its whole length: extra strength is not free. A heavier rung presses harder on its base, and strength that the step never spends is decoration that can break the ladder outright. Between the two jaws — true at the bottom, spent in the step — there is often exactly one good load, and finding it is craft, not luck.

The runner is a number family with a dramatic biography. In 1640 Fermat wrote to Frénicle de Bessy about the numbers \(2^{2^n} + 1\), now called Fermat numbers; he had checked five of them, found primes every time, and conjectured the streak was eternal. In 1732 Euler ended the streak in public — the sixth number factors as \(641 \times 6{,}700{,}417\) — and the conjecture factory's oldest lesson was taught a century before P02 wrote it down: tables confirm and never close. But something else in the family survived the veto, something Euler's arithmetic had no quarrel with, and proving that survivor — the stubborn fact that Fermat numbers share no common factors, pairwise, forever — is where this page's ladder first refuses to climb. The refusal, the diagnosis, and the one-line repair are §2; the machine extracted from it is §3.

2 · A conjecture dies; a stubborn fact survives; a ladder gets stuck

Write the numbers down, the way P02 taught: \(F_0 = 3\), \(F_1 = 5\), \(F_2 = 17\), \(F_3 = 257\), \(F_4 = 65537\) — each one \(F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1\), each one prime as far as pencil and patience reach, five for five. Fermat's conjecture, 1640, in a letter: all of them, forever. The table is impressive; the table is also a table. In 1732 Euler ran the arithmetic P02 would have demanded: \(F_5 = 2^{32} + 1 = 4{,}294{,}967{,}297\), and \(4{,}294{,}967{,}297 = 641 \times 6{,}700{,}417\). Composite. The conjecture died at rung five of an infinite ladder, and the obituary is a lesson this book keeps re-learning: ninety-two years of confirming cases proved exactly nothing.

But run a different census on the survivors, the kind P02 runs when the first question dies: not "are they prime?" but "what do they share?" Compute greatest common divisors, pairwise. \(\gcd(3, 5) = 1\). \(\gcd(3, 17) = 1\), \(\gcd(5, 17) = 1\). \(\gcd(3, 257) = 1\), \(\gcd(5, 257) = 1\), \(\gcd(17, 257) = 1\) — and the six pairs among \(3, 5, 17, 257\), and the ten among the first five: all of them, every pair, gcd \(1\). No Fermat number shares so much as a single prime factor with another. Euler's factorization \(641 \times 6{,}700{,}417\) kills the primes, and even the two factors of the deceased are strangers to every earlier Fermat number — check: \(641\) divides none of \(3, 5, 17, 257, 65537\), and neither does \(6{,}700{,}417\). The table whispers a survivor: the Fermat numbers are pairwise coprime. The whisper is the right kind of claim — an every-\(n\) claim, exactly the shape the ladder eats.

So try the ladder, in good faith, on the claim as asked. Rung \(Q(n)\): "the first \(n{+}1\) Fermat numbers are pairwise coprime." Base: \(Q(0)\) is vacuous — one number, no pairs; \(Q(1)\) says \(\gcd(3, 5) = 1\) — one honest check. Step, for every \(k\): assume the loan — \(F_0, \ldots, F_k\) pairwise coprime — and reach \(F_0, \ldots, F_{k+1}\). Now watch closely, because the failure is instructive. All the coprime-ness of the old numbers is already in the loan; the new pairs to certify are \((F_{k+1}, F_i)\) for each \(i \leq k\), and about \(F_{k+1}\) the loan says nothing at all. It cannot: pairwise coprimality among the first \(k{+}1\) numbers is a fact about those numbers, and the newcomer \(F_{k+1}\) has never been mentioned. The step reaches for information and the rung hands over none. This is P66's Exhibit C in new clothes: there the hypothesis was too weak because it carried no slack; here it is too weak because it carries no reach. Same disease, same organ — the rung as drafted — and, as it turns out, same cure.

Now multiply instead of dividing — the move that looks like busywork and is anything but. \(3 = 3\). \(3 \times 5 = 15\) — and \(15 = 17 - 2 = F_2 - 2\). Interesting; possibly a coincidence. \(3 \times 5 \times 17 = 255\) — and \(255 = 257 - 2 = F_3 - 2\). A coincidence twice is a pattern, says the factory; a third time is a conjecture out loud: \(3 \times 5 \times 17 \times 257 = 65535 = 65537 - 2 = F_4 - 2\). The product of all Fermat numbers before \(F_k\) lands exactly two short of \(F_k\), every time, with the unsettling exactness of a loaded rung:

The loaded rung did not make the step harder; it changed what the step was allowed to do. That is the general mechanism, and §3 names it.

3 · Name the move

P74 · Strengthen the claim — when the rung as given is too weak to hand the step what it needs, draft a stronger rung: prove the loaded claim, and let the original arrive as cargo

When the claim is of the form "\(P(n)\) for every \(n\)", the bare-ladder attempt is honest — the base verifies, the step is written in earnest — and the step fails for one specific, nameable reason: the hypothesis does not state what the step needs (P66's Exhibit C: the loan plus the toll overshoots the target; §2 above: the loan describes the old objects and the step needs the new one) — then do not compute harder, and do not abandon the ladder. Instead: name the deficit — write the failed step down honestly and read off, as an explicit condition, what the hypothesis lacked; design the load — add to the rung the smallest clause that pays that deficit; re-verify the base — a heavier rung presses harder on its bottom rung, and a load that is false at the base is a false claim wearing a proof's clothes; re-prove the step, spending the load — every added clause must appear in the step's work, because an unspent clause proves nothing; and discharge the cargo — verify that the loaded rung implies the original claim outright, then let the ladder conclude and deliver both. What the move buys: a climbing ladder out of a stuck one, at the price of designing rather than inheriting the rung — which is P07's craft, one level up: where P07 invents the object the problem forgot to give, this page invents the claim the problem forgot to ask for.

In words: the hypothesis is a tool the step works with, and a weak tool is repaired by redesign, not by effort. The repair sounds paradoxical only until the bookkeeping is shown, and the bookkeeping has two entries. At the step, the hypothesis is income and the rung \(k{+}1\) is expense; a bare rung's income is too small for the step's outgoings. Loading raises the income — but it also raises the expense, because \(P^*(k{+}1)\) says more than \(P(k{+}1)\) did. The design question is whether the extra income outgrows the extra expense, and the three ways it can are the three kinds of deficit this page will meet: grip — the step needs slack, and a bound with a shrinking margin supplies it (Example 1); exactness — the step needs an equation, not an inequality, and an exact remainder supplies it (§2's product identity, Example 2); coverage — the step itself creates new cases, and a wider rung covers them before they are born (Example 3). Different deficits, one machine.

Why this is legal, and what it costs. The legality costs one sentence: the loaded rung \(P^*(n)\) is just another rung, and P66's four obligations bind it exactly as written — base by computation, step as an identity, loan spent honestly, ladder concluded. The costs are real and §5 prices all three. A load that is too strong is false, and false rungs climb beautifully and prove nothing — the census catches them at the base if anything does. A load that is not spent is decoration, and decoration on a rung can break the climb outright. And a loaded claim that does not imply the original is a theorem about the wrong thing — true, proved, and useless to the question asked. The machine below exists to keep those three bills from arriving unannounced.

Box P74.A · The loading machine — five obligations, in order

Obligation 1 — name the deficit. Write the bare step's attempt in full and mark the exact spot where it needed something the loan did not contain: "the loan of \(\sum \leq 2\) plus the toll \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) exceeds the target \(2\)" — deficit: slack. "The loan certifies pairs among \(F_0, \ldots, F_k\); the target needs pairs with \(F_{k+1}\)" — deficit: reach. A deficit you cannot name is a diagnosis that the failure is not a weak rung at all: P66's gap (base and step not touching), sampled or circular steps, or a false claim — each has its own repair, and loading is not it (§5's third exhibit). The naming is the whole craft's foundation: a load designed for an unnamed deficit is a guess.

Obligation 2 — design the load small, working backwards. Turn the deficit into a condition on the rung: the slack deficit says the bound \(s(n)\) must release margin at least \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) per rung — that is an inequality \(s(k) + \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2} \leq s(k{+}1)\) with \(s\) unknown; solve it backwards from the step's needs, and the family of solutions is the design space. Then do not over- or under-shoot it: the load \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) releases exactly what the toll consumes, while the timid \(2 - \frac{1}{10^n}\) releases almost nothing and fails its step at the first rung — and too strong a load dies at the base instead, which is §5's first exhibit. The squeeze of Example 1 lands on exactly one member of its family, and that uniqueness is the design working correctly.

Obligation 3 — re-verify the base, and send the load through the census first. The loaded claim is a new claim, and it owes P02's full toll before the ladder runs: a small-cases table for the loaded rung, checked by hand or machine, exactly as for any conjecture. The base check is the sharpest of those rows: the load \(2 - \frac{2}{n}\) of §5's first exhibit climbs — its step is a valid identity — and it is false at \(n = 1\), \(2\), and \(3\), where the bare sum already exceeds it. A climbing step attached to a false rung is Exhibit A: the most convincing broken proof this page knows.

Obligation 4 — spend the load, and audit by removal. Every clause of the loaded rung must appear in the step's work, in the sense that removing it breaks the step. The audit runs clause by clause: strike the clause mentally, re-run the step, watch it fail — if no clause's removal fails the step, the load was never needed and the bare rung, examined again, probably climbed all along. §5's second exhibit shows the audit's teeth: a clause that is never spent can still end the climb — a decorated rung broke at its decoration's boundary, rung \(101\), and took the whole ladder with it.

Obligation 5 — discharge the cargo explicitly. After the climb, connect \(P^*(n)\) to \(P(n)\) in writing: "\(\sum_{i \leq n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{1}{n} \leq 2\), so the bare claim holds"; "\(F_{k+1} \equiv 2 \pmod{F_i}\) and both are odd, so the gcd is \(1\)". The connection is a separate check from the climb — a loaded rung that implies the wrong thing delivers a theorem and not the answer — and it is where re-based claims must be watched: "for every \(n \geq 5\)" honestly implies only what it says, and §8's diagnosis exercise prices a "proof" that forgot the difference.

Box P74.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. The move is wherever an induction "almost closes" — the phrase that, in the wild, almost always means the rung is one clause short. The number families keep meeting it: §2's Fermat identity and Example 2's Sylvester identity are twins — exact-remainder loads paying coprimality and smallness as cargo — and B12's Fibonacci world is full of rungs that only climb when they carry two seeds or two coordinates at once (P66's plural bases were the same design instinct in miniature). P66's own pages used the move without naming it: the tromino board's rung said "every missing square" from birth — a coverage load, Example 3 below — and the strong form of induction (borrow every rung below \(k\), not just rung \(k\)) is a depth load, the same machine set to a different slot. Beyond the book, the largest consumer is computer science: a loop or recursion is proved correct by an invariant, and the usable invariant is almost never the property wanted — it is a loaded claim, strong enough to survive one step and implying the property at the end; every "assume the invariant" in a verified program is Obligation 2 run by a compiler. And the classic showcase with its own shape — Cauchy's proof of the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality by forward–backward induction — loads the ladder rather than the rung, and §8's third exercise climbs it end to end.

The three-key signature — the checklist that says the move applies, and the checklist that says the load was honest. (1) Claim type: an every-\(n\) claim whose bare-ladder step fails at the hypothesis — not a false claim (P66's \(2^n > n^2\) witnesses stay), not a base gap (the base and step never touch), not a sampled or circular step — a step that would close if only the loan said more. (2) Problem shape: the deficit can be written as a condition the rung could satisfy — an inequality to release slack, an equation to carry exactness, a quantifier to extend coverage — so the design space is visible before any load is chosen. (3) Tools at hand: a census that can test the loaded claim at small \(n\) before the climb is attempted, and a base that can bear the extra weight — some true loads still die at the base, and §5's first exhibit shows the death. And the diagnostic read off the finished proof: a loaded induction is exactly as strong as its spent load — the audit by removal is part of the proof, not a politeness, and the reader should run it on every loaded proof they meet, including this page's.

4 · Three loaded ladders in public

The machine runs three times, in public, the way P02 ran its tables: every claim closed here is one the reader can re-derive with a pencil, and §8 hands over the keys. The three climbs are chosen for their different deficits: the first loads for grip — the step needs slack no bare bound provides; the second loads for exactness — the step needs an equation, and the load is an exact remainder; the third loads for coverage — the step itself manufactures the cases it must then handle. Together they show the machine's whole range, and each pays a debt this book already owes: P66's stuck Exhibit C is finally finished properly, the Egyptian fractions promised in the on-ramp are built and closed, and the tromino board's quiet strength from P66's §2 is named for what it was.

Example 1 · P66's stuck sum, finished properly — the load designed backwards

Moves used: P66 (the ladder, unchanged — only the rung is new), P02 (the census that tests the load before the climb, and the table that suggests it).

The debt, as P66 left it: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2\) is true, wanted, and unclimbable — the bare loan of "at most \(2\)" plus the toll \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) overshoots \(2\), and no honest algebra closes the gap. Obligation 1 — name the deficit. The step needed the loan to come with slack: room below the ceiling, at least \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) of it, to absorb the toll. The bare rung carries none. Obligation 2 — design, backwards. Let the rung be \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq s(n)\) with \(s\) unknown. The step's requirement, written as a condition on \(s\):

\[s(k) + \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2} \;\leq\; s(k{+}1) \quad \text{for every } k \geq 1,\]

and the base's requirement: \(s(1) \geq 1\). Now design backwards: a rung bound that releases slack steadily is a sequence \(s(n)\) that grows — but it must stay below the ceiling \(2\), or it is false in the limit. The natural candidates shrink their margin geometrically or harmonically: try the family \(s(n) = 2 - \frac{C}{n}\), whose margin \(C/n\) releases exactly \(\frac{C}{k} - \frac{C}{k{+}1} = \frac{C}{k(k{+}1)}\) per rung. The step demands \(\frac{C}{k(k{+}1)} \geq \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\), which cross-multiplies to \(C(k{+}1) \geq k\) — true for every \(k\) exactly when \(C \geq 1\). The base demands \(1 \leq 2 - C\), so \(C \leq 1\). Two jaws, one point: \(C = 1\) — the load \(s(n) = 2 - \frac{1}{n}\) is the unique member of its family that both climbs and survives its base. The squeeze is the design's signature, and §5's first exhibit shows what happens on the wrong side of it.

The census, before the climb — the loaded rung is a new conjecture and pays P02's toll first: \(n = 1\): \(1 \leq 1\), equality. \(n = 2\): \(1.25 \leq 1.5\). \(n = 3\): \(1.3611 \leq 1.6667\). \(n = 10\): \(1.5498 \leq 1.9\). Two honest sentences close the account. First: the loaded claim is stronger than asked, and it was easier to prove — the paradox delivered in one worked example, with the mechanism visible: the strength is not weight, it is working capital, and the step spent it. Second: the load is the frontier of its family, not of all loads — §8's exploration finds bound families that climb and run tighter than \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\), and the true frontier is the exact value of the infinite sum, a number with a name (\(\pi^2/6\)) that this book's ladder will not reach for many pages yet; the bound \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) is the best the harmonic family gives, and its proof never pretended otherwise.

Example 2 · Sylvester's sequence — the load that carries an exact remainder

Moves used: P66 (the ladder), P20 (the step is an identity, checked by clearing denominators — the same trust in algebra over arithmetic).

Build the greediest Egyptian expansion of \(1\) there is: start with the gap \(1\), and at each stage take the largest unit fraction, with a denominator not yet used, that keeps the running total strictly below the target — never allowed to close it. The first bite is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), leaving a gap of \(\tfrac{1}{2}\); \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) itself is spent, so the largest bite left in the gap is \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), leaving \(\tfrac{1}{6}\); then \(\tfrac{1}{7}\) (the unit \(\tfrac{1}{6}\) would close the gap exactly, and is forbidden), leaving \(\tfrac{1}{42}\); then \(\tfrac{1}{43}\), leaving \(\tfrac{1}{1806}\); then \(\tfrac{1}{1807}\), leaving \(\tfrac{1}{3263442}\). The denominators are Sylvester's sequence

\[s_1 = 2, \qquad s_{k+1} = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_k + 1 \;\;=\; S_k + 1, \qquad 2,\; 3,\; 7,\; 43,\; 1807,\; 3263443,\; 10650056950807,\; \ldots\]

and the machine never closes the gap: it shrinks it to \(\tfrac{1}{S_k}\) and forces the next denominator to be \(S_k + 1\), one larger than the product of everything spent so far — greed, punished by exactly one unit, forever. The claim wanted is the one Sylvester stated in 1880: the bites converge to the target, \(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{s_i} = 1\). The finite version to climb: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{s_i} < 1\) for every \(n\).

The bare ladder, and where it dies. Loan the rung its conclusion — "the partial sum is below \(1\)" — and attempt the step: from \(\sum_{i \leq k} \frac{1}{s_i} < 1\), add \(\frac{1}{s_{k+1}}\) and conclude \(< 1\). Nothing follows: "below \(1\)" is compatible with being below by \(\tfrac{1}{1000}\), and the toll \(\frac{1}{s_{k+1}}\) could be larger than the room left. The bare rung fails at the hypothesis — the same seat as Example 1's failure, but the missing clause is different: not slack but exactness. The step needs to know how much room is left, to the unit, and an inequality cannot say it.

The load. Sylvester's own rung is exact:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{s_i} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{1}{S_n}, \qquad S_n = s_1 s_2 \cdots s_n.\]

The room left is not "some" — it is \(\frac{1}{S_n}\), a single specified unit fraction. The census, before any climbing:

Sylvester's ladder, rung by rung. The partial sum is always a unit short of 1, and the shortness is exactly the reciprocal of the product — the identity the load carries. The greedy denominators follow from it: the gap after rung \(k\) is \(1/S_k\), and the largest unit fraction that fits inside a gap of \(1/S_k\) without closing it is \(1/(S_k{+}1)\), forcing \(s_{k+1}\).

The climb. Base, \(n = 1\): \(\frac{1}{2} = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\), a computation. Step, for every \(k\): assume the loaded rung \(\sum_{i \leq k} \frac{1}{s_i} = 1 - \frac{1}{S_k}\), add the toll \(\frac{1}{s_{k+1}} = \frac{1}{S_k + 1}\), and check the identity the P20 way — clear denominators, trust algebra, touch no calculator:

\[1 - \frac{1}{S} + \frac{1}{S+1} \;=\; 1 - \frac{(S+1) - S}{S(S+1)} \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{S(S+1)} \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{S_{k+1}},\]

The cargo is the claim itself: \(1 - \frac{1}{S_n} < 1\) for every \(n\), so the bare rung \(\sum_{i \leq n} \frac{1}{s_i} < 1\) rides for free, and the infinite statement follows — the partial sums are increasing and bounded, so they converge, and the loaded identity pins the limit: the bites really do converge to \(1\), with \(\frac{1}{S_n} \to 0\). Sylvester's 1880 claim, proved by loading it harder.

The twin cargo: coprimality, again. The Fermat numbers of §2 pay smallness; the Sylvester numbers pay the same bill twice, by the same mechanism: \(s_{k+1} = S_k + 1\) is congruent to \(1\) modulo every earlier \(s_i\) (each divides \(S_k\)), so \(\gcd(s_i, s_j) = 1\) for \(i \neq j\) — an infinite family of pairwise coprime integers, produced as a by-product of an exactness load. Two pages of this book now own infinite coprime families, and both got them the same way: not by studying coprimality, but by loading a different climb until coprimality fell out of the luggage. When a proof needs infinitely many coprime moduli, the reflex to reach for is not "find primes" — it is find a product-plus-one recurrence: it built §2's Fermat family and this one, and it is the same reflex that powers the oldest trick in the theory of numbers — Euclid's product-plus-one witnesses for the infinitude of primes.

Example 3 · The tromino board — the load that widens the quantifier

Moves used: P66 (the ladder and, in its §2, this very rung — used quietly, without the loading lens), P07 (the center tromino is an auxiliary construction, built for the step's sake and gone from the final tiling's account books).

The claim is P66's tromino theorem: a \(2^n \times 2^n\) board with any one square missing can be tiled by L-shaped trominoes. It is true for every position of the missing square — and the strength is not decoration; it is the engine. Run the ladder honestly and watch where the bare version dies.

The weak rung, and its death. Try to climb the weaker, "natural" claim \(W(n)\): a \(2^n \times 2^n\) board with some missing square can be tiled — weaker because it asks less of the board. Base: \(W(1)\), the \(2 \times 2\) board, tiles around any missing square. Step: given a \(2^{k+1} \times 2^{k+1}\) board with missing square \(s\), split it into four \(2^k \times 2^k\) quadrants and look for help. The quadrant containing \(s\) is covered — \(W(k)\) applies. The other three are full: no missing square at all, so \(W(k)\) says nothing about them — its hypothesis is not satisfied, and no tiling of a full quadrant exists anyway — a full \(2^k \times 2^k\) board has \(4^k\) cells, never divisible by \(3\). The weak rung cannot even state what the three full quadrants need. The deficit is coverage: the step does not get to choose which square is missing in each sub-board — the split chooses. A rung quantified over "some" position has no grip on sub-boards whose position is chosen for them.

The load. Strengthen to \(E(n)\): every \(2^n \times 2^n\) board with every possible missing square can be tiled — quantifier widened from existence to coverage. This is exactly the rung P66's §2 wrote down without comment; this page's job is to show it was not caution but necessity, the load designed backwards from the step: since the step will need every position-pattern, the rung must promise every position-pattern. The step, loaded: given the \(2^{k+1}\) board with missing square \(s\), split into quadrants; \(s\) sits in one of them, and \(E(k)\) covers that quadrant whatever position \(s\) has. For the other three, load the choice backwards — place one center tromino so it covers exactly one square of each full quadrant, the three squares adjacent to the board's center, P07's auxiliary construction appearing in the same role it played in P66. Each full quadrant is now a \(2^k \times 2^k\) board with one missing square, at a position the construction chose — and \(E(k)\) covers it. Four quadrants, four appeals to the loaded rung, base \(E(1)\) checked at all four positions. The load is spent: strike the word "every" and the proof collapses at the three full quadrants.

The cargo is the theorem's own strength: the conclusion "every position tiles" is not a bonus bolted onto the claim — it is the load that made the step close, delivered as cargo. And the machine generalizes: the same split-and-plug step proves tilings for boards of size \(2^n\) with any pattern of one missing square per quadrant, because the rung's quantifier was widened once, at design time, to cover every position the step could ever manufacture. The lab's census re-derives the \(4 \times 4\) sub-boards' tilings by depth-first search and re-pins the count P66's lab found for every missing position; the page's §8 hands one of them over as an exercise's engine.

5 · When it fails — the false load, the unspent clause, and the diseases loading cannot cure

Three exhibits, one per way the machine jams. The first two are the beginner's accidents; the third is the diagnosis that protects the whole book from over-use.

Exhibit A — the load that climbs but cannot land. Return to Example 1's design space and pick the wrong \(C\): the family \(s(n) = 2 - \frac{C}{n}\) with \(C = 2\), the bound \(2 - \frac{2}{n}\). The step is fine — better than fine: the release \(\frac{2}{k} - \frac{2}{k+1} = \frac{2}{k(k{+}1)}\) covers the toll \(\frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) with room to spare, since \(\frac{2}{k(k{+}1)} \geq \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) cross-multiplies to \(2(k{+}1) \geq k\), true for every \(k\) at once. The ladder climbs beautifully. And the claim is false: at \(n = 1\) the bound says \(0\) while the sum is \(1\); at \(n = 2\), \(1\) against \(1.25\); at \(n = 3\), \(1.3333\) against \(1.3611\). The cure is P02's, not this page's: census before climb. The loaded rung is a new conjecture and owes small cases its first inspection — and the census kills \(C = 2\) in one row. Base placement is part of load design, and the census is where that price is quoted. §8's fifth exploration returns to exactly this frontier.

Exhibit B — the load that is never spent. The mirror-image accident. Take Example 1's good load and bolt on an extra clause, for no reason: the rung becomes "\(\sum_{i \leq n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{1}{n}\), and \(n \leq 100\)". Every part of it is true; every small case passes; the climb starts fine. But a clause that plays no role in the step is not part of the load — it is ballast, and ballast falls off at the worst moment: the step from \(k = 100\) to \(k + 1 = 101\) needs the rung at \(n = 101\), where the ballast clause is false. The proof breaks at rung 101, having been unsound in intent from rung 1. The diagnostic is Obligation 4 run in reverse: every clause of the load must be spent in the step — if the step never uses a clause, the clause must still be true at \(k+1\), and a clause true only at small \(k\) will eventually veto the climb. This is the precise sense in which the load must be "strong enough": strength beyond what the step spends is not safety margin; it is a new, unproved conjecture smuggled into the rung.

Exhibit C — the diseases loading cannot cure. The machine has one power: it repairs steps that fail at the hypothesis. Three neighboring failures look like it and are not. The false claim: \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\) for all pairs with \(b = a + 1\) is true, but "every \(n\) has an odd number of divisors" is false at \(n = 2\) — no load rescues a claim with a counterexample; the counterexample owns the veto, and the only moves are P02's (find the witnesses) or a re-base past the wreckage, which concedes the small cases rather than proving them. The base gap: when the base and step never touch — the step starts at \(k = 1\) but the claim only holds from \(n = 5\) on, as with \(2^n > n^2\), true from \(n = 5\) but false at \(n = 2, 3, 4\) — the repair is P66's re-base, the base moved up to \(n = 5\) and the claim restated for \(n \geq 5\); loading changes nothing, because the step was never the failure. The depth deficit: when the step needs not the last rung but a lower one — the Fibonacci recurrence climbing from two seeds — the repair is strong induction, P66's borrowing ladder, which is a change to what the ladder lends, not to what the rung says. The reflex to check before loading anything: where does the step fail, and at what seat? Hypothesis — load. Base — re-base. Floor — borrow. Counterexample — surrender or repair the claim itself.

Scope and price of P74

The move costs. A stronger claim is more to prove at the base, and the census of the loaded rung is a fresh debt to P02 — Example 1's squeeze took two tables, not one. A load that is never found leaves the original debt standing: the design space of rung strengthenings is not guaranteed to contain one that both climbs and lands, and the \(\sum 1/i^2\) frontier below \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) is exactly the place where the harmonic family runs out. And the audit by removal is not optional hygiene: Exhibits A and B are both caught by it — A by the census, B by the spend check — and both survive a careless "the step works". Loaded induction is a loan against the ladder's credit; the page's whole discipline is the bookkeeping that keeps the loan honest.

6 · What next

The doors this page opens

Nearest: B11 · The discrete derivative and telescoping, the bridge that pays this page's debts forward. Example 1's release \(\frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k{+}1} = \frac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\) is a telescoping sum in miniature — a bound engineered so its pieces cancel — and Example 2's exact remainder \(1 - \frac{1}{S_n}\) is the telescoping identity with the last term left visible. B11 makes the mechanism a named tool: the difference operator, the sum that un-does it, and the designs — like both of this page's loads — where the rung bound is chosen because its differences telescope. The bridge then feeds P69 · Telescoping, which collects the general move.

Working range: P77 · Work backwards from the answer — this page's Obligation 2 is P77's whole method, specialized to induction. Designing the load backwards from the step's requirement is the same reversal as designing a construction from its endpoint; P74 is, in a sense, P77 run on a proof instead of a problem. When a load resists design, the general page's tactics — start from the last line, ask what the previous line must have been — are the ones to reach for, and several of §8's exercises are easier backwards.

Far range: P72 · Monovariants — the load's cousin in the world of processes. A monovariant is a quantity that must move one way and cannot move forever; the proof loads the process with a measurement strong enough to terminate it. The family resemblance is real — both moves prove a target claim by proving a stronger, more usable auxiliary one — and the difference is the setting: P74 loads statements about \(n\); P72 loads measurements on states. A reader who felt the paradox click here will feel it again there.

The default habit

When an induction step fails, do not reach for a bigger hammer — reach for a pen. Write the step's requirement as a condition on the rung: "\(s(k) + \text{toll} \leq s(k{+}1)\)". The condition is the design spec for the load; only then go shopping in the family of rungs that satisfy it, and take the census along. The habit in one sentence: the step tells you what the rung needed to say; the load is just the rung, finally saying it.

7 · Lineage

The oldest loads are Fermat's. In 1640, writing to Frénicle, Fermat reported his numbers \(F_k = 2^{2^k} + 1\) and the identity §2 climbed — the product of the earlier ones falls two short — and he believed, on the census's evidence, that every \(F_k\) was prime. Nearly a century later, in 1732, Euler opened the case that shows why this page never trusts a census alone: \(F_5 = 4294967297 = 641 \times 6700417\), composite, the first witness sitting far past every table Fermat could have written. The coprimality cargo — §2's real payload — survived the wreckage untouched, which is the loading lesson in historical form: the load outlived the claim it was built for. The product-plus-one reflex Fermat used is the same one Sylvester industrialized.

Sylvester, 1880. Investigating unit fractions — the Egyptian obsession — Sylvester hit the sequence \(2, 3, 7, 43, 1807, \ldots\) and proved Example 2's identity: the greedy bites converge to 1, always exactly one reciprocal of a product short. The sequence was not his target; it was the cargo of a load designed for an exactness deficit — the historical proof that the move predates its name by centuries.

Cauchy, 1821 — the showcase load. In the Cours d'analyse, Cauchy proved the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality by forward–backward induction: prove the case \(n = 2\), climb to every power of 2, then climb back down by averaging. The proof loads the ladder's shape rather than the rung's content — every power of 2 is a stronger, stranger claim than "every \(n\)" — and it remains the standard exhibit of a load that changes what is proved and how. §8's third exercise climbs it end to end.

Pólya names the paradox; Gowers builds the registry. In How to Solve It (1945, Princeton University Press), Pólya named the inventor's paradox and stated it in the words this page's opening already quoted: "The more ambitious plan may have more chances of success." Sixty-four years later, Timothy Gowers launched the Tricki, a wiki of proof techniques — first made public in April 2009 — with contributors including Terence Tao and Ben Green, and its induction articles are effectively a field guide to loads: strengthen the induction hypothesis, with worked guidance on what to strengthen and why. This page's Box P74.A is that folklore, given the book's obligations-and-audit discipline; the move is old, the bookkeeping is what this atlas adds.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 · The geometric twin ●○○
Prove that \(\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{8} + \cdots + \frac{1}{2^n} < 1\) for every \(n \geq 1\), by loading. (The bare rung fails exactly the way §4's first example's did — find the seat of the failure first, then run the full Obligations 1 through 5.)

Hint

The step from \(k\) to \(k+1\) needs to know how much room is left below \(1\); the loaded rung that knows it is an equality. Run the \(C\)-family squeeze exactly as §4 and §5 did: write the loaded bound as \(1 - \frac{C}{2^n}\), find the base's jaw on \(C\), the step's jaw on \(C\), and the unique \(C\) that passes both.

Worked

Deficit: exactness — "below \(1\)" says nothing about the room left, and the toll \(\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\) might not fit. Load: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{2^i} = 1 - \frac{1}{2^n}\). Family squeeze: the bound \(1 - \frac{C}{2^n}\) releases \(\frac{C}{2^{k+1}}\) per rung; the step demands \(\frac{C}{2^{k+1}} \geq \frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\), so \(C \geq 1\); the base demands \(\frac{1}{2} \leq 1 - \frac{C}{2}\), so \(C \leq 1\); \(C = 1\) again, unique. Base: \(\frac{1}{2} = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\), equality. Step: \(1 - \frac{1}{2^k} + \frac{1}{2^{k+1}} = 1 - \frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\), one line of algebra, every clause spent. Cargo: \(1 - \frac{1}{2^n} < 1\) for every \(n\), so the original claim rides free. Note the structural identity with §4's climb: the harmonic sum's load \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) and this geometric load are the same machine on two ladders — release engineered to cover the toll, with the constant squeezed to a point at both jaws.

Exercise 2 · Fibonacci change-making ●●○
Prove that every positive integer is a sum of distinct Fibonacci numbers, using \(G_1, G_2, G_3, \ldots = 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \ldots\) (each term the sum of the two before it). The bare ladder over \(m\) fails at a seat this page has named — identify it, then design the load that covers it.

Hint

Trying to climb "\(m\) is a subset sum of the first \(k\) Fibonacci numbers" in \(m\) fails because the step must manufacture facts about smaller numbers it never assumed — the coverage deficit of Example 3. Load with an interval: prove the rung \(R(n)\): "every \(m\) with \(1 \leq m < G_{n+1}\) is a sum of distinct numbers among \(G_1, \ldots, G_n\)."

Worked

Base \(R(1)\): \(m < G_2 = 2\) means \(m = 1 = G_1\). Step: assume \(R(k)\), take any \(m < G_{k+2}\). If \(m < G_{k+1}\), the rung itself finishes. Otherwise \(G_{k+1} \leq m < G_{k+1} + G_k\), so \(m - G_{k+1} < G_k < G_{k+1}\), and the rung (at the same \(k\)) writes \(m - G_{k+1}\) as a sum of distinct \(G_1, \ldots, G_k\); adding \(G_{k+1}\) keeps all denominations distinct, and \(R(k+1)\) is proved — the load was spent, not stretched. Cargo: every \(m \geq 1\) has \(m < G_{n+1}\) for some \(n\) (the \(G\) grow without bound), so every positive integer is Fibonacci change. Two honest footnotes. First, the load is a real load: \(R(n)\) is a stronger statement than "some \(m\)" — it is a uniform bound on coverage, and the step could not close without the interval's lower edge. Second, the representation is far from unique (\(3 = 3 = 1 + 2\), \(6 = 5 + 1 = 3 + 2 + 1\)); the unique representation theorem — Zeckendorf's, which forbids consecutive \(G\)'s — is a different and deeper climb, and this page claims nothing about it.

Exercise 3 · Cauchy's forward–backward ladder ●●●
Climb §7's showcase end to end: for \(n\) positive reals \(a_1, \ldots, a_n\), prove \(\frac{a_1 + \cdots + a_n}{n} \geq \sqrt[n]{a_1 \cdots a_n}\). (a) Prove the case \(n = 2\) from \((\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2 \geq 0\). (b) Prove the forward step: the case \(n = 2m\) follows from the case \(m\) applied twice plus the case \(2\). (c) Prove the backward step: the case \(n\) follows from the case \(n + 1\) — given \(a_1, \ldots, a_n\), apply the \(n+1\) case to \(a_1, \ldots, a_n, g\), where \(g = \sqrt[n]{a_1 \cdots a_n}\). (d) Assemble the full proof, and explain why the naive single-variable load — "assume the case \(n\), derive the case \(n+1\)" — fails here, and what was loaded instead. (e) The lab's detector re-checks your arithmetic: forward chain, backward identity, and the inequality itself on random tuples.

Hint

For (b), split \(2m\) numbers into two halves of \(m\); the big mean is the mean of the two half-means, the big product-roots multiply. For (c), write the \(n+1\) inequality for \((a_1, \ldots, a_n, g)\) and simplify — the \(g\) inside the root makes the right side collapse. For (d), ask what the step \(n \to n{+}1\) would need: there is no way to average \(n+1\) numbers that reuses a mean of \(n\) of them; the load that works is on the ladder, not the rung.

Worked

(a) \((\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2 \geq 0\) expands to \(a + b \geq 2\sqrt{ab}\), so \(\frac{a+b}{2} \geq \sqrt{ab}\). (b) Given \(2m\) numbers, call the half-means \(M_1, M_2\) and half geometric means \(G_1, G_2\). The full mean is \(\frac{M_1 + M_2}{2} \geq \sqrt{M_1 M_2}\) by the case \(2\), and \(M_1 \geq G_1\), \(M_2 \geq G_2\) by the case \(m\); the full geometric mean is \(\sqrt{G_1 G_2} \leq \sqrt{M_1 M_2} \leq \frac{M_1 + M_2}{2}\). Doubling covers every power of 2. (c) Apply the \(n+1\) case to \(a_1, \ldots, a_n, g\): the left side is \(\frac{nA + g}{n+1}\) (where \(A\) is the \(n\)-mean), the right side is \((a_1 \cdots a_n \cdot g)^{1/(n+1)} = (g^n \cdot g)^{1/(n+1)} = g\). So \(\frac{nA + g}{n+1} \geq g\), which rearranges to \(nA \geq ng\), the case \(n\) — the descent runs from every \(n+1\) down to \(n\). (d) From powers of 2, descend repeatedly to reach every \(n\) — forward to a power of 2 above \(n\), backward the rest of the way. The naive load fails for a structural reason: a mean of \(n+1\) numbers is not built from a mean of \(n\) of them — adding one number re-weights everything — so no rung-only strengthening helps; Cauchy loaded the ladder's shape (only powers of 2 climbed forward), a stronger and stranger claim about where the proof lives, and paid for it with a second machine (the descent) to cover the gaps. This is P74's one exhibit where the load is not on the rung, and it completes the page's taxonomy: grip (Example 1), exactness (Example 2), coverage (Example 3), and now shape. (e) The lab verifies the case \(2\), the doubling inequality, and the descent identity \(\frac{nA+g}{n+1} \geq g\) on randomized data, and samples the full inequality for \(n\) up to 12; all of it is checkable by hand for small \(n\), and the detector is a census, not a proof.

Exercise 4 · Three broken loads, diagnosed cold ◆
Below are three "proofs" built on this page's move. Each is broken. No hint; name the disease, the seat of the failure, and the honest repair — or the honest verdict, if no repair exists. (i) "Theorem: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{2}{n}\) for every \(n \geq 1\). Proof: the step is fine — \(\frac{2}{k(k{+}1)} \geq \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) — and the base is \(n = 1\)." (ii) "Theorem: \(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{1}{n}\) for every \(n \geq 1\). Proof by loaded induction on the rung '\(\sum_{i \leq n} \frac{1}{i^2} \leq 2 - \frac{1}{n}\), and \(n \leq 100\)': base \(n = 1\) holds, step from \(k\) to \(k+1\) holds, so every \(n\) holds." (iii) "Theorem: \(2^n > n^2\) for every \(n \geq 1\). Proof by strengthening: prove instead the stronger claim \(2^n > n^2\) for every \(n \geq 5\) — base \(n = 5\), step routine — and the original follows."

Hint

None — that is the exercise. §5 has already shown you every disease; run the three diagnostics (census, spend check, cargo check) and see which one vetoes which proof.

Worked

(i) False load: the step genuinely climbs, but the base is false — \(1 \leq 0\) fails at \(n = 1\) (and \(n = 2, 3\) after it). The census vetoes in one row. Repair: re-base at \(n = 4\) with the three small cases checked by hand and the claim restated for \(n \geq 4\) — true, and tighter than the page's load, at the price §5 quoted. (ii) Unspent clause: the ballast "\(n \leq 100\)" plays no role in any step, so it is not part of the load; it must still hold at the next rung, and it fails there — the step from \(k = 100\) needs the rung at \(n = 101\). The spend check vetoes. Repair: drop the clause; the remaining rung is exactly Example 1's load and climbs. (iii) Not a load at all: "for every \(n \geq 5\)" is weaker than "for every \(n \geq 1\)" — it abandons \(n = 1, \ldots, 4\) rather than strengthening anything, so the cargo check fails: nothing carries the small cases home. The honest verdict is harsher: no repair exists, because the original claim is false — \(n = 2, 3, 4\) are counterexamples (\(4 \not> 4\), \(8 \not> 9\), \(16 \not> 16\)). The "proof" is not a loading that failed; it is a counterexample wearing a load's clothing. The three diagnostics together are the page in miniature: census, spend, cargo.

Exercise 5 · The frontier of the family ◐
Example 1 loaded (sum 1/i^2) with \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) and called it "the frontier of its family, not of all loads". Map both frontiers. (a) Recap: why is \(C = 1\) the exact working point of the family \(2 - \frac{C}{n}\)? (b) The \(p\)-family: which \(p\) make the bound \(2 - \frac{1}{n^p}\) a load (release \(\frac{1}{k^p} - \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^p} \geq \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^2}\) for every \(k\), base \(1 \leq 1\))? Run a census over \(p \in \{\frac{1}{2}, \frac{5}{4}, \frac{3}{2}, 1, 2\}\) and report each verdict honestly. (c) The whisper: the true ceiling is \(\frac{\pi^2}{6} = 1.6449340668\ldots\); at \(n = 10^6\) the partial sum is \(1.6449330668\ldots\), a gap of almost exactly \(\frac{1}{10^6}\). Explain why that gap is no coincidence. (d) The true frontier: the tightest honest rung would be the exact tail, and it is not a nice formula — but the telescoping comparison \(\frac{1}{i^2} < \frac{1}{i^2 - \frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1}{i - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{i + \frac{1}{2}}\) sums to \(\frac{2}{3}\) over \(i \geq 2\), giving the bound \(\frac{5}{3}\). Verify the identity, chase where it comes from, and say which page of this atlas owns the general move.

Hint

For (b), the release shrinks as \(p\) grows; compute the first failing \(k\) for each vetoed \(p\), and for \(p = \frac{1}{2}\) do not settle for numerics — find the \(k\) past which the mean-value estimate \(\frac{1}{k^p} - \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^p} \approx \frac{p}{k^{p+1}}\) settles the climb, then check the small \(k\) by hand or by the lab's detector. For (c), compare the tail \(\sum_{i > n} \frac{1}{i^2}\) with the telescoping bound \(\sum_{i > n} \frac{1}{i(i{+}1)} = \frac{1}{n{+}1}\). For (d), the identity is two lines of algebra; the door it opens was named in §6.

Worked

(a) Two jaws: the base demands \(C \leq 1\), the step demands \(C \geq 1\); squeeze to a point. Nothing in the family is left over. (b) Verdicts from the census (the lab's detector re-runs all of them): \(p = 2\) fails first at \(k = 3\) (\(0.0486 < 0.0625\)); \(p = \frac{3}{2}\) fails at \(k = 4\); \(p = \frac{5}{4}\) fails at \(k = 5\) (\(0.0273 < 0.0278\), the nearest miss of the census); \(p = 1\) climbs (Example 1); \(p = \frac{1}{2}\) climbs — the release \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{k{+}1}}\) beats the toll at \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\) by direct computation, and for \(k \geq 4\) the estimate \(\approx \frac{1}{2k^{3/2}}\) against \(\frac{1}{k^2}\) gives a safety factor of about \(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2}\), which only grows — the release beats the toll by an ever-widening ratio, though both shrink in absolute size. Honest labeling: the small-\(k\) checks and the detector are a census, not a proof for all \(k\); the all-\(k\) statement wants the machinery of estimates this book reaches much later, and the reader who wants it now has found the right door (below). Pattern, worth stating plainly: the \(p\)-family works for \(p \leq 1\) and dies for \(p > 1\), with the first vetoed rung creeping outward as \(p\) falls to \(1\) — the frontier \(p = 1\) mirrors the \(C\)-family's \(C = 1\), and that is no accident: both are the statement "the release must beat the toll, with equality allowed exactly at the design point". (c) The gap \(\frac{\pi^2}{6} - \sum_{i \leq n} \frac{1}{i^2}\) is squeezed between the telescoping tails \(\sum_{i>n} \frac{1}{i(i{+}1)} = \frac{1}{n+1}\) and \(\frac{1}{n}\); at \(n = 10^6\) both are \(10^{-6}\) to seven digits. The load \(2 - \frac{1}{n}\) was honest but slack — its gap is \(\frac{1}{n}\) above a ceiling that sits \(0.355\) below \(2\). (d) The identity: \(\frac{1}{i - \frac{1}{2}} - \frac{1}{i + \frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{(i - \frac{1}{2})(i + \frac{1}{2})} = \frac{1}{i^2 - \frac{1}{4}} > \frac{1}{i^2}\). Summed from \(i = 2\), every interior term cancels and \(\frac{5}{3} = 1 + \frac{2}{3}\) bounds the whole series — a ceiling \(0.333\) tighter than the page's. The cancellation is a telescoping sum, the design is "choose the rung bound because its differences telescope", and the pages that own the general move are B11 and, through it, P69 — the door §6 already pointed at, and the next page this book opens.
