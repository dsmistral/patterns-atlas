The Patterns Atlas · P52 · the fifth move of tier 5

Microlocal propagation of singularities

Roughness has an address — a place and a set of directions — and a differential equation draws rays through address space. The propagation theorem is the verdict: in elliptic directions the roughness dissolves; along the equation's own rays it travels. What arrives must have been sent.

New word: the wavefront set. A function's roughness is where it refuses to be smooth — a jump, a corner, a spike. P42 taught the shelf census: split the frequency content into dyadic octaves (the Littlewood–Paley bands) and watch which shelves stay stocked — roughness is the shelf traffic that never dies. This page adds the second half of the address: at each location, ask which directions the stubborn shelves live in. The wavefront set \(WF(f)\) is the full address book: the set of pairs (location, frequency-direction) where some windowed shelf census refuses to die. A differential equation has a principal symbol \(p(x, \tau, \xi)\) — its top-order part, read as a function on the address book (for the wave equation, \(p = \tau^2 - c^2\xi^2\)). The symbol's bicharacteristics — the rays — are the curves of Hamilton's equations through the places where \(p = 0\). The propagation of singularities is the verdict this page builds toward: where \(p \neq 0\) the equation erases roughness; where \(p = 0\) the roughness can only travel along the rays — it cannot stop, cannot stray, and cannot appear unsent.

1 · Before we begin

P42 named this page at its working door, the first of the two its §6 opened — propagation of singularities, P52, microlocal analysis — and drew the commission: "a singularity is located not just in space but in the directions and scales of a function's frequency content — a dyadic census of bands, one per octave — and the theorem says that singularities travel along explicit curves through that census. The Littlewood–Paley bands of Example 3 are the door's hinge." P42 built the hinge and left the turning unexplained: the shelves were counted, but nothing yet said what a governing equation does to the traffic. P33 taught the habit this page now spends: an obstruction is local information that fails to glue, and it is counted, not wished away. P47 sharpened the errand at its propagation door: "a singularity is an obstruction with an address (P33) at a scale (P42), and the propagation theorem says obstructions travel — along the bicharacteristic flow, what arrives must have been sent." P33 had already put this page in its waiting room — "P52, P54, and P65 wait behind these, each a customer of the same cochain bookkeeping" — and the debt falls due here: the wavefront set is an address book of obstructions, and the theorem that moves them is the bookkeeping made kinetic. And P49 closed the tier's fourth footer with the same errand from the model ocean's far side: "tier 5 leaves the model ocean for rough terrain: the microlocal propagation trick, where singularities are tracked along rays to predict where the roughness will go."

Two older habits hold the ladder. P24 taught that complicated signals are sums of simple ones — this page's exact identity \(u^n_j = F(j+n) + G(j-n)\) is that page's wave rack, now with the sums forced to hold in integers, bit-for-bit. And P09 taught the invariant-habit — find what the process cannot change — which returns here as the ray bookkeeping: along a bicharacteristic, \(\tau\) and \(\xi\) are conserved, the address rides at speed \(c\), and the corner positions of a plucked string are forced before anyone computes a single step.

Here is the tension. P42's census says where roughness lives, but not where it is going; P33 and P47 count obstructions, but nothing on their pages moves. The good news is that for the wave equation the answer is forced and computable at integer scale: a plucked string's kink splits into two half-height tents whose corners ride straight rays at speed \(c\), and the split can be checked cell by cell. The bad news is that the full theorem — Hörmander's propagation of singularities — is proved with pseudo-differential operators, a positive-commutator estimate, and Egorov's conjugation theorem. This page's honesty policy: state the general theorem as a tagged sketch with every gap named, and earn everything else by exact counting, at sizes where every number is an integer.

2 · Meet the problem

A plucked string, on a lattice you can audit cell by cell. A loop of \(96\) cells. The pluck is a tent: height \(32\) (half-units) at cell \(48\), sloping down one unit per cell to zero at cells \(32\) and \(64\) — a corner at the apex, two more at the feet. The string obeys the discrete wave equation at the unit step (the lattice's fastest honest speed, CFL \(= 1\)): \[ u^{n+1}_j = 2u^n_j - u^{n-1}_j + u^n_{j+1} - 2u^n_j + u^n_{j-1}, \] and the pluck is released from rest: no initial velocity.

Before any forward stepping, the bookkeeping. At CFL \(= 1\) the scheme has an exact general solution in integers: \[ u^n_j = F(j+n) + G(j-n), \] one shape traveling left, one traveling right, each moving exactly one cell per step. Verify it cold: \(361\) cells, \(40\) steps, pseudo-random integer waves, the scheme against the identity — the drift is not small, it is exactly \(0\). Released from rest, \(F = G\): the pluck splits into two half-height tents. And at step \(20\) the corner census is forced, not observed: feet at \(12\) and \(44\) with peak \(16\) at \(28\); feet at \(52\) and \(84\) with peak \(16\) at \(68\); and dead flat — exactly zero — everywhere else. Six corners, \([12, 28, 44, 52, 68, 84]\). Nobody chose those positions; the equation and the pluck dictated them.

Three questions, none of them answerable by any single page before this one.

Q1 — where does the roughness go? Given only the equation and the initial kink, can the corner positions at step \(20\) be forced in advance — no forward stepping, pure prediction? And is the prediction stable: does the kink ever dim, halt, or wander?

Q2 — which directions does the roughness live in? The string's kink has an address in direction space. A step in \(x\) only, a step rotated \(45^\circ\), a point spike — three pieces of roughness, three direction censuses. What does each one look like, and what does the census say a "direction of roughness" even is?

Q3 — which equations move roughness, and which erase it? The wave equation moves it. Does every equation? Run the same pluck under the heat equation and watch; then ask what property of the equation — readable off a single function, its principal symbol — decides the verdict.

The answers, in short. The corners are forced (Q1): they ride the characteristics \(j \pm n = 32, 48, 64\) — the rays through the pluck's own three corners — and after \(200\) steps the kink's sharpness still reads half the original in each tent: split, not decayed. The directions are forced (Q2): the axis and cone shares read \(1.0000000000000000\), to sixteen digits. And the equations split into three verdicts (Q3): hyperbolic moves roughness along rays, parabolic melts it, elliptic erases it — the difference is the symbol's zero set. The rest of the page is those three answers, earned honestly.

3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P52 — Microlocal propagation of singularities. Three steps, each a question with a forced answer. (1) Address the roughness. Give every piece of roughness its full address: a location, and the frequency-directions in which a windowed shelf census there refuses to die — the wavefront set \(WF\). A corner's address is (its location, the conormal direction); a spike's is (its location, every direction). (2) Ask the equation for its rays. Strip the equation to its principal symbol \(p(x, \tau, \xi)\) — the top-order part, read on address space — and solve Hamilton's equations on the set \(p = 0\): the bicharacteristics, or rays. Where \(p \neq 0\) there are no rays to ride: those directions are elliptic. (3) Read the verdict. In elliptic directions the solution is smooth — roughness there is erased. In the characteristic set, \(WF(u)\) is a union of full ray segments: roughness travels along the bicharacteristic flow at the flow's speed, and what arrives at an address must have been sent there. Nothing halts, nothing strays, nothing appears unsent.

Or, in words: don't ask where the roughness is; ask for its address, then ask the equation to draw the rays through address space. P33 counted obstructions where they stood; this page lets the count ride. The move's strength is what it refuses to promise: it never says how much roughness travels (that is amplitude — a separate account), and it never draws rays where the equation has none (that is the elliptic wall — the smoothing verdict). What it promises is exactly the traffic pattern — sent, riding, arriving — and the promise is read off two objects, a census and a symbol, both checkable at sizes where every number is an integer.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the address must be directional. Location alone is too coarse: the point spike and the \(x\)-step are both rough, but their direction censuses are opposites — every direction against exactly one. Jam-mode: a location-only census predicts roughness in every direction for every rough point, the spike's verdict applied to corners, and every prediction downstream turns to mush.

Key 2 — the equation must have rays. The symbol's zero set must be more than the origin for travel to be possible. Jam-mode: elliptic equations have empty zero sets — the Laplacian's symbol \(\xi^2 + \eta^2\) never vanishes on the census circle — so there are no rays, the verdict is universal smoothing, and the move has nothing to move (Exhibit A).

Key 3 — the rays must stay apart. The verdict reads one ray at a time, and it expires where rays converge to a focus. Jam-mode: caustics — at a focal point the ray traffic piles up without bound, amplitudes outgrow the ray picture, and the address book needs more than rays (Exhibit B).

Box P52.A — the theorem's engine, in miniature. For the wave equation \(u_{tt} - c^2 u_{xx} = 0\), the principal symbol is \(p = \tau^2 - c^2\xi^2\), and Hamilton's equations read \(\dot t = 2\tau\), \(\dot x = -2c^2\xi\), with \(\tau\) and \(\xi\) conserved (the symbol involves neither \(t\) nor \(x\)): straight rays at speed \(|\dot x / \dot t| = c\). The general theorem says the same thing about \(WF(u)\) for a wide class of equations: the wavefront set of a solution is invariant under the bicharacteristic flow of the principal symbol. The proof's engine, named but not built here: window address space with pseudo-differential localizers \(b(x, D)\); along a ray segment, build an operator whose commutator with the equation is positive — a monotone quantity that forbids roughness from entering the window unsent (the positive-commutator estimate); and keep the bookkeeping honest as the window is carried along the flow, which is Egorov's conjugation theorem — conjugation by the flow transports symbols along the rays. Each of the three is a door in §6's literature; none is proved on this page.

Box P52.B — where the move appears. Elliptic regularity: where an equation is elliptic — no rays — its solutions are smooth there; the workhorse fact behind harmonic and analytic PDE arguments. Geometrical optics: Hadamard's program — waves as sums of ray-carried phases; the modern address book is the ray diagram made into a theorem. Propagation at full scale: the Duistermaat–Hörmander theorem moves the wavefront sets of solutions of general pseudo-differential equations along the bicharacteristic flow — this page's lattice and census verdicts are its integer-scale shadow. Ray tracing through media: Snell's law, total internal reflection, and lens design are the ray verdict for symbols with layers — §4's two-layer string runs the whole arc, from bending to the closing door. At the research frontier: eigenfunction concentration, scattering theory, and microlocal sheaf methods all open with the same two questions — where is the roughness, and what are the rays.

4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — move or melt. Put the pluck on a \(600\)-cell loop and step the wave scheme \(200\) times. The kink's sharpness — the largest corner bend, \(\max_j |u_{j+1} - 2u_j + u_{j-1}|\) — starts at \(4\), and after \(200\) steps reads \(2\) in each traveling half: \(4 = 2 + 2\), split and not decayed. Sharper still is the riding count: at step \(30\), the left half sits on cells \([254, 286]\) and the right half on \([314, 346]\), each is the original tent's profile exactly (peak \(16\), half the pluck), and every other cell of the loop is exactly \(0\). Under the wave equation, roughness does not fade; it relocates, bit-for-bit.

Now the same pluck under the heat scheme, \(200\) steps: the sharpness reads \(2.88 \times 10^{-2}\) of the original — melted. The shelf stories of P42 say why, in three registers. The step's shelves hold steady, octave by octave (\(1.031, 1.019, 1.046, 1.202\); its coefficients obey \(k|c_k| \to 1/\pi\)); the kink's shelves halve per octave (\(0.287, 0.407, 0.486\), closing on \(1/2\) — exactly the halving its known series \(c_{2m} = 2/(\pi(4m^2-1))\) demands, within the \(3.5\%\) sampling band); the smooth bump's shelves collapse outright (\(8.45 \times 10^{-1}, 1.25 \times 10^{-1}, 4.30 \times 10^{-4}, 8.24 \times 10^{-10}\), with the band-limited limit case machine-zero — \(2.11 \times 10^{-15}\) — above \(k = 8\)). Under the wave equation, no shelf is touched: the split halves carry the original tent's profile, so the kink's halving signature rides on. Under the heat equation, every shelf is damped, and the damping grows with \(k\) — the melt is fastest exactly where the roughness lives. Move or melt is decided per octave, and the top shelves go first.

Encounter 2 — the direction census. Roughness has direction, and the direction is countable. Take a \(64 \times 64\) grid and run the two-dimensional frequency census (the energy in each cell \((k_x, k_y)\) of the spectrum):

  • A step in \(x\) only — a jump across the line \(x = 32\), constant in \(y\). The energy sits entirely on the \(k_y = 0\) axis: the axis share is \(1.0000000000000000\), to sixteen digits. The step's direction of roughness is the frequency direction across the jump — the conormal.
  • A step rotated \(45^\circ\) — the torus step \((x + y) \bmod 64 < 32\), a jump across the diagonal. The energy sits entirely on the diagonal cone: share again \(1.0000000000000000\). Rotate the jump, and the census rotates with it — the conormal of the diagonal line.
  • A point spike — one nonzero cell. All \(4096\) spectrum magnitudes are equal, \(1/4096\) each (\(2.44 \times 10^{-4}\)): roughness in every direction, no preference.

So far the jumps run the whole width, and their direction is exact. The last census is the hinge of the whole page: window the step — multiply it by a bump of width \(\sigma = 2\) at the jump line — and direction becomes a limiting question. Octave by octave, the axis share reads \(0.206, 0.223, 0.381, 0.719\): the low octaves are smeared (the window is small, and the census sees mostly window), and each higher octave sharpens toward the conormal. That limit — the directions where the shelves refuse to die as the window tightens and the frequency climbs — is the wavefront set, and here it is watched happening: direction is not a metaphor but a limiting share, and it climbs to the conormal every time.

Encounter 3 — the rays. The address book's second half is drawn by the equation. For \(p = \tau^2 - \xi^2\), Hamilton's equations integrate to straight rays: ten ray families, stepped by a fourth-order runner, hold their invariants to \(8.88 \times 10^{-14}\), and the speed reads \(|dx/dt| = 1\) exactly (\(8.22 \times 10^{-15}\) worst deviation). In two space dimensions, \(p = \tau^2 - (\xi^2 + \eta^2)\): launch \(32\) travelers from one point, one per direction, and every one sits at \(|x| = ct\) when the clock reads \(t\) — the light cone, \(1.08 \times 10^{-14}\) worst deviation across the whole fan.

Layers bend the rays without breaking the bookkeeping. A two-layer string — \(c = 1\) on the left, \(c = 1/2\) on the right — meets a ray at \(60^\circ\) from the fast side: \(\sin\theta_1 = 0.866025404\), \(\sin\theta_2 = 0.433012702\), and the quotient \(\sin\theta/c\) is conserved across the interface (\(0.866025404\) on both sides) — the ray bends toward the normal in the slow layer, exactly as the invariant dictates. Launched from \(x = -3\), the ray crosses the interface at \((0, 5.196152)\), which is \(3\tan 60^\circ\). And the invariant closes a door: from the slow side at \(\sin\theta_1 = 0.8\), past the critical value \(1/2\), the transmitted ray would need a sine above \(1\) — the discriminant reads \(-1.56\) — so nothing crosses: total internal reflection, the address bounced back. Snell's law is the ray verdict for a symbol in layers; the lens-maker's diagrams are this census at industrial scale.

SKETCH — the propagation theorem, honestly incomplete. What this page earns by counting is the integer-scale verdict: the exact lattice identity, the forced corner census, the direction shares, the ray invariants, the Snell arc. The general theorem — for solutions of pseudo-differential equations, \(WF(u)\) is invariant under the bicharacteristic flow — rests on three pieces of machinery this page names and does not build: (i) pseudo-differential operators, the localizers that let a window sit at one address, location and direction at once — the finite censuses of §4 are their brute-force stand-ins; (ii) the positive-commutator estimate, the monotone quantity that forbids unsent arrivals along a ray segment; (iii) Egorov's theorem, which keeps the symbol readable as the flow carries the window. The sketch is a promise about where the theorem's engine lives, and an honest note of what stops short: nothing on this page proves the general case.

5 · When the trick breaks

Every pattern page owes an honest list of failure modes. The propagation move fails in three distinct ways, and each has a repair, a price, and a default.

Exhibit A — the elliptic wall. The Laplacian's symbol \(\xi^2 + \eta^2\) vanishes only at the origin: the zero-set census over \(720\) directions finds the wave's symbol vanishing in \(4\) directions and the Laplacian's in none — its census minimum is \(1.000000\). No zero set, no rays; no rays, nothing to ride. The smoothing is structural, not a bonus: in frequency space the periodic Laplacian multiplies every shelf by its symbol — exactly, to \(1.50 \times 10^{-13}\) over the whole spectrum — and the symbol grows like \(k^2\) (within \(6\%\) for \(k = 2\) through \(8\); worst deviation \(0.0504\)). To solve \(\Delta u = f\) is to divide each shelf by its symbol: the high shelves collapse, and the roughness of \(f\) cannot survive into \(u\). The ladder runs in exact integers — \(u'' = f\) with \(f\) the step is solved by two integrations, \(u_1\), with \(\Delta^2 u_1[j] = f[j-2]\) cell by cell, and one more integration solves \(u'' = u_1\) the same way: each integration smooths one order. Averaging says the same thing without symbols: \(500\) Jacobi sweeps on a rough \(18 \times 18\) board leave \(2.1833 \times 10^{-10}\) of the interior's Laplacian energy. The wall is a fact about the symbol, and the wall wins.

Mitigation: use the wall — elliptic equations are the smoothers of the trade (Poisson solves, harmonic extensions, and every "the solution is smoother than the data" theorem in sight).

Price: ellipticity is all-or-nothing per direction. If the symbol vanishes somewhere — degenerate or mixed-type equations — the wall has gaps, and the verdict must be read gap by gap.

Default: before promising smoothness, run the zero-set census on the symbol; the count of vanishing directions is the count of escape routes.

Exhibit B — caustics, where the rays converge. The ray verdict reads one ray at a time, and it quietly assumes the rays stay spread out. A family of parallel rays entering §4's slow layer refracts into a converging fan — and where a fan of rays converges, the ray picture breaks: that place is a caustic. At the focus the ray count per area grows without bound; amplitudes — which the move never sizes — outgrow the ray approximation; and past the focus the ray drawing imposes a shadow boundary that the true solution only respects approximately. The honest repair is a different expansion near the caustic (Airy-type uniform approximations — named, and deferred to the literature door): the focus is smoothed over a boundary layer, and the ray verdict resumes on the far side.

Mitigation: stop the ray verdict at the caustic and re-expand — treat the focus as a new boundary, not a prediction.

Price: near the caustic the address book needs more than rays — oscillatory corrections — so the price of the ray picture's speed is a known blind spot at every focus.

Default: when ray spacing shrinks toward zero, stop trusting the rays; the spacing census is the early-warning signal.

Exhibit C — what the move never does. The propagation theorem locates roughness; it never sizes it. The verdict says the corner rides the ray through cell \(48\); it does not say the corner is tall — that its height is \(16\), half the pluck, is a separate fact with a separate account (amplitudes ride the rays too, but under transport equations: named here, deferred there). P42 made the same confession for the census — shelf traffic is counted, not valued. The pairing matters in practice: two solutions can share a wavefront set and behave utterly differently, one with tame amplitudes, one howling; and any argument that reads "the roughness travels here" as "the danger travels at this size" has confused the address with the account.

Mitigation: when sizes matter, run the transport account after the address account — they are separate theorems with separate machinery.

Price: the address verdict alone cannot rank dangers; it is a map, not a forecast.

Default: state propagation claims as where, never as how much.

Scope, honestly. The lattice identity is exact at CFL \(= 1\) with integer data; the melt, the Jacobi count, and the bit-for-bit rides are counts under two specific schemes; the direction censuses are \(64 \times 64\); the ray runs step Hamilton's equations for the constant and two-layer wave symbol. The general propagation theorem is stated as a tagged sketch: pseudo-differential operators, the positive-commutator argument, and Egorov's theorem are named gaps with their doors in §6. Nothing on this page proves Hörmander's theorem; what it settles by exact counting is the identity (zero drift, \(361\) cells, \(40\) steps), the forced corner census, the direction shares (\(1.0000000000000000\), twice), the ray verdicts (speed, cone, Snell, total internal reflection), and the elliptic contrast (symbol multiplication exact to \(1.50 \times 10^{-13}\), zero sets counted, energy erased to \(2.1833 \times 10^{-10}\)).

The move is a hub: once roughness has an address and equations draw rays, several neighboring patterns become readable as the same bookkeeping in different clothes.

  • Door — deformation cohomology (P53): the nearest door, and this page's footer. Where this page sends addresses along rays, P53 sends counting through parameter space, with the same cochain bookkeeping keeping the tally.
  • Door — stable compactification (P54) and Floer homology (P65): the other two customers in P33's waiting room. Both count obstructions at research scale, and both consume the address habit this page formalized — one at the boundary a family was missing, one along strips between Lagrangians.
  • Door — rescale for asymptotic geometry (P56): the other way to handle scale. This page resolves scale into shelves and directions; P56 zooms the whole problem until its shape stabilizes. The two moves trade: when the census is too coarse, rescale; when rescaling blurs the address, census.
  • Door — sheafify on a site (P62): the address book itself can be treated as gluing data. The microlocal branch of sheaf theory builds a category whose objects carry the wavefront bookkeeping structurally; the door is where this page's census habits meet P33's gluing habits at full generality.
  • Door — the literature: Hörmander's four-volume Analysis of Linear Partial Differential Operators is the standing corpus (honored with the 2006 Steele Prize); the Duistermaat–Hörmander paper of 1972 (Acta Mathematica 128) is where the propagation theorem lives at full scale; Egorov's 1969 note on canonical transformations (Uspekhi Mat. 7 · Lineage

The move's content is as old as ray diagrams; its proof is twentieth-century; its working life is now.

  • 1831 — Hamilton. The optico-mechanical analogy: the same equations that draw light's rays draw mechanics' paths, rays and wavefronts two readings of one flow. The bicharacteristics of this page are Hamilton's curves with a new job — carrying roughness, not light.
  • 1923 — Hadamard. Lectures on Cauchy's Problem in the Linear Partial Differential Equations (the Yale lectures): the method of characteristics for hyperbolic equations, and the parametrix — the approximate solution built from ray data — which is named for him. §4's ray verdicts are Hadamard's program run on integers.
  • Mid-1960s — the calculus. Pseudo-differential operators are codified (the work of Kohn, Nirenberg, and Hörmander among others): differential equations become functions on address space — symbols — and the bookkeeping of windows and shelves gets its algebra.
  • 1969 — Egorov. "The canonical transformations of pseudodifferential operators" (Uspekhi Mat. Nauk 24 (5)): conjugation by a canonical transformation transports a pseudo-differential operator's symbol along the flow — the commuting square that lets the propagation proof carry its windows without losing its bookkeeping.
  • 1972 — Duistermaat–Hörmander. Fourier integral operators, II (Acta Mathematica 128): the propagation of singularities along the bicharacteristic flow of the principal symbol, stated at full generality — what arrives must have been sent, as a theorem about wavefront sets.
  • The corpus. Hörmander's four-volume Analysis of Linear Partial Differential Operators is the standing reference where the wavefront set, the symbol calculus, and the propagation theorem live in final form; the 2006 Steele Prize honored the corpus as a whole.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the counts above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Three shelf stories, sorted. Three functions on the \(128\)-point circle were censused octave by octave (each shelf is the sum of magnitudes in one dyadic band). (a) Shelf ratios \(1.031, 1.019, 1.046, 1.202\). (b) Shelf ratios \(0.287, 0.407, 0.486\). (c) Shelf ratios \(8.45 \times 10^{-1}, 1.25 \times 10^{-1}, 4.30 \times 10^{-4}, 8.24 \times 10^{-10}\). Match (a), (b), (c) to the step, the kink \(|\sin|\), and the smooth bump; give the rule you used; then say which of the three the heat equation must work hardest to melt — and why the wave equation at CFL \(= 1\) melts none of them.

Hint

Odd–even structure first: one of the three has machine-zero even coefficients, another machine-zero odd coefficients. Then sizes: whose coefficients follow a known closed series, and whose simply die?

Worked answer

(a) is the step: even coefficients machine-zero, odd coefficients obeying \(k|c_k| \to 1/\pi\) (deviations \(3.20 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(k = 1\), \(2.88 \times 10^{-4}\) at \(k = 3\)) — so every octave's shelf is roughly the last: ratios near \(1\). (b) is the kink: odd coefficients machine-zero, even ones the known series \(c_{2m} = 2/(\pi(4m^2-1))\) (first value \(c_2 = 0.212334\), all within the \(3.5\%\) sampling band) — halving shelves, ratios closing on \(1/2\). (c) is the smooth bump: super-exponential collapse, with the band-limited limit case machine-zero (\(2.11 \times 10^{-15}\)) above \(k = 8\). Heat melts roughness where the shelves are stocked, and its damping grows with \(k\): the step's shelves are stocked widest and highest, so the grinding is most dramatic there — the step is the one it must work hardest on; the kink's halving shelves melt milder; the bump had no roughness to melt in the first place. The wave equation at CFL \(= 1\) multiplies no shelf by anything: the traveling halves at step \(30\) are the original profile bit-for-bit, so none of the three stories changes at all.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The identity, derived and ridden. (a) Show that \(u^n_j = F(j+n) + G(j-n)\) satisfies the CFL \(= 1\) scheme exactly, for any \(F\) and \(G\). (b) The rest start — \(u^1_j = (u^0_{j-1} + u^0_{j+1})/2\), the scheme's "no initial velocity" — is exactly what \(F = G = u_0/2\) produces; conclude that a pluck released from rest splits into two half-height tents. (c) Predict the corner census at step \(20\) from the characteristics \(j \pm n = 32, 48, 64\) alone, and check against \([12, 28, 44, 52, 68, 84]\). (d) Hand-run three steps of the scheme on a few cells near the apex, and verify each value against the identity.

Hint

For (a), compute the second difference in time and in space of one traveling piece, and compare. For (c), each corner of the tent sits on a cell at \(t = 0\); ask which cells that corner's two characteristics reach at \(t = 20\).

Worked answer

(a) For a left-mover \(L(j+n)\): the second time difference is \(L(j+n+1) - 2L(j+n) + L(j+n-1)\), and so is the second space difference — the scheme holds for \(L\) alone, and likewise for the right-mover, so it holds for the sum. (b) With \(F = G = u_0/2\): \(u^0_j = (u_0(j) + u_0(j))/2 = u_0(j)\) ✓, and \(u^1_j = (u_0(j+1) + u_0(j-1))/2\) ✓ — the rest start; since the scheme determines every later row from these two, the identity is the evolution, and the pluck is two half-height copies of itself, one per direction. (c) The tent's corners sit at cells \(32, 48, 64\); their characteristics are \(j = 32 \pm n\), \(j = 48 \pm n\), \(j = 64 \pm n\); at \(n = 20\): \(12, 52\); \(28, 68\); \(44, 84\) — sorted, \([12, 28, 44, 52, 68, 84]\), the §2 census. (d) At the apex cell \(48\): step \(0\) reads \(32\) (half-units), and step \(1\) reads \(u_0(49) + u_0(47) = 15 + 15 = 30\) — the height dips immediately, because the two half-peaks are already one cell apart, separating one cell per step each way; every hand-run value matches \(u_0(j+n) + u_0(j-n)\) exactly, which is the point: the identity is the audit.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The rotated step, predicted then censused. (a) Predict: for the torus step \((x + y) \bmod 64 < 32\), which frequency directions carry the energy? (b) The census puts the cone share at \(1.0000000000000000\) — explain the exactness. (c) Window the step with a bump of width \(\sigma = 2\): predict the octave-by-octave behavior of the cone share, and check against \(0.206, 0.223, 0.381, 0.719\). (d) State the wavefront set of the unwindowed rotated step.

Hint

Rotate the whole picture: what does the \(x\)-step's census look like after a \(45^\circ\) turn of the axes? For the windowed version, ask which frequencies can see the jump from far away, and which see only the bump.

Worked answer

(a) The jump runs across lines \(x + y \equiv 0 \pmod{64}\); its conormal is the \((1,1)\) frequency direction — energy on the lines \(k_x = k_y\). (b) The torus step is a step in the rotated coordinate \(s = (x + y) \bmod 64\), constant along the \((1,-1)\) fibers; the census is the one-dimensional step spectrum planted on the diagonal: each line \(k_x = k_y\) carries a one-dimensional step spectrum (whose shelves hold, per Encounter 1), and every off-diagonal line carries exactly zero — hence share \(1\), to the last digit, not merely close. (c) The window makes direction a limiting question: the low octaves see a small bump (nearly isotropic, cone share \(0.206\)), and each octave sharpens toward the conormal (\(0.223, 0.381, 0.719\)) — the census in action as a limit. (d) \(WF = \{(\text{location on the jump lines}, \text{conormal } (1,1)/\sqrt{2})\}\): one location set, one direction, the whole roughness.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Three students, three claims, no safety net. Diagnose each claim — true, false, or true only under a condition — and cite the exact verdict of this page that settles it. (a) "With a clever enough initial velocity, a kink on the string can be made to stand still: the roughness stops traveling." (b) "Pluck the string off-center, at cell 20, and the two half-tents come out uneven — the side with less room gets more height." (c) "Two wave packets can interfere and create a brand-new corner mid-string, in a zone that was perfectly smooth, where nothing was sent."

Worked answer

(a) False. The rays are forced by the symbol: \(\tau\) and \(\xi\) are conserved along a bicharacteristic (invariants held to \(8.88 \times 10^{-14}\) over ten families), and every address travels at \(|dx/dt| = 1\), exactly. A nonzero corner cannot halt; the only "standing" patterns are sums of counter-traveling smooth waves (the cosine superposition), and they have no corners to stand. (b) False, for a pluck released from rest: the rest start forces \(F = G = u_0/2\) wherever the tent is centered — centering at cell \(20\) moves where the two halves land (peaks at \(20 \pm n\)), but the heights stay equal, half the pluck each. Unequal halves require \(F \neq G\): a nonzero initial velocity, a different pluck, a different question. (c) False, and the falsity is the theorem's very statement: \(WF(u)\) is a union of full ray segments — what arrives must have been sent. On the lattice: at every step the value at any cell is \(F(j+n) + G(j-n)\), a sum of two translated copies of the initial data; a corner of a sum of piecewise-linear pieces can only sit at a corner of one of the pieces, so the corner census is contained in the sent corners at every step — overlap, crossing, and interference never mint a new address. All three claims fail against the same clause: the equation draws the rays, and the rays carry all the roughness there is.

Exercise 5 ◐ — The two-layer string, open end. A string with wave speed \(c = 1\) on the left and \(c = 1/2\) on the right (denser material); rays bend at the interface, and the conserved quantity is \(\sin\theta/c\). (a) Verify the §4 arc: \(60^\circ\) from the fast side gives \(\sin\theta_1 = 0.866025404\) and \(\sin\theta_2 = 0.433012702\); a ray launched from \(x = -3\) crosses at \((0, 5.196152)\). (b) Show the door closes from the other side: at \(\sin\theta_1 = 0.8\) in the slow layer, the transmitted discriminant reads \(-1.56\) and the ray reflects totally. (c) Open end: a family of parallel rays from the fast side refracts into a converging fan. Sketch where the fan focuses — a caustic — and say what the ray verdict can and cannot tell you near the focus, per Exhibits B and C.

Hint

For (a), the horizontal speed is \(0.5\) (the \(x\)-component of velocity at \(60^\circ\)), so the interface takes time \(6\). For (c), draw three refracted rays and extend them; the crossings mark the fan's focus.

Worked answer

(a) The interface cannot change \(\tau\) or \(\eta\), so \(\sin\theta/c = |\eta|/\tau\) is conserved: from the fast side, \(\sin\theta_2 = \sin\theta_1 \cdot \frac{1/2}{1} = 0.433012702\), bending toward the normal. From \(x = -3\): the horizontal speed is \(\cos 60^\circ = 0.5\), the interface is \(6\) time units away, and the crossing sits at \(y = \sin 60^\circ \cdot 6 = 5.196152 = 3\tan 60^\circ\). (b) From the slow side, transmission into \(c = 1\) demands \(\sin\theta_2 = 2\sin\theta_1 = 1.6 > 1\); in symbols the transmitted \(\xi^2 = \tau^2/c_2^2 - \eta^2 = 1 - 2.56 = -1.56 < 0\): no real ray crosses, and the address bounces — total internal reflection, the door of §4's census closed. (c) Open: the refracted rays all bend by different amounts, and a parallel band (say \(55^\circ\)–\(65^\circ\)) crosses itself a finite distance into the slow layer — the caustic. Near it, ray spacing shrinks toward zero and the ray verdict expires: amplitudes outgrow the ray picture (Exhibit B), while the traffic pattern — that roughness arrives along these rays — still holds; the sizes near the focus are exactly what the move never promises (Exhibit C). The honest prescription: re-expand near the caustic (Airy-type, deferred), resume the ray verdict beyond it, and compute the focus's position by hand for a narrow band as the open end's concrete goal.
