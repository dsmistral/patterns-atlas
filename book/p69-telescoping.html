The Patterns Atlas · P69 · the eleventh move of tier 2

Telescoping

A hundred square roots sit on the page — \(1 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} + \cdots + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{100}}\) — and the question sounds almost insulting: is the total bigger or smaller than \(10\)? Adding it honestly is an afternoon, guessing is a coin toss, and the difference of two square roots looks like the last thing on earth that could help. But \(\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}}\) turns out to be a near-step: every term of the sum sits sandwiched between one such step and two of them, the sandwich is all the theorem needs, and when the steps are summed the middle dies and the survivors are \(\sqrt{n}\) and \(2\sqrt{n}\) — the hundred-term question answered without one addition. B11 built the machine: recognize a term as a step, check the shift, keep the survivors. This page teaches the reach — the diagnosis that fires when nothing is already a step. When the sum hides inside an inequality, the staircase has to be designed, and the arms of the cage are engineered steps. When the object is a product, the steps are ratios, and the middle cancels in pairs. When the sum is not even wearing its own clothes — when it is disguised as an identity in Pascal's triangle — recognition is the whole craft, and the telescope turns out to have been sitting in a table you already own. Runners: the cage of the square-root sum, with both arms built from the discrete derivative \(\Delta f(k) = f(k{+}1) - f(k)\) of one staircase; the product \(\prod_{k=2}^{n} \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\bigr)\) collapsing to \(\tfrac{n{+}1}{2n}\); and the hockey-stick identity \(\sum_{k=r}^{n} \binom{k}{r} = \binom{n{+}1}{r{+}1}\), unmasked as a telescope in counting clothes. Prerequisites: P02 (the census habit — every survivors-claim pays it), B11 (the discrete derivative, the telescoping theorem, the anti-difference machine and its three obligations — this page is the consumer B11 was built for; P66's ladder and P15's squeeze stand behind it). Next: the ledger leaves the number line — B09 · Graphs and numbers on them.

What this page is. This is a pattern page: it owns a move, not a concept. The concept — the step, the staircase, the theorem that the middle dies — belongs to B11, and this page will lean on B11's three obligations (recognize by one subtraction, check the shift, test the survivors) without restating their proofs. What B11 could not teach is when to reach for the machine, because B11's sums arrived with their staircases half-announced: a term like \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\) is visibly a factored difference. In the wild, telescopes arrive disguised — as inequalities, as products, as identities about other objects — and the disguise is the subject here. Three new words do the heavy lifting. New word: near-step. A term \(g(k)\) that is not itself a step but is trapped between (multiples of) consecutive steps of one staircase: \(\Delta f(k{-}1) \le g(k) \le c \, \Delta f(k{-}1)\) for a constant \(c\). Sums of near-steps cannot be evaluated by the telescoping theorem — but they can be caged by it, and the cage is often exactly what an estimate asked for. New word: product telescope. A product whose factors are ratios of one function at shifted arguments, \(\prod \tfrac{g(k{+}1)}{g(k)}\): the middle cancels pairwise exactly as a sum of steps does, because a product of ratios is one big cancellation waiting to happen — each numerator kills a denominator one or two factors away. New word: telescoping certificate. The staircase \(f\) itself, handed over with the claim: whoever doubts the collapse checks \(\Delta f \overset{?}{=} g\) by one subtraction and is convinced. The word is the modern literature's own (§7 tells where it came from), and it names the right picture: a telescoping claim is easy to verify and hard to find — exactly the shape of a certificate. You already own every tool on this page: the census from P02, the machine from B11, the squeeze from P15, the ladder from P66. Take a pencil: you are about to sentence a hundred additions to death, watch a product eat itself, and catch a telescope hiding in the world's most famous triangle.

1 · Before we begin

The reader arrives owning a machine and its rulebook. From B11: the discrete derivative \(\Delta f(k) = f(k{+}1) - f(k)\), the telescoping theorem — a sum of consecutive steps keeps only its two ends, \(\sum_{k=m}^{n} \Delta f(k) = f(n{+}1) - f(m)\) — and the anti-difference machine with its three obligations: recognize the term as a step by one subtraction, never memory; check the shift before trusting the ends; test the survivors against a row you can add by hand. From P02: the census habit that powers the third obligation. From P66, one structural fact worth carrying: the induction ladder is a telescope walked one rung at a time — base plus steps equals ends — so every ladder this page meets is already half-known. From P15: the design of the trap — an answer caged between two computable things. And from P74, a debt in the other direction: its loads — the rung \(2 - \tfrac{1}{n}\), Sylvester's exact remainder — were designed so their differences telescope, which is this page's move used backwards, at proof-strength; P74 pointed here twice, and this page is the promise kept.

Here is the tension this page resolves. B11's machine answers one question — whose step is this? — and B11's examples were kind: the terms were factored differences, staircase sums, geometric series, shapes that practically introduce themselves. But the question the machine was built for arrives in the wild wearing three disguises, and the disguises are the pattern's whole content. Disguise one: no sum at all. The problem asks for an inequality — "show this is bigger than that" — and the sum to be telescoped is one you must build yourself, as the arms of a cage. Disguise two: not a sum. The object is a product, or a power, or a nested chain — and the telescope runs on ratios instead of differences. Disguise three: no term at all. The sum is invisible inside an identity somebody else already proved for other reasons, and the move is the recognition — "this famous fact is a telescope wearing clothes." The three disguises structure everything below: §4 works one of each, in public.

The runner for the whole page is the hundred square roots of the opening. We will not evaluate \(\sum_{k=1}^{100} \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\) — no closed form exists in this book's toolkit, and §5 says so honestly. We will do something better than evaluating it: we will prove, in two lines of algebra, that it lies between \(10\) and \(20\); then, by reading the same staircase from the other side, push the lower arm from \(10\) to \(18.0998\) and change the honest sentence from "between 10 and 20" to "between \(18.0998\) and \(20\)" — a window \(1.90\) wide for a hundred-term sum, bought with zero additions. The machinery that does this is B11's, verbatim; what is new is the diagnosis that aimed it.

2 · A hundred square roots and a suspicious ten

Here is the problem, exactly as a problem-set would print it: let \(S_n = 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} + \cdots + \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\). Is \(S_{100}\) bigger or smaller than \(10\)? No closed form for \(S_n\) is on offer; nobody is going to add a hundred irrational numbers by hand; and the question demands a verdict, not a value. This is the shape problems actually arrive in — an estimate about a sum that cannot be evaluated — and it is the shape the rest of this page exists for.

P02's reflex first: a census, before any theory. Compute the small sums and set them against the only reasonable measuring stick in sight, \(\sqrt{n}\) itself:

\(n\)\(S_n\)\(\sqrt{n}\)\(S_n / \sqrt{n}\)

\(4\)\(2.7845\)\(2\)\(1.3922\)
\(9\)\(4.7048\)\(3\)\(1.5683\)
\(16\)\(6.6640\)\(4\)\(1.6660\)
\(100\)\(18.5896\)\(10\)\(1.8590\)

The table says two things at once. The sum beats \(\sqrt{n}\) — by a margin that itself grows — so the answer to the printed question is bigger, almost surely. And the last column is the tell: \(S_n/\sqrt{n}\) creeps upward, row after row, toward something near \(2\). A conjecture takes shape, the way P02's conjectures always do: \(\sqrt{n}\) sits under \(S_n\), and something like \(2\sqrt{n}\) sits over it. Two arms; a cage; P15's shape. But a census is a detector, not a proof, and a hundred rows would not close the gap between "almost surely" and "proved". What would even be a proof? No term of \(S_n\) factors helpfully; nothing looks like \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\); the anti-difference machine of B11 has no table row for \(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\).

The move that breaks the problem open is a P02 move made on the guess instead of the sum. The arms \(\sqrt{n}\) and \(2\sqrt{n}\) came from the table; so take the candidate staircase \(f(k) = \sqrt{k}\) and compute its steps — the discrete derivative of B11, \(\Delta f(k{-}1) = f(k) - f(k{-}1)\) — and lay them beside the terms:

The near-step census. Each term of the sum sits between one step and two steps of the staircase \(f(k) = \sqrt{k}\): the step's denominator \(\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}\) straddles \(\sqrt{k}\), one square root below it and one above it.

Read the middle row and the mechanism is on the table. The step is a near-miss of the term, and the miss has a reason: \(\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}}\), and the step's denominator \(\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}\) is caught between \(\sqrt{k}\) and \(2\sqrt{k}\) —

\[\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k}}}_{=\;\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}} \;\;\le\;\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}}}_{=\;\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1}} \;\;\le\;\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{k} + 0}}_{=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}} \;\;\le\;\; \underbrace{\frac{2}{\sqrt{k} + \sqrt{k{-}1}}}_{=\;2\bigl(\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1}\bigr)},\]

and the chain says exactly what the census showed, with the arithmetic reason attached: the step is never below half the term and never above the term — and the term is never above two steps. Now let B11's theorem do the only thing it knows how to do — sum consecutive steps and keep the ends:

\[\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1}\bigr) \;=\; \sqrt{n} - \sqrt{0} \;=\; \sqrt{n}, \qquad\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{n} 2\bigl(\sqrt{k} - \sqrt{k{-}1}\bigr) \;=\; 2\sqrt{n}.\]

Each term of \(S_n\) sits between the \(k\)-th step and twice the \(k\)-th step, so the whole sum sits between the sum of steps and the sum of double steps — and both of those sums collapse, because they are sums of consecutive steps of one staircase:

\[\sqrt{n} \;\;\le\;\; S_n \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \;\;\le\;\; 2\sqrt{n}.\]

The proof is complete, and it was two lines of algebra plus one theorem plus one table that found it. At \(n = 100\): \(10 \le S_{100} \le 20\), and the printed question is answered — bigger — with a proof that cost no additions. The machine-walk value \(18.5896\) in the first table agrees, and it agrees loudly: near the top of the window, because the lower arm is slack — §4 tightens it, and §8's second exercise pushes it almost all the way up.

What the pencil just showed. The sum \(S_n\) was never a telescope. It was trapped between two telescopes — two sums of steps of one staircase, engineered so the prisoner could not escape either side. Four questions demand answers before this is a tool and not a lucky day. What exactly was found? — a term that is not a step but sits between multiples of steps: a near-step, and it is a species, not an accident. How do you find the staircase? — the census produced the arms' shape (\(\sqrt{n}\) and \(2\sqrt{n}\) from the ratio column), and the staircase was read off the shape by differencing: the pattern's design step, to be made explicit. What can the trap honestly deliver? — a window, never a value; when the window must shrink, what are the design choices? And what happens when the object is not a sum at all — a product, an identity in someone else's table? The next section names the move; §4 answers all four questions with worked mathematics; §5 says what the move costs and where it dies.

3 · Name the move

P69 · Telescoping — when an iterated object (sum, product, chain) is, contains, or can be trapped between expressions built from the consecutive differences of one function, rewrite it so the middle cancels: the survivors are read off the two ends, or the prisoner is read off the cage

When the problem is of the form "an identity, inequality, estimate, or limit involving a chain over a range of \(k\)" — a sum \(\sum g(k)\), a product \(\prod g(k)\), a nested chain, or an estimate about one — and inspection or a small-cases census suggests the answer behaves like the values of one candidate function \(f\) at the ends of the range, then do not evaluate the chain term by term. Instead: find the staircase — compute the discrete derivative \(\Delta f(k) = f(k{+}1) - f(k)\) of the candidate and compare it with the term \(g(k)\); classify the match — exact (\(g = \Delta f\): a direct telescope), near (\(g\) between multiples of \(\Delta f\): a trap with telescoping arms), or shifted onto a lattice (\(g\) runs on every second value, a half-lattice: the same theorem one floor over); run the machine honestly — B11's three obligations, with the shift bookkeeping first and the survivors tested against a hand-computed row; or, for the trap, prove each arm by the one-line comparison that exhibits the middle cancellation. What the move buys: identities evaluated without addition, estimates proved without the value ever being found, and inequalities whose arms were designed rather than found — at the price of a recognition that can fail, and an answer that arrives as survivors or as a window, never as a term-by-term total.

In words: a chain that iterates over consecutive \(k\) is asking to be read as a staircase. If its terms are the steps of one staircase, the chain is a distance — read the two ends. If its terms merely straddle the steps of one staircase, the chain is a prisoner — read the cage. If its factors are ratios of one function at shifted arguments, the chain is a staircase on its side — read the two ends again, one product at a time. And if some other result already proved is secretly one of these three, the recognition alone is the proof. The pattern is a diagnosis first and a computation second: the whole craft lives in the question B11 taught — whose step is this? — asked of objects that do not arrive announcing the answer.

Why this is legal, and what it costs. The legality is B11's theorem, which assumed nothing about the staircase and is therefore available for every staircase a diagnosis can name — including staircases you designed yourself, which is exactly the freedom the trap design needs. The costs are real, and §5 prices all three. The recognition can fail: some terms have no staircase of any useful kind, and the failure is not shameful but informative — it is how \(\sum \tfrac{1}{k^2}\) announces that no closed form is coming (§5's second exhibit). The shift can lie: a wrong-by-one survivors-claim looks exactly like a right one, and only the census tells them apart. And the trap can outgrow its arms: a cage is two telescopes, and designing arms that both collapse and hug the prisoner is the craft's highest tariff — §4's first example pays it in full, and §8's second exercise shows the design space has more than one good answer.

The move's own obligations extend B11's three to the wild. Obligation A — diagnose the disguise before choosing the design: an identity wants a direct telescope; an estimate wants a trap; a product wants ratios; a foreign identity wants unmasking. Choosing the wrong design for the disguise is the beginner's whole cost. Obligation B — derive the staircase from data, not from hope: when no staircase is given, the census supplies the shape of the answer (§2's ratio column), the shape names a candidate \(f\), and the candidate is confirmed by differencing — the design step that made §2 work and that §4 makes explicit. Obligation C — test the survivors, then test the arms: B11's third obligation, doubled; every trap in this book is caught in the end by a row the reader can add by hand, and §5's exhibits are all cases where the row was not added.

Box P69.A · The playbook — five designs, one theorem

Every telescope this page uses is one of five designs. All five run on B11's theorem; they differ in what arrives and what has to be built. Learn the designs as designs: the recognition question is always "which one is this object wearing?"

Design 1 — the direct telescope (the term is a step). Given: a sum whose term is or factors into a difference. Move: find the staircase \(f\) with \(\Delta f = g\) — B11's starter kit covers the standard shapes, and the one-subtraction check confirms any candidate. Pay: the survivors, \(f(n{+}1) - f(m)\). §2's proof summed two of these; B11's section 3 is a gallery. This is the floor design — the other four are it in costume.

Design 2 — the trap (the term is a near-step). Given: an estimate, and no closed form for the sum. Move: census the small cases for the shape of the answer; name a candidate staircase from the shape; compute its steps \(\Delta f\); prove each arm by a one-line comparison — \(c_1\,\Delta f(k{-}1) \;\le\; g(k) \;\le\; c_2\,\Delta f(k{-}1)\) — and telescope both arms. Pay: a window, \(c_1 f(n) \le \sum g \le c_2 f(n)\), honest about its width and its boundary rows (where equality can occur). The arms must themselves be telescopes — that is the whole design constraint, and the reason the move coexists with P15: a squeeze only computes if both arms compute.

Design 3 — the product telescope (the factor is a ratio). Given: a product. Move: factor each factor into ratios of one function at shifted arguments, \(\prod_{k} \frac{g(k{+}1)}{g(k)}\); the middle cancels pairwise — every interior value of \(g\) appears once on top and once on the bottom — leaving \(\frac{g(n{+}1)}{g(m)}\). Products with factors like \(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\) split into two shifted ratio-chains at once. Pay: the two end-factors. A product is a sum in logarithm clothes — a reader with logarithms can take that literally — but the ratio telescope needs no logarithm: the cancellation is in the factors, not above them.

Design 4 — the found telescope (the sum is inside another identity). Given: an identity somebody proved for other reasons. Move: read the identity as a difference — ask whether its left side is \(\Delta f\) of a family one index over — and the sum appears with its survivors already attached. Pay: a familiar fact recognized as this move, and often a whole family of identities at once. §4's third example finds Pascal's hockey stick here; the found design is also where the lattice shift lives — a telescope can run on the odd numbers, the even numbers, or any evenly spaced lattice, because B11's theorem asks only that the steps be consecutive on the lattice the sum lives on.

Design 5 — the engineered load (the telescope is the proof's engine). Given: a proof that will not close — an induction step too weak, an inequality too loose. Move: design the rung or the bound because its differences telescope; the proof then closes by the collapse. Pay: the theorem, with the telescope never visible in the statement. P74 is this design at full strength: its rung \(2 - \tfrac{1}{n}\) releases exactly \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\) per rung — a telescoping slack — and its Sylvester remainder is an un-collapsed telescope with the last term showing. This page does not re-teach the design; it files it where it lives: telescoping is what the loads are made of.

One warning, stated once because it prices all five designs: the middle cancels only if the steps are consecutive. The delayed telescope — steps two apart — is not a broken telescope but a different one, with two survivors on each end; B11 worked it for \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}2)}\), and the census is the only guard that never sleeps on the count. Every design above inherits B11's obligation 2 unchanged: write the end-values, count the delay, test the row.

Box P69.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. The move lives wherever a chain iterates and a middle can die. In estimates: growth of sums and products — §4's first example cages \(\sum \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\); the same design cages the tail of \(\sum \tfrac{1}{k^2}\) (§5) and, one floor down in B11, the harmonic series between logarithmic arms. In identities: binomial sums (§4's third example), factorial sums, Fibonacci sums (§8's third exercise) — families whose recurrences make terms into differences. In products: cancellable factorizations, infinite products with closed partial products (§8's exploration runs the most famous one). In proofs: P74's loads, and — through B11's boundary reading, that a sum of steps around a closed loop is zero — the potential-difference tests on networks that B09 and B10 build toward the cohomology pages of the far ladder. And in the machine age, literally: the algorithms of §7 decide, for entire families of terms, whether a staircase exists — and hand it over as a certificate when it does.

The three-key signature — the checklist that says the move applies. (1) Claim type: an identity, an inequality, or a limit, involving an object iterated over a range — and the claim is about the total, not about individual terms. (2) Problem shape: the term (or factor) carries chain structure — a factorization into neighbours \(k(k{+}1)\), a conjugate pair \(\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}\), a ratio of consecutive values, a recurrence linking \(k\) and \(k{+}1\) — or, for an estimate, the census shows the total growing like the values of some one candidate \(f\), which is chain structure one level up. (3) Tools at hand: a candidate staircase (from the kit, from the census, or from a recurrence in the problem), the one-subtraction check to confirm it, and a row the reader can add by hand to test the survivors. When all three keys turn, reach for the move; when key 2 alone turns — chain structure but no candidate and no shape — the honest answer may be that no staircase exists, and §5's exhibits show what that looks like from the inside.

The diagnostic read off a finished proof: a telescoping claim is exactly as strong as its two ends and exactly as honest as its census. A proof that exhibits the staircase has handed over its certificate — check it by one subtraction; a proof that exhibits a cage owes you both arms' telescoping; and a proof whose survivors were never tested against a hand-added row has not yet earned the word proof. That is the whole audit, and it is the audit §5 runs on the broken examples.

4 · Three telescopes in public

One worked example per disguise, each ending the way this book ends everything: survivors stated, then tested against a row the reader can add by hand. Example 1 builds a cage — the telescopes are in the arms, and the prisoner never collapses at all. Example 2 runs the move on a product — the steps are ratios, and the middle cancels in pairs. Example 3 finds a telescope wearing someone else's clothes — an identity in Pascal's triangle — where the recognition is the entire work. Three designs, one theorem.

Example 1 — the designed cage: the hundred square roots, tightened

Builds on B11, P02, P15 · design: the engineered staircase, read forwards and backwards

Section 2 left the prisoner in a window ten wide — \(10 \le S_{100} \le 20\) — and confessed that the lower arm was slack. The slack has a reason, and the reason has a fix, and both are the pattern's own property. Look at what section 2 actually built: both arms came from the backward steps of the staircase \(f(k) = \sqrt{k}\). The term was caged as \(\Delta f(k{-}1) \;\le\; \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \;\le\; 2\,\Delta f(k{-}1)\), and both sums of backward steps collapsed to \(\sqrt{n}\) and \(2\sqrt{n}\). One reading of one staircase; one direction.

The staircase has a second reading. B11's discrete derivative runs forwards, \(\Delta f(k) = f(k{+}1) - f(k)\), and the forward step of the square-root staircase is, by the same conjugate move, \(\Delta f(k) = \sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}\). Its denominator is now caught on the other side: \(\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k} \ge 2\sqrt{k}\), so

\[\underbrace{\frac{2}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}}_{=\;2\,\Delta f(k)} \;\;\le\;\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}},\]

and the term now sits between a double forward step below and a double backward step above — \(2\,\Delta f(k) \le \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \le 2\,\Delta f(k{-}1)\) — the same term, the same staircase, caged by the same trick with the shift moved one place. Sum the new arm by B11's theorem, with obligation 2 — check the shift — performed out loud: forward steps \(k = 1\) to \(n\) pay \(f(n{+}1) - f(1)\), not \(f(n) - f(0)\):

\[\sum_{k=1}^{n} 2\,\Delta f(k) \;=\; 2\bigl(\sqrt{n{+}1} - \sqrt{1}\bigr) \;=\; 2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2.\]

The cage, tightened:

\[2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2 \;\;\le\;\; S_n \;\;\le\;\; 2\sqrt{n}.\]

At \(n = 100\) the honest sentence changes from "between \(10\) and \(20\)" to "between \(18.0998\) and \(20\)" — a window \(1.9002\) wide, bought with one conjugate and one shift-check. And the window never grows: its width is \(2\sqrt{n} - \bigl(2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2\bigr) = 2 - 2\bigl(\sqrt{n{+}1} - \sqrt{n}\bigr) < 2\), always, while the prisoner itself grows without bound. The cage does not evaluate \(S_n\); it pins the growth to a relative error that dies like \(1/\sqrt{n}\).

Which lower arm should the reader believe — \(\sqrt{n}\) from the backward reading, or \(2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2\) from the forward one? Both are proved; the census decides which is tighter, and the answer is: it depends on \(n\).

The cage, censused. The prisoner sits above both lower arms at every row; the two lower arms cross between \(n = 1\) and \(n = 2\).

At \(n = 1\) the backward arm wins — and wins exactly: \(S_1 = 1 = \sqrt{1}\), the one row where the prisoner touches an arm, because \(\sqrt{0} = 0\) puts the staircase's first step on the floor. From \(n = 2\) on, the forward arm wins, and by \(n = 100\) it is the whole story: \(18.0998\) against \(10\). The honest cage keeps both lower arms and reads the tighter one off the census — P02's habit applied to the proof's parts, not just to the claim. Fig P69.1 draws all of it.

What Example 1 adds to section 2. The arms of a cage are design choices: the staircase \(f\), the direction of the step, the multiplier — and the shift is the design variable that mattered. The same staircase, read backwards and forwards, produced two lower arms eight units apart at \(n = 100\) (\(10\) against \(18.0998\)); obligation 2 of B11's machine — check the shift — is literally the difference between them, and §5's Exhibit A runs this same design one step wrong and watches it die. Exercise 2 in §8 hands over the design space itself and asks which cages beat which. And the design is portable: B11's harmonic trap — \(\ln(n{+}1) \le H_n \le 1 + \ln n\) — is the identical construction one floor down, forward logarithm steps below, backward above, with the same crossing at \(n = 1\) and the same census deciding which arm to quote. The hundred square roots were never special; they were the design's first customer.

Example 2 — the product telescope: a product that eats itself

Builds on B11, P02 · design: ratios, not differences

P02's reflex works on products exactly as it works on sums — census first. Multiply out \(\prod_{k=2}^{n} \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\bigr)\) for the small \(n\), by hand: \(n = 2\) gives \(\tfrac{3}{4}\); \(n = 3\) gives \(\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot \tfrac{8}{9} = \tfrac{2}{3}\); \(n = 4\) gives \(\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{15}{16} = \tfrac{5}{8}\); \(n = 5\) gives \(\tfrac{5}{8} \cdot \tfrac{24}{25} = \tfrac{3}{5}\). The census — \(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{8}, \tfrac{3}{5}\) — is shouting a pattern: \(\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4}\), \(\tfrac{2}{3} = \tfrac{4}{6}\), \(\tfrac{5}{8} = \tfrac{5}{8}\), \(\tfrac{3}{5} = \tfrac{6}{10}\). Conjecture: the product is \(\tfrac{n{+}1}{2n}\), always.

The recognition — obligation 1, never by memory — is one multiplication. The factor \(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\) is a difference of squares in disguise, and a difference of squares is a product of neighbours:

\[1 - \frac{1}{k^2} \;=\; \frac{k^2 - 1}{k^2} \;=\; \frac{(k{-}1)(k{+}1)}{k^2} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{k{-}1}{k}}_{\text{one chain}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{k{+}1}{k}}_{\text{a second chain}}.\]

Each factor is now a ratio of one function at shifted arguments — the multiplicative shape of B11's step. And the theorem transfers verbatim, with the identity \(1\) playing the role that \(0\) played for sums: a product of consecutive ratios keeps only its ends, because every interior value arrives once as a numerator and once as a denominator, and divides out. No logarithm is needed to see it — the cancellation is direct, factor against factor — though the reader who takes \(\ln\) of everything will recognize the sum-telescope underneath the product one, and B11's exploration already met that mirror. Fig P69.2 draws both chains dying.

Obligation 2 — the shift — collects its fee in the product setting too. The identity \(\prod_{k=2}^{n} \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\bigr) = \tfrac{n{+}1}{2n}\) starts at \(k = 2\), and the start is load-bearing: at \(k = 1\) the factor is \(1 - \tfrac{1}{1^2} = 0\), and a product that touches \(0\) is \(0\) forever. The census of the conjecture — \(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{5}{8}, \tfrac{3}{5}\) — agrees with the survivors at every row, and the two ends of the algebra meet: proved.

And the limit is a dividend the sum-telescope could not pay. As \(n\) grows, \(\tfrac{n{+}1}{2n}\) closes in on \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the infinite product \(\prod_{k=2}^{\infty} \bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{k^2}\bigr)\) has an exact finite value, \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) — read off partial products that telescope, with no infinite process ever performed. Infinite objects with finite values, computed by finite bookkeeping: that is the product telescope's signature service, and §8's exploration runs the most famous one in mathematics — Wallis's product for \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\), whose partial products collapse by this page's move into double factorials — and whose exploration shows exactly where the telescope's reach ends.

Example 3 — the found telescope: the hockey stick in Pascal's triangle

Builds on B11, P02, P23 · design: the found telescope

Disguise three is the subtlest: no sum is printed at all, and the telescope is hiding inside an identity somebody proved for other reasons. Take the binomial coefficients — P23's lattice, Pascal's triangle — and the identity known as the hockey stick: for any \(r \le n\),

\[\sum_{k=r}^{n} \binom{k}{r} \;=\; \binom{n{+}1}{r{+}1}.\]

A census first, because the claim is about a sum the reader may never have written as a sum. With \(r = 2\): the terms are \(\binom{2}{2}, \binom{3}{2}, \binom{4}{2}, \binom{5}{2}, \binom{6}{2} = 1, 3, 6, 10, 15\); the partial sums are \(1, 4, 10, 20, 35\); and the right side, \(\binom{3}{3}, \binom{4}{3}, \binom{5}{3}, \binom{6}{3}, \binom{7}{3}\), reads \(1, 4, 10, 20, 35\). Agreement at every row, and the shape of the agreement — a diagonal run of entries in the triangle summing to one entry one row down — is what the name draws.

Now the recognition, and it is one line, because Pascal already wrote it. Pascal's rule says each entry is the sum of the two above it: \(\binom{k{+}1}{r{+}1} = \binom{k}{r{+}1} + \binom{k}{r}\). Slide one term across:

\[\binom{k}{r} \;=\; \binom{k{+}1}{r{+}1} \;-\; \binom{k}{r{+}1} \;=\; \Delta f(k), \qquad f(k) = \binom{k}{r{+}1}.\]

Pascal's rule is the statement that a binomial is a step of the binomials one column over — the triangle is a machine for anti-differences, and nobody asked it to be. Obligation 1, recognize by one subtraction, is hereby somebody else's theorem; the craft was only to notice that a two-term rearrangement of Pascal's rule has the exact shape \(f(k{+}1) - f(k)\). Then B11's theorem pays out the survivors, and obligation 2 — the shift — is settled by a boundary convention stated out loud: the steps run \(k = r\) to \(n\), so the survivors are \(f(n{+}1) - f(r) = \binom{n{+}1}{r{+}1} - \binom{r}{r{+}1}\), and \(\binom{r}{r{+}1} = 0\) — choosing \(r{+}1\) things from \(r\) is impossible — the same floor-start as Example 1's \(\sqrt{0} = 0\).

The stick, drawn: Fig P69.3.

One identity, three costumes already worn in this book. At \(r = 0\) the stick says \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} 1 = n{+}1\) — counting. At \(r = 1\) it says \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} k = \binom{n{+}1}{2} = \tfrac{n(n{+}1)}{2}\) — Gauss's formula, which B11 bought as a net climb of the triangular staircase and P66 bought by the induction ladder, now arriving in counting clothes: the same theorem's third costume, and the proof above is the shortest of the three. And the general stick has a second, completely different proof — count the same objects two ways — that belongs to P70, the next pattern page after this one; the two proofs teach different things, and §6 says what.

What the three examples jointly teach. Example 1's telescope was engineered — the staircase chosen, the shift chosen, the arms built to order. Example 2's was converted — a product turned into ratios by one multiplication, then treated as a sum of multiplicative steps. Example 3's was found — sitting inside a table the reader already owned, revealed by reading a familiar rule as a difference. Design, conversion, recognition: the three ways a telescope arrives, and the diagnosis of which one a problem calls for is the whole of this pattern's craft. What none of the three examples showed is failure — and failure is where the honest boundary of the move lives. That is §5.

5 · When it fails — three broken telescopes, autopsied

The three disguises of §4 were successes; the honest curriculum needs the failures more. Each exhibit below is a real claim, of the kind a problem-set prints and a student ships — each passes a casual glance, each dies at a specific row of a census, and each autopsy names the one obligation whose absence killed it. Read them as drills in the other direction: here is what the move looks like from inside a mistake.

Exhibit A — the wrong shift: four survivors masquerading as two

The claim: \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(\tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}2}\bigr) \;=\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{n{+}2}\). The fake reasoning is fluent and almost right: "each term is a difference of reciprocals two apart, so the middle cancels and the ends survive — the first \(1\) and the last \(-\tfrac{1}{n{+}2}\)." Test it the cheap way first, at \(n = 1\): both sides say \(1 - \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{3}\), and the claim passes — the single-row check is not enough here, which is exactly why this exhibit is the dangerous one. At \(n = 2\): the left side is \(\tfrac{2}{3} + \bigl(\tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{4}\bigr) = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{11}{12}\); the claimed right side is \(1 - \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4} \ne \tfrac{11}{12}\). Dead. The autopsy: a gap of two slots delays the cancellation — each value leaves two terms after it arrives — so two values survive on each end: the truth is \(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{n{+}1} - \tfrac{1}{n{+}2}\), as B11 section 3(c) worked in full. Obligation 2 — check the shift — is the guard: a shift of \(s\) leaves \(s\) survivors on each side, and only the written-out small case shows which values actually survive. Example 1's two lower arms were this same bookkeeping done right; the exhibit is the same bookkeeping done from memory.

Exhibit B — no staircase: the sum that fences but never falls

The claim, in the shape it actually arrives: "evaluate \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{k^2}\)." The attempt: hunt for a staircase. The term \(\tfrac{1}{k^2}\) factors as \(\tfrac{1}{k} \cdot \tfrac{1}{k}\), and neither factor is a neighbour of the other; the split that worked for \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\) fails here because the two factors are the same factor — there is no shift between them, and B11 already named the verdict: no staircase in the toolkit has \(\Delta f = \tfrac{1}{k^2}\). But the failure is not useless, and saying so is the honest part: for \(k \ge 2\), \(\tfrac{1}{k^2} < \tfrac{1}{k(k{-}1)} = \tfrac{1}{k{-}1} - \tfrac{1}{k}\), and the comparison term telescopes. Summed past \(n\), the fence is exact: \(\sum_{k=n{+}1}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{k(k{-}1)} = \tfrac{1}{n}\), so the tail of the refused sum sits under \(\tfrac{1}{n}\) — P74's exploration squeezed \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\) between exactly such fences. The autopsy: key 2 of the signature turned (the term has multiplicative structure), but no candidate staircase existed, and pretending one did would have produced a formula that dies at the census. The sum converges to a real number, \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\), that no telescoping produces: the move's boundary is a fact about the term, not a failure of effort — and Example 1's cage design is precisely what survives at that boundary.

Exhibit C — recognition without the check: the missing half

The claim: \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}2)} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}2}\), "by the same split as \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\)". The recognition is right in kind — the term is a difference of reciprocals two apart — and wrong by a factor: one subtraction, the whole of obligation 1, says \(\tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}2} = \tfrac{2}{k(k{+}2)}\), twice the term. The split that is true is \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}2)} = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}2}\bigr)\). Ship the unchecked version and sum it at \(n = 1\): the claim pays \(1 - \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{3}\); the sum is \(\tfrac{1}{3}\). Dead by a factor of two, at the first row. The autopsy: this is the purest form of the disease the obligations exist for — the recognition (a difference of reciprocals) was correct, and only the constant was wrong, which is why "looks right" is not a check. The one-subtraction check costs five seconds and catches it every time; every worked example on this page performed it out loud, and the habit is the entire difference between §4 and this exhibit.

Scope, honestly. Reach: by B11's kit, every polynomial term and every pure exponential has a manufactured staircase; found art (\(k \cdot k!\), Pascal's rule) extends it case by case; products convert by one multiplication; and near-steps — terms trapped between multiples of steps — buy cages when evaluation is impossible. The boundary: \(\tfrac{1}{k}\) and \(\tfrac{1}{k^2}\) refuse, and the refusal is a property of the term, not of the solver; what survives there is the fence, never the value. Bookkeeping: the shift decides how many survivors exist (Exhibit A), the census catches every wrong answer cheaply (A and C both died at one row), and the check-by-one-subtraction catches the rest (C). One more boundary belongs to the machine age, and §7 tells it: for an entire named family of terms — the hypergeometric ones — an algorithm decides whether a staircase exists and hands it over when it does, so the recognition half of the craft is now automatable in that family; the design half — building cages, choosing shifts — is not.

6 · Where this goes

The move next door. The hockey stick has a second proof, and it belongs to P70 · Double counting: count the \((r{+}1)\)-element subsets of \(\{1, \ldots, n{+}1\}\) directly — \(\binom{n{+}1}{r{+}1}\) — and then again by their largest element: subsets whose largest element is \(k{+}1\) choose their other \(r\) elements from below, \(\binom{k}{r}\) ways, and \(k\) runs from \(r\) to \(n\). The two counts must agree, and the hockey stick falls out. The two proofs teach different things, which is why both exist: the telescope proof is bookkeeping — it works wherever a rule like Pascal's rearranges into a difference — while the counting proof is an explanation, and P70 owns the craft of finding one. But notice the division of labour that §4 already drew: Example 3's identity has both proofs; Example 1's cage has only one. An inequality — "the prisoner sits in this window" — is not a count of anything, and no double counting will ever produce it: the engineered cage is telescoping's private territory.

Two floors up, the ledger becomes a machine. P73 · Generating functions wears whole sequences as single objects: the sequence \(\binom{k}{r}\) becomes the polynomial \(\sum_k \binom{k}{r} x^k\), and partial-summing a sequence becomes multiplying its polynomial by \(\tfrac{1}{1{-}x}\). The hockey stick, in that language, is the identity \((1{-}x)^{-r{-}1} \cdot (1{-}x)^{-1} = (1{-}x)^{-r{-}2}\) read off at one coefficient — and the collapse that makes the reading possible is the same middle-dies cancellation this page has been running by hand. Telescoping is the engine inside P73's gearboxes; P73 is where the engine gets a transmission.

The group's forward seed. This page and B11 are group C's number-line floor, and the group grows in the direction B11 planted: a sum of steps depends only on its ends, so around a closed loop it is zero. B09 moves the ledger onto dots joined by lines — values on dots, steps on edges — and B10 asks when edge-numbers come from dot-numbers and finds the loop obstruction: a test the data can fail, measuring exactly the failure that P33's far ladder names. One pattern page nearer helps too: P72's monovariants are staircases read for direction — a quantity that only moves one way is a staircase whose steps never change sign, and P72's termination proofs are cage proofs where the cage's existence is the verdict.

Looking back through the tier. This is the eleventh move of tier 2, and every one of its load-bearing habits was already on the shelf: P02's census is obligation 3 and the design tool that found the square-root cage; P15's squeeze is the cage's architecture; P66's ladder is this page's theorem walked one rung at a time; P74's engineered loads were this page's move run at proof-strength; P23's lattice turned out to be a table of anti-differences wearing different print. The pattern pages are not a catalogue; they are the same small set of honest habits — look at small cases, trap what you cannot evaluate, check every shift — recombined. Telescoping is what the habits look like when the object is a ledger.

7 · Lineage — the canon and the machine age

In the canon. The move is old, and the problem-solving canon has always carried it. Engel's Problem-Solving Strategies runs telescoping sums through its induction chapter — the ladder needs its steps, and the steps are exactly this page's staircases — and its sequences chapter works the product telescopes and cancellation chains of §4's second design. The classroom coinage itself — the folding spyglass — was told on B11, with Boole's 1860 treatise on the difference calculus; nothing here repeats it. What the canon never wrote down, and what this page tried to, is the diagnostic: not "here is a telescoping sum" but "here is how to notice one that is not wearing the costume" — the three disguises, the three-key signature, the design space of cages. That part is the pattern's own.

The machine age. In 1978 Bill Gosper published a decision procedure for indefinite hypergeometric summation in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences: for an entire named family of terms — the hypergeometric ones, whose ratio of consecutive terms is a rational function, a family that includes most of B11's starter kit and many wilder sums — an algorithm now decides whether a staircase of the same family exists, and hands it over when it does. The recognition half of this page's craft, the half that §5's Exhibit C broke, is for that family a solved problem. Thirteen years later, Doron Zeilberger's method of creative telescoping — published in the Journal of Symbolic Computation in 1991 — turned the same idea on definite sums, where the telescope must be created rather than found: the method engineers the telescoping representation the way Example 1 engineered its arms. The Wilf–Zeilberger certificates that descend from it are exactly this page's third new word in print: a telescoping certificate is the staircase handed over with the claim, checkable by one subtraction — the modern literature's own name for obligation 1, a hundred years after the classroom named the spyglass.

Honest boundaries of this section. None of the history above is load-bearing for the mathematics: the page's claims stand on the census, the conjugates, and B11's theorem, as §2 through §5 proved them. The lineage is told for the same reason the rest of the book tells lineage — so the reader knows which parts are the species' ancient inheritance, which are the canon's coaching, and which are this page's own diagnosis — and so that when the reader meets "Gosper's algorithm" or "creative telescoping" in the machine-age literature, the meeting is not a first one. The algorithm decides existence for its family; it does not design cages, choose shifts, or say when a fence is the honest answer. Those, for now, are still yours.

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the odd lattice

Sum \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{1}{4k^2 - 1}\). (a) Spot the lattice: write the term as a difference of two reciprocals of odd numbers, checking the split by one subtraction. (b) State the survivors out loud and close the sum. (c) Census at \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4\), then the limit as \(n \to \infty\) — and say what the limit means that §2's square-root sum could never have.

Hint

\(4k^2 - 1\) is a difference of squares on the odd lattice: \((2k{-}1)(2k{+}1)\). The two reciprocals are one step apart on the lattice of odd numbers — which is exactly the shape B11's table row for \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}1)}\) wears on the ordinary lattice.

Worked answer

(a) \(\frac{1}{2k{-}1} - \frac{1}{2k{+}1} = \frac{2}{(2k{-}1)(2k{+}1)} = \frac{2}{4k^2 - 1}\), so the split is \(\frac{1}{4k^2 - 1} = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\frac{1}{2k{-}1} - \frac{1}{2k{+}1}\bigr)\) — checked, not remembered. (b) The run of steps is consecutive on the odd lattice, so exactly two values survive: the first numerator's \(\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1\) and the last denominator's \(-\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2n{+}1}\). The sum is \(\frac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{2n{+}1}\bigr) = \frac{n}{2n{+}1}\). (c) Census: \(n = 1\): the term is \(\tfrac{1}{3}\), and the survivors pay \(\tfrac{1}{3}\); \(n = 2\): \(\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{15} = \tfrac{6}{15} = \tfrac{2}{5}\); \(n = 3\): \(\tfrac{2}{5} + \tfrac{1}{35} = \tfrac{3}{7}\); \(n = 4\): \(\tfrac{3}{7} + \tfrac{1}{63} = \tfrac{4}{9}\) — agreement at every row. The limit is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\): an infinite sum of positive terms with a finite value, read off two survivors — the square-root sum of §2 grows without bound and no closed form exists, but this one closes and converges: the telescope evaluates it at every \(n\), and the limit is a dividend (Example 2's move), not a computation.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the design space of cages

Example 1 used the multiplier \(2\) on both readings of the staircase, and the exercise is to show that \(2\) was not a choice but the ceiling. (a) Prove \(\sqrt{k}\,\bigl(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k}\bigr) < \tfrac{1}{2}\) for every \(k \ge 1\), and show the left side closes in on \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) from below. (b) Conclude that \(c = 2\) is the largest constant with \(c\bigl(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k}\bigr) \le \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\) for every \(k\) — and that any \(c > 2\) fails once \(k\) is large — so the forward arm \(2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2\) is the best cage its family can buy. (c) Re-assemble Example 1's tightened cage from parts and census it at \(n = 1, 4, 100\).

Hint

(a) One conjugate: \(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}\), so the left side is \(\tfrac{\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}\) — compare it with \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) by cross-multiplying. For the approach to \(\tfrac{1}{2}\), divide numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{k}\) and let \(k\) grow. (b) The inequality \(c\bigl(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k}\bigr) \le \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\) rearranges to \(c \cdot \sqrt{k}\bigl(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k}\bigr) \le 1\); now use (a). (c) The cage is Example 1's; the census rows are already on this page — find them and quote them.

Worked answer

(a) \(\sqrt{k}\bigl(\sqrt{k{+}1} - \sqrt{k}\bigr) = \frac{\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}\), and \(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}} < \frac{1}{2}\) exactly because \(2\sqrt{k} < \sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}\), that is \(\sqrt{k} < \sqrt{k{+}1}\) — true, strictly, for every \(k\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{k}\): the left side is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 1/k} + 1}\), which climbs toward \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) as \(k\) grows. (b) The workable constants are \(c \le \inf_k \frac{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{k}}\), and \(\frac{\sqrt{k{+}1} + \sqrt{k}}{\sqrt{k}} = 1 + \sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{k}}\) is always \(> 2\) but closes in on \(2\); so \(c = 2\) works at every \(k\) (with slack), and every \(c > 2\) eventually asks for more than the term has — the failure is a large-\(k\) fact, invisible at any fixed row, which is why the design question needs the inequality, not a census. (c) The cage: \(2\sqrt{n{+}1} - 2 \le S_n \le 2\sqrt{n}\). Census: \(n = 1\): \(0.8284 \le 1 \le 2\); \(n = 4\): \(2.4721 \le 2.7845 \le 4\); \(n = 100\): \(18.0998 \le 18.5896 \le 20\) — the rows of Example 1's table, now yours by a different route: the same three numbers, arrived at from the design side.

Exercise 3 ●●● — Fibonacci squares, and a second route

The Fibonacci numbers \(F_1 = F_2 = 1\), \(F_{k{+}1} = F_k + F_{k{-}1}\) hide a telescope in a place no table would suggest: the claim is \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} F_k^2 \;=\; F_n F_{n{+}1}\). (a) Verify from the recurrence that \(F_{k{+}1} - F_{k{-}1} = F_k\), then run obligation 1 on the candidate staircase \(f(k) = F_{k{-}1} F_k\): compute \(\Delta f\) by one subtraction and recognize the term. (b) State the survivors — noting the floor-start \(F_0 = 0\) — and close the sum. (c) Census at \(n = 1, \ldots, 8\). (d) Climb P66's rung as the second route: show the formula plus the next term's step \(F_{n{+}1}^2\) simplifies to the formula at \(n{+}1\).

Hint

(a) The recurrence says \(F_{k{+}1} = F_k + F_{k{-}1}\); slide one term across. Then \(\Delta f(k) = F_k F_{k{+}1} - F_{k{-}1} F_k\), and both terms share a factor. (b) The survivors are the two end-values \(f(n{+}1)\) and \(f(1)\); \(f(1) = F_0 F_1\). (c) The squares run \(1, 1, 4, 9, 25, 64, 169, 441\); the Fibonacci numbers run \(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, 34\). (d) Factor \(F_n F_{n{+}1} + F_{n{+}1}^2\) before expanding anything.

Worked answer

(a) \(F_{k{+}1} - F_{k{-}1} = (F_k + F_{k{-}1}) - F_{k{-}1} = F_k\). Then \(\Delta f(k) = f(k{+}1) - f(k) = F_k F_{k{+}1} - F_{k{-}1} F_k = F_k \bigl(F_{k{+}1} - F_{k{-}1}\bigr) = F_k \cdot F_k = F_k^2\) — the term, recognized by one subtraction, and the subtraction was somebody else's recurrence rearranged, exactly Example 3's kind of found art. (b) \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} F_k^2 = f(n{+}1) - f(1) = F_n F_{n{+}1} - F_0 F_1 = F_n F_{n{+}1} - 0\): the floor-start does the whole bookkeeping, as in Example 1 and Example 3. (c) Census: partial sums \(1, 2, 6, 15, 40, 104, 273, 714\); products \(F_n F_{n{+}1}\): \(1 \cdot 1 = 1\), \(1 \cdot 2 = 2\), \(2 \cdot 3 = 6\), \(3 \cdot 5 = 15\), \(5 \cdot 8 = 40\), \(8 \cdot 13 = 104\), \(13 \cdot 21 = 273\), \(21 \cdot 34 = 714\) — agreement at every row. (d) The rung: \(F_n F_{n{+}1} + F_{n{+}1}^2 = F_{n{+}1}(F_n + F_{n{+}1}) = F_{n{+}1} F_{n{+}2}\), the formula at \(n{+}1\); base \(n = 1\): \(1 = F_1 F_2\). Two routes, one identity — the survivors and the ladder agreeing is what P78 means by believing a result.

Exercise 4 ◆ — three telescopes diagnosed cold

Three claims ship below with no warnings attached. For each: verdict, the killing row, the disease, and the cure — the four-part diagnosis, twice as fast as it reads. (a) \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}} - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k{+}1}}\bigr) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\). (b) \(\sum_{k=2}^{n} k \cdot k! = (n{+}1)!\). (c) \(\tfrac{1}{k(k{+}3)} = \tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}3}\).

Hint

All three are §5's diseases wearing fresh coats, and none survives contact with a two-row census. For (a), write the staircase's two end-values explicitly before checking anything — where does the last step land? For (b), B11's table has the row for \(k \cdot k!\) — summed from \(k = 1\); this claim starts at \(k = 2\). For (c), one subtraction, at \(k = 1\), with the true split's constant still to be found — and a shift of three says how many survivors each side carries.

Worked answer

(a) Dead — shift disease. The staircase is \(f(k) = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\), and the run \(k = 1\) to \(n\) pays \(f(n{+}1) - f(1) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n{+}1}}\), not \(1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\): the last step lands at \(n{+}1\), inside the staircase, and the claim read the landing one early. Killing row: \(n = 1\), where the claim says \(1 - 1 = 0\) and the sum is \(1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.2929\). Cure: obligation 2 — write both end-values before believing the survivors. (b) Dead — start disease. B11's table row sums \(k \cdot k!\) from \(k = 1\): \((n{+}1)! - 1\); a start at \(k = 2\) removes the first term, \(1 \cdot 1! = 1\), so the truth is \((n{+}1)! - 2\). Killing row: \(n = 2\), where the claim says \(3! = 6\) and the sum is \(2 \cdot 2! = 4\). Cure: the census, plus the habit of recomputing the end-values whenever the start moves — survivors belong to a run, not to a term. (c) Dead — constant disease, Exhibit C's family one slot wider. One subtraction: \(\tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}3} = \tfrac{3}{k(k{+}3)}\), three times the term; the true split is \(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{1}{k{+}3}\bigr)\). Killing row: \(k = 1\), claim \(\tfrac{3}{4}\), term \(\tfrac{1}{4}\). Cure: obligation 1 — the check that costs five seconds — plus the shift census: a gap of three leaves three survivors on each side, so the honest full sum is \(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{n{+}1} - \tfrac{1}{n{+}2} - \tfrac{1}{n{+}3}\bigr)\), which at \(n = 1\) pays \(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(\tfrac{11}{6} - \tfrac{13}{12}\bigr) = \tfrac{1}{4}\), as it must.

Open exploration ◐ — Wallis, and where the telescope stops

The most famous product in mathematics is Wallis's, and it is Example 2's design pushed until the telescope creaks: \(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{4n^2}{4n^2 - 1} = \frac{\pi}{2}\). (i) Verify the factor split \(\frac{4n^2}{4n^2{-}1} = \frac{2n}{2n{-}1} \cdot \frac{2n}{2n{+}1}\) — one multiplication. (ii) Show the \(N\)-th partial product collapses to double factorials, \(p_N = \frac{(2N)!!}{(2N{-}1)!!} \cdot \frac{(2N)!!}{(2N{+}1)!!}\), and then explain, in the language of Fig P69.2, exactly why the collapse stops there: which values arrive in both a numerator and a denominator, which refuse to cancel, and what — in one honest sentence — makes this product harder than Example 2's. (iii) Run the census: the exact partials begin \(p_1 = \tfrac{4}{3} \approx 1.3333\), \(p_2 = \tfrac{64}{45} \approx 1.4222\), \(p_3 = \tfrac{256}{175} \approx 1.4629\), \(p_4 = \tfrac{16384}{11025} \approx 1.4861\), \(p_5 = \tfrac{65536}{43659} \approx 1.5011\), and the decimals climb on: \(p_6 \approx 1.5116\), \(p_7 \approx 1.5193\), \(p_8 \approx 1.5253\) — toward \(\tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5708\), slowly. (iv) State the honest boundary: what the telescope delivers (closed partial products, an increasing census, a limit that exists) and what it never delivers (the value \(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\) itself — the survivors never finish, and no rearrangement of evens and odds will pay out \(\pi\)). Wallis reached the limit in 1655 by interpolation, an argument not regarded as rigorous; the first honest proofs came with integration, far up this book's ladder — and the reader who wants a preview has already met the shape: the survivors that refuse to cancel are exactly what \(\pi\) is made of.
