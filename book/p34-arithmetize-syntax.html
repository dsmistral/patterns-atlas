The Patterns Atlas · P34 · the third move of tier 3

Arithmetize the syntax

Give every statement a number; then arithmetic argues about language. What a machine can check, a machine can search — and what it can never check, the numbers say so.

New word: Gödel numbering. A codebook assigns each symbol of a formal language a number, and each string of symbols maps to a single integer: the first symbol's code becomes the exponent of the first prime, the second symbol's code the exponent of the second prime, and so on — the string \(0=0\) becomes \(2^7 \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^7\). Because whole numbers factor into primes in exactly one way, the process runs both ways: divide the number out, read the exponents, recover the string, with no ambiguity anywhere. That is the whole device. It is not encryption and not compression — it is a dictionary that lets arithmetic speak about sentences, proofs, and programs, all of which become numbers the moment the dictionary is opened. This page opens the dictionary on a language small enough to census by machine, and watches the diagonal argument — which last tier needed a sponsor — learn to speak about itself.

1 · Before we begin

P14 taught the diagonal: to refute an alleged complete census, build the one object that differs from every row. Its third example sketched the halting predictor — feed it any program and input, and it answers, correctly for every pair, whether the run halts — and broke it with the flipper \(F\), which consults the predictor about \((F, y)\) and does the opposite. The one-line refutation was honest, and it came with two gaps, stated there as debts. First, program needed formal meaning. Second — the deeper one — the flipper had to be able to quote itself and its partner to the predictor: programs must be quotable to programs, or "consult and contradict" is not a construction but a hope. The toy census said so plainly: the skeptic \(t_3\) was placed in the universe by hand, a sponsored refuter, and the real theorem's content is that the universe of programs needs no sponsor.

Other pages hold the tools this page assembles. P01: before answering, say exactly what is asked — here, what a sentence and a proof formally are, before any argument about them. P05: reduce to canonical form and prove it once — prime factorization is the canonical form this page cannot live without. P07: invent the object the problem forgot to supply — a dictionary nobody asked for. P25: when the possibilities are finite, let the machine count them — the censuses below are its work, run twice over. B01 and B02: divide and read the remainder — the recovery operation at the heart of decoding, and the packing trick that stores sequences in number pairs. And P66: the grammar's definitions bottom out in inductions — this page trusts them and checks their shadows, by counting what they produce.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P14's flip was theater until "consult and contradict" became an operation a machine can perform on things the machine can store. The missing bridge is a way to make syntax itself — symbols, strings, sentences, proofs — into arithmetic. The flipper reads a prediction and negates it; if predictions and programs are numbers, reading is division and negating is subtraction. Build the bridge, and the diagonal stops being a sketch about an imagined universe of programs and becomes a theorem about the integers themselves. The bridge is paid for up front and honestly: everything on this page is small enough to check, the receipts are censuses, and censuses do not lie.

2 · Meet the problem

Fix a toy language, small enough to census completely and honest enough to mean something. Call it Mini-Arithmetic. Its symbols, twelve of them: brackets \((\,)\), the connectives \(\neg\) and \(\to\), the quantifier \(\forall\), the equals sign, the numeral \(0\), the successor letter \(S\), the operations \(+\) and \(\times\), the variable \(x\), and the prime \('\) that makes new variables \(x', x'', \ldots\). Its terms: \(0\); any \(x', x'', \ldots\); \(S\) applied to a term; and bracketed sums and products \((s+t)\), \((s \times t)\). Its formulas: equations \(s = t\) between terms; \(\neg F\) from a formula; \(\forall x\,F\) from a formula; \((F \to G)\) from two. Nothing else is anything. That is a grammar — a machine can run it, and this page will make it.

Now the dictionary. The codebook gives each symbol its position as a code:

A string \(c_1 c_2 \ldots c_k\) becomes one integer: \(2^{c_1} \cdot 3^{c_2} \cdot 5^{c_3} \cdots\), each symbol's code the exponent of the next prime. Four receipts, computed by hand and checked by machine: \(0=0\) maps to \(2^7 \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^7 = 7{,}290{,}000{,}000\); \(x=0\) to \(2^{11} \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^7 = 116{,}640{,}000{,}000\); \(0=x\) to \(2^7 \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^{11} = 4{,}556{,}250{,}000{,}000\); and \(x=x\) to \(2^{11} \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^{11} = 72{,}900{,}000{,}000{,}000\). Decoding is division: factor, read exponents, look up symbols. The machine census tried every string of at most three symbols — all \(1{,}884\) of them — through the dictionary and back: zero mismatches. The map is honest both ways, and P05 is why: factorization is the canonical form, so no two strings share a number and no string has two.

Three questions, one dictionary. First: which numbers name sentences? Most do not — of the \(19{,}999\) integers from 2 to 20,000, exactly \(190\) are string-numbers of any kind (the rest are jams: gaps among the exponents, or exponents past 12), and not one is a sentence-number. What is the smallest number that names a sentence? Second: can a machine check a proof without understanding it? A proof is a sequence of sentences, each either an axiom or licensed from earlier lines by a rule — if sentences are numbers, checking each step is arithmetic, and the sequence itself must become a number too. Third: can a sentence talk about its own number? P14's flipper needed programs quotable to programs; here the same need returns as: can a sentence carry, inside itself, a name of its own number? Each question looks linguistic. Each will be answered by counting, dividing, and multiplying — arithmetic about language, which is the move this page names.

3 · Name the move

§2 built a dictionary and let it ask arithmetic's questions. The move, named:

Pattern P34 — Arithmetize the syntax. When the objects of study are linguistic — sentences, proofs, programs — and the question is about all of them at once, do not argue about language from outside. Instead: (1) install an honest codebook: every symbol a number, every string one integer by prime exponents, decodable by division, collision-free by unique factorization; (2) translate each relation the argument needs — being a sentence, being a proof, being a prediction — into arithmetic a machine can decide, by census on small cases and by construction in general; (3) run the ordinary arithmetic moves on the numbers: P14's diagonal flips predictions, P25's censuses count what the grammar produces. Syntax becomes a subject of arithmetic; self-reference becomes an equation; and limits on what numbers can decide become limits on what language can say about itself.

Or, in words: before asking a machine to reason about sentences, teach it to count them. Name every sentence with a number; check every property a proof needs by dividing; then the liar's old trick — "about me, it says the opposite" — stops being a paradox and becomes a piece of arithmetic, with a number, and a theorem either side can lose.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the objects are finite syntax. Sentences, proofs, programs: things spelled from a finite alphabet, listable, countable. Jam-mode: the objects are numbers already, or physical things without a grammar — then no dictionary is needed, or none is possible, and the question belongs to P01 before it belongs anywhere.

Key 2 — the relations are mechanically decidable. "Is this string a sentence?" runs the grammar; "is this array a proof?" checks each line against axioms and rules; "is this entry a prediction?" looks it up. Jam-mode: the relation needs insight — "is this a beautiful proof", "is this program useful" — then nothing checkable is on the table, and the P44 discipline of saying what a step is comes first.

Key 3 — the argument wants self-reference. The diagonal lives on quoting: the flipper quotes the predictor, the sentence quotes its own provability. The codebook is what makes quoting storable. Jam-mode: no self-reference is needed — then plain arithmetic on the objects suffices, and the dictionary is overhead; open it anyway if the census is coming, since P25 counts numbers far more readily than strings.

Box P34.A — the machine, in three obligations. Every instance of the pattern — the toy below, Gödel's 1931 proof, a modern proof assistant — owes the same three duties, and each is checkable by hand on small cases:

1 · The codebook is honest. Every string gets a number; every number that gets anything gets one string; decoding is division; and the census question — which numbers name anything at all — is decidable. The receipts here: \(190\) string-numbers in \(2 \ldots 20{,}000\), not one a sentence; the smallest sentence-number \(7{,}290{,}000{,}000\), certified in Example 1. A codebook with gaps or collisions fails here, and the machine jams before it starts.

2 · The relations become decidable arithmetic. "Is a sentence" is a grammar run by machine; "is a proof of \(B\) from lines \(1 \ldots k\)" is line-by-line arithmetic: each earlier line's number looked up, each rule's license checked as an equation. Sequences pack into number pairs — the \(\beta\)-trick of Example 2 — so even the history of a derivation is one datum. The duty is exactness: the arithmetic must decide, not guess, and the census is its receipt.

3 · The diagonal hook is a real hook. Quoting must be an operation: "the predictor's entry about program \(i\)" must be readable off the stored numbers, and "the opposite" must be writable back. With the codebook, reading is division and writing is multiplication; the flipper becomes a fixed arithmetic expression of the predictor's own number, and the universe of programs is closed under consult-and-contradict — the sponsor P14 needed is unemployed. Example 3 collects the debt.

Box P34.B — where the move appears. The foundation — Gödel's 1931 paper, its codebook on page 179, built precisely to let proofs and sentences share the integers with the numbers they talk about. The wall — Tarski's theorem that truth never arithmetizes, Exhibit C below: the dictionary covers the mechanical relations and stops exactly there. The machine age — Turing's 1936 universal machine is the codebook as hardware: a machine whose inputs are program-numbers, quoting made wire. The census of computation — Kleene's 1943 \(T\) predicate, "run \(r\) on input \(x\) ends in \(y\)", as pure arithmetic, with the hierarchy that grades which relations arithmetize at all. The information-theoretic re-run — Chaitin's 1974 limits, where some facts carry more bits than the axioms can hold. And the industry — proof assistants, the P44 door: the blind line-by-line checking of Example 2 at the scale of real mathematics. The signature in the wild: any question of the form "can the system itself decide, prove, verify, or predict statements about itself" — reach for the codebook first.

4 · The move at work — three encounters

The three encounters watch Box P34.A's three duties get paid, one per example: the census (obligation 1 — the honest codebook), the proof machine (obligation 2 — relations as decidable arithmetic), and the diagonal (obligation 3 — the hook that quotes). Every receipt below is small enough to recompute by hand.

Example 1 · The census — which numbers are sentences

Moves used: P01 pinning what a sentence is · P25 letting the machine count every case · P05 factorization as canonical form · B01 division for decoding.

Run the grammar by machine. Every string of up to four symbols — all \(22{,}620\) of them — tried against the term and formula rules: exactly \(14\) are terms, exactly \(20\) are formulas, and the 20 are worth listing, because a census that prints its catch is a census that can be checked: the four atoms \(0=0\), \(0=x\), \(x=0\), \(x=x\); their four negations; and the twelve atoms that use \(S\) or \(x'\), from \(0=S0\) to \(x'=x\). Length five adds \(45\) more formulas; length six, \(109\). The terms pile up as \(2, 3, 4, 5, 14, 39\) across lengths one to six; the formulas as \(0, 0, 4, 16, 45, 109\) — while the raw strings multiply by twelve at every step. The funnel narrows fast, and that narrowness is a theorem, not a vibe: it is what makes the smallest sentence-number findable.

Now the certification, in three parts, of a precise claim: no integer below \(7{,}290{,}000{,}000\) names a sentence, and that number names \(0=0\).

Part one — census. Every formula of length at most five is known by exhaustive machine search: \(65\) of them, over \(271{,}452\) strings tried. The smallest number among all \(65\) is code\((0=0) = 7{,}290{,}000{,}000\). Nothing shorter or equal in length beats it.

Part two — no brackets below six. A bracketed term costs five symbols at minimum — \((0+0)\) — so any formula containing a bracket is an atom of seven or more symbols, or an implication of nine or more. Formulas of length six are bracket-free, and a bracket-free formula avoids the symbols with codes 1 and 2, so every exponent is at least 3, and its number is at least \(2^3 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5^3 \cdot 7^3 \cdot 11^3 \cdot 13^3 = 30{,}030^3 = 27{,}081{,}081{,}027{,}000\). The census found \(109\) such formulas; every one of them dwarfs the claim.

Part three — brackets are expensive too. A bracketed formula has at least seven symbols, including one of code 1, one of code 2, one of code 6 — every formula contains an equals sign, because the grammar builds everything from atoms. Even the most number-friendly arrangement — the 6 riding the largest of seven primes, ones elsewhere — lands at least at \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 13 \cdot 17^6 = 724{,}851{,}197{,}070\). Both bounds stand above \(7{,}290{,}000{,}000\), and the claim is certified: the first sentence-number is \(0=0\)'s, and below ten billion there is exactly one sentence-number — that one. A three-symbol sentence already out-ranks every integer most people ever meet; a five-symbol one, \(SS0=SS0\), carries a forty-three-digit number (about \(1.1 \times 10^{42}\)). The dictionary is honest, and it is not cheap.

Example 2 · The proof machine — checking without understanding

Moves used: P25 the machine census · P07 the proof machine as auxiliary object · P01 pinning what a proof is, before trusting any.

Make "proof" a machine-checkable thing. Pool: all \(81\) equations \(s = t\) with terms of at most three symbols (nine terms: \(0, x, S0, Sx, x', SS0, SSx, Sx', x''\)). Axioms: \(t = t\) for each of the nine terms. Rules: R1 — from \(a = b\), infer \(Sa = Sb\); R2 — transitivity. A proof is a list of pool equations, each line an axiom or licensed from earlier lines. Checking is blind: match the line against the axiom shapes, or against R1 of an earlier line (add an \(S\) to both sides, compare), or against R2 of two earlier lines. No step consults meaning; each step consults arithmetic on the lines' numbers — Box P34.A's second duty, exactness rather than insight, running at a scale you can audit line by line.

The census, by machine: one-line proofs — the nine axioms. Two-line — \(81\). Three-line — \(729\). The pattern is a proof about the rules: for each axiom first line, the legal second lines are exactly the nine axioms again — R1 of \(t=t\) is \(St=St\), an axiom whenever it fits the pool, and R2 of axioms collapses to axioms — and the third line's new candidates are always inside the same nine. And the proofs of \(SS0=SS0\) run \(1, 9, 81\): the target is itself an axiom (\(t = t\) for \(t = SS0\)), so every legal prefix pads into a proof of it. The census counts proofs, not difficulty — blind checking cannot see depth, and that blindness is not a flaw but the point: what a machine checks, a machine checks about everything equally. The canonical chain \([0=0, \; S0=S0, \; SS0=SS0]\) — R1 twice — is present among the \(81\), and its lines are numbers: \(7{,}290{,}000{,}000\), then about \(9.8 \times 10^{23}\), then about \(1.1 \times 10^{42}\).

One duty remains: the sequence of lines must itself become a number, or the machine can check steps but not store derivations as single data. The \(\beta\)-trick does it with two numbers. To store the triple \((7, 6, 7)\) — the exponents of \(0=0\), say — pick \(c = 8\); the moduli \(1+(i+1)c\) are \(9, 17, 25\), pairwise coprime (any common divisor of two of them divides their difference \((j-i)\cdot 8\) and also an odd number \(1+ic\), hence divides \(j-i \in \{1,2\}\) — and is odd, so is 1), and each exceeds the value it stores. Then one number \(b\) holds all three as remainders: \(b = 907\) is the smallest, and \(907 = 9 \cdot 100 + 7 = 17 \cdot 53 + 6 = 25 \cdot 36 + 7\). Read off: divide, keep the remainder — B01's operation, three times, and a whole sequence is one datum. Both minimality facts — smallest \(c\), then smallest \(b\) — are census receipts, and the machine verified them by search: arithmetic storing arithmetic.

Example 3 · The diagonal's debt repaid — the skeptic becomes arithmetic

Moves used: P14 the flip along the diagonal · P07 the skeptic as auxiliary object · P34 the codebook that lets it quote.

Re-run P14's toy census, with the dictionary open. The universe: three programs on inputs \(1, 2, 3\). Program \(t_1\) halts on every input. Program \(t_2\) loops on every input. Program \(t_3\), the skeptic, on input \(i\), does the opposite of what the currently published predictor table says for program \(i\). A claimed predictor publishes \(p = (p(1), p(2), p(3))\), a prediction of each program's self-run. Eight tables are possible; the machine tried them all: four die at row 1 (wrong about \(t_1\), which always halts), two die at row 2 (right about \(t_1\), wrong about \(t_2\), which always loops), and the last two — the two honest about \(t_1\) and \(t_2\) — die at the skeptic, because \(t_3(3)\) is defined as the opposite of \(p(3)\), whatever \(p(3)\) is. Zero survivors, and the refutation is one line, the same line as the digit machine's: the row built from the table cannot appear in the table.

P14 flagged the debt immediately, and this page pays it. The toy's small print was: \(t_3\) was put in the universe by hand, graciously; the real theorem needs the universe of programs to contain its own skeptics. With the codebook, "publish a table" is "output a number" — encode the three verdicts as a triple like \((7,6,7)\), and the published table is a single integer. "Read entry \(i\)" is division: factor out the \(i\)-th prime's exponent, B01's operation. "Do the opposite" is arithmetic: a verdict is a value, and the opposite is a subtraction from it — \(1 - v\), if verdicts are 0 and 1. So "consult the published table at \(i\) and contradict it" is a fixed arithmetic expression of two numbers — the table's and the input's — and the program computing it is as much a member of the universe as anything else, needing no sponsor at all. The universe of programs is closed under consult-and-contradict because the consulting is dividing and the contradicting is subtracting. That closure, informal in P14, is a construction here; it is the content of the move — Key 3's hook made real: the codebook is what makes quoting storable, and storage is what makes the universe self-contained.

The sentence version of the flip goes one step further, and this step is honestly labeled: sketch, with one borrowed proof. If "provable in the system" arithmetizes — Example 2's checking, scaled — then a substitution trick (the fixpoint lemma: with the codebook in place, any formula \(F(x)\) has a sentence \(G\) that says \(F\) about \(G\)'s own number) builds a sentence \(G\) with \(G \leftrightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{Prov}(\ulcorner G \urcorner)\). If \(G\) were provable, the machine's arithmetic would check a proof of a sentence whose number it can also compute to be refuted — a falsehood recorded as checked, which consistency forbids. So, if the system is consistent, \(G\) is unprovable — and true, in the honest reading, though the proof never needs to say so (Exhibit C explains the careful silence). The fixpoint lemma is the one step this page does not prove; it is the point where the toy census becomes a cited theorem, and the citation is dated 1931. Everything below the sketch — the table-as-number, the read-by-division, the contradict-by-subtraction, the census, the bounds — is checked.

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits

Exhibit A · The map is not the territory

The number \(7{,}290{,}000{,}000\) is even. The sentence \(0=0\) is not "even" — sentences are not the kind of thing that is even. The number is divisible by five; the sentence says nothing about five. Every plain arithmetic fact about a code is an artifact of the codebook: shift the positions by one and all the parities flip. What is not an artifact: the deliberately-built bridges — being a string-number at all (decidable: exponents present and contiguous), being a sentence-number (decidable: run the grammar), being a proof-number (decidable: check the lines). The failure signature is numerology about the dictionary: reasoning from a code's digits, size, or parity to claims about the sentence it names. Decode \(12\) and the point is visible at toy scale: the exponents \((2,1)\) spell \()(\) — a perfectly decodable string that closes before it opens. Decodable is not meaningful; only the bridges carry meaning, and each bridge must be built and checked, never assumed.

Exhibit B · Jams, and the price of honesty

Obligation 1 fails silently if the codebook is careless. Use positions with gaps, or forget a symbol, and "is this number a sentence?" stops being decidable by division — the machine jams at the front door, and everything downstream (proofs, censuses, the diagonal) inherits the jam. The census receipts show the door working: of \(19{,}999\) integers up to 20,000, exactly \(190\) decode to strings, the rest are jams — exponent gaps like \(10 = 2 \cdot 5\), or exponents past 12 — and the machine can tell you which is which, every time. The price is paid in magnitude: three symbols cost \(7.29 \times 10^9\), five can reach \(10^{42}\), and the certification bounds — \(30{,}030^3\) for bracket-free six-packs and \(724{,}851{,}197{,}070\) for bracketed ones — are receipts of how fast the numbers outrun readability. The codebook is a proof device, not storage: real proof assistants encode leaner. But the theorems only need the dictionary to exist, and the censuses only need the small end, where every number is still checkable by hand.

Exhibit C · Tarski's wall

Run Example 3's flip on true instead of provable. Suppose arithmetic had a formula \(\mathrm{True}(x)\) holding exactly of the numbers of true sentences — then the same substitution trick builds a sentence \(L\) with \(L \leftrightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{True}(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\), and the definition of "true" collapses it: \(\mathrm{True}(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\) holds exactly when \(L\) is true, exactly when \(L\) holds, exactly when \(\mathrm{True}(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\) fails. Contradiction — so no such formula exists: truth-in-arithmetic is not arithmetizable. Gödel noted the break in 1931; Tarski proved the theorem in 1933, published in Polish, in German in 1935. The wall is the move's exact boundary, the one Box P34.B marked in advance: provable arithmetizes because proof-checking is mechanical; true does not, because truth is not a syntax relation. This is not a defect of the codebook but its deepest finding — the dictionary covers precisely the mechanical relations, and the difference between what a system can prove and what is true is measurable, from inside, with numbers. The careful silence at the end of Example 3 ("true, in the honest reading") is the wall's shadow: the sentence \(G\) can be seen to be true from outside the system, and can never be proved true inside it.

Scope, honestly. What is machine-checked on this page: the round-trip (\(1{,}884\) strings, zero mismatches), the formula censuses (\(14, 20, 45, 109\) and the full length-\(\le 5\) list), the smallest sentence-number with its three-part certification, the proof census (\(9, 81, 729\)), both \(\beta\) receipts including minimality, and the eight-table census. What is borrowed on trust: the fixpoint lemma, the scaling from Mini-Arithmetic to real arithmetic, and the consistency assumptions in Example 3's final step — cited, dated in §7, and never presented as checked. The theorems here are theorems about the toy; the real theorems belong to the literature, and this page's job was to make their machinery touchable, countable, and small.

6 · The doors this opens

Every pattern page ends by pointing at the doors it passes without opening. Three here, at honest distances.

The nearest door — P35 · Build two worlds. This page found a sentence the axioms cannot prove (if they are consistent). The next question is the older one wearing new clothes: can a sentence be neither provable nor refutable — genuinely independent? The answer is a construction: build two worlds, one where it holds and one where it fails, and the axioms cannot decide it. P35 is the nearest door because the codebook is its raw material: the worlds are built from numbers, and the independence statement is a sentence with a number. Distance: one move. What it carries: the difference between "unprovable so far" and "unprovable ever".

The working door — P44 · Formal verification. Example 2's machine checks without understanding, one line at a time. P44 takes the same discipline to scale: real proofs, machine-checked, every step refereed by a kernel small enough to trust completely. Distance: medium — the toy needs no kernel, but real mathematics does. What it carries: the census's blind checking, industrialized, and the surprising fact that blind checking is more reliable than understanding, because it cannot be talked out of a verdict.

The far door — P49 · Ultraproduct transfer. This page's sentences are about numbers; the two-worlds door builds many worlds at once. P49 is the machinery for moving statements between worlds — what is true almost everywhere is true somewhere idealized — and it is the far door because its infinitary bookkeeping ("ultrafilters") has no toy census this size. Distance: far. What it carries: the model-builder's equivalent of the codebook: a way to make "most worlds" a mathematical object.

Default habit: when a question about sentences, proofs, or programs arrives, ask first which relations arithmetize — the census discipline of §4 — then walk the doors in order: independence needs two worlds (P35), machine-checking needs a kernel (P44), and moving between worlds needs the ultraproduct (P49). The codebook is the ticket to all three.

7 · Where the move came from

Non-load-bearing, and honest about it: the page above works if this section is skipped. But the move has a documented history, and one week of it is stranger than the mathematics suggests.

Königsberg, September 1930. Kurt Gödel — twenty-four, dissertation on completeness just behind him — had found that consistency and decidability fail for the systems of the day, and mentioned it quietly: to Carnap, Feigl, and Waismann on 26 August, then in a conference roundtable on 7 September. It drew one hearer's full attention: John von Neumann, who pulled him aside afterward. The following evening, from the same city, David Hilbert — sixty-eight, retired, the program's founder — broadcast his credo by radio: Wir müssen wissen. Wir werden wissen. — we must know; we will know. The two events were not connected by anyone present. Two months later, from Berlin, von Neumann wrote the young man a letter, dated 20 November 1930: using the methods of Gödel's announced result, he had shown that the consistency of mathematics is itself unprovable — the second theorem, reached independently, arriving as congratulations.

The paper, and the codebook on its page 179. Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I — On formally undecidable propositions — appeared in the Monatshefte für Mathematik und Physik in 1931. Its §2 builds the dictionary this page miniaturizes: odd codes (\(0 \mapsto 1\), \(\neg \mapsto 5\), \(\forall \mapsto 9\), variables at the odd primes), strings as products of prime powers, proofs as sequences of such numbers, sequences packed into pairs by the \(\beta\)-function — Example 2's \((907, 8)\) is its direct descendant, shrunk to census size. The paper's epochal result is Example 3's sketch made rigorous; and Gödel's own footnote records the observation that Exhibit C would make a theorem.

The wall, the machine, the census, the quotation, the bits. Tarski proved the wall — truth is not arithmetizable — in Polish in 1933, in German with a postscript in 1935: the exact boundary of the dictionary, Exhibit C. Turing's 1936 paper built the universal machine — a machine that consults a program-number and obeys it, the codebook as hardware — with a section pointedly titled The application of the diagonal process: P14's flip, made rigorous, again. Kleene in 1943 arithmetized computation itself — the \(T\) predicate, "run \(r\) on input \(x\) ends in output \(y\)", as pure arithmetic — and built the hierarchy that grades which relations arithmetize at all. Quine, in an essay published as "Paradox" in April 1962 (reprinted as The Ways of Paradox), showed the diagonal wearing no numbers at all: "yields a falsehood when appended to its own quotation" yields a falsehood when appended to its own quotation — quotation, not arithmetic, is the load-bearing trick, and the codebook is quotation made mechanical. And Chaitin in July 1974, in the Journal of the ACM, re-measured the limits information-theoretically: some mathematical facts carry more bits than the axioms can hold, so the dictionary's world has walls even for randomly chosen questions. From a roundtable nobody heard to the measured walls of what provability can carry: the move's lineage is part of its meaning.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ · Codes by hand

(a) Compute the numbers of \(\neg 0=0\) and of \(0=S0\). (b) Decode \(2{,}048\) and \(6{,}750{,}000\). (c) Explain why \(10\) is not a string-number at all.

Hint

Codes are positions: look each symbol up in the §2 table, then raise the primes to those exponents in order. For decoding, factor and read exponents off the primes 2, 3, 5, \(\ldots\) in order — and mind the contiguity.

Worked answer

(a) \(\neg 0=0\): exponents \((3, 7, 6, 7)\), so \(2^3 \cdot 3^7 \cdot 5^6 \cdot 7^7 = 225{,}136{,}067{,}625{,}000\). And \(0=S0\): exponents \((7, 6, 8, 7)\), so \(2^7 \cdot 3^6 \cdot 5^8 \cdot 7^7 = 30{,}018{,}142{,}350{,}000{,}000\). (b) \(2{,}048 = 2^{11}\): one symbol, position 11 — the variable \(x\). \(6{,}750{,}000 = 2^4 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5^6\): exponents \((4, 3, 6)\) spell \(\to \neg =\) — a string, and pointedly not a formula: decoding succeeds, meaning does not (Exhibit A). (c) \(10 = 2^1 \cdot 5^1\): the exponent 5 sits at the prime 5, skipping the prime 3 entirely — exponents must be present and contiguous from 2, or there is no string to read. The machine census counts exactly \(190\) string-numbers up to 20,000; \(10\) is one of the \(19{,}809\) jams.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · The census, by productions

(a) Count the \(109\) formulas of length six without any machine, by the four grammar productions. (b) Prove that no formula of length six contains a bracket. (c) Derive the lower bound \(30{,}030^3\) that certification part two uses, and check it against the census.

Hint

An atom \(s=t\) has length \(\mathrm{len}(s) + \mathrm{len}(t) + 1\). For (b): what is the cheapest bracketed term? For (c): bracket-free means no symbol of code 1 or 2 — so what is the smallest possible exponent?

Worked answer

(a) Atoms of length 6 need term lengths \((1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1)\): with \(2, 3, 4, 5\) terms of each length, that is \(2 \cdot 5 + 3 \cdot 4 + 4 \cdot 3 + 5 \cdot 2 = 44\). Negations: \(\neg\) on the \(45\) formulas of length 5: \(45\). Universal quantifiers: \(\forall x\) on the \(16\) of length 4, and \(\forall x'\) on the \(4\) of length 3: \(16 + 4 = 20\). Total \(44 + 45 + 20 = 109\), agreeing with the exhaustive machine census — two methods, one count, the production analysis certified by the odometer. (b) The cheapest bracketed term is \((0+0)\): five symbols. An atom containing one has length at least \(5 + 1 + 1 = 7\); \((F \to G)\) costs at least \(9\); \(\neg\) and \(\forall\) add no brackets themselves, so brackets appear first at length seven. (c) Bracket-free means every symbol's code is at least 3 — the 1 and 2 belong to \((\) and \()\). Six symbols, six primes, exponents at least 3: \(2^3 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5^3 \cdot 7^3 \cdot 11^3 \cdot 13^3 = 30{,}030^3 = 27{,}081{,}081{,}027{,}000\) — and indeed every one of the \(109\) censused numbers exceeds it, by a wide margin.

Exercise 3 ●●● · Why 9, 81, 729 — and why the toy is trivial

(a) Show that the two-line proofs number exactly \(81\): every legal second line is an axiom. (b) Show the three-line count is \(729\) for the same reason. (c) Show that the theorems of this toy are exactly its axioms, and explain what the census of proofs of \(SS0=SS0\) — the sequence \(1, 9, 81\) — reveals about checking versus proving. (d) What is the smallest change that would make the toy prove something that is not an axiom?

Hint

Where does R1 send \(t=t\)? What does R2 do to two lines that are both of the form \(t=t\)? For (d): the axioms are all reflexive; think about what a non-reflexive axiom would license.

Worked answer

(a) A two-line proof starts with an axiom (nine ways). The second line is legal if it is an axiom, or R1 of the first, or R2 of the pair. R1 of \(t=t\) is \(St=St\) — an axiom whenever \(St\) fits the pool, and otherwise not in the pool at all. R2 needs \(a=b\) and \(b=c\); with \(a=b=c\) (all axioms are \(t=t\)) it yields \(t=t\) again. So the legal second lines are always exactly the nine axioms: \(9 \times 9 = 81\). (b) The same argument at the third line: new R1 and R2 candidates from lines one and two are axioms or out-of-pool; the third line's legal values are again the nine axioms: \(81 \times 9 = 729\). (c) By induction over proof length — P66's ladder — every line of every proof is an axiom: R1 preserves "both sides identical", and R2 chains \(t=t\) with \(t=t\). The theorems are exactly the axioms. Meanwhile \(SS0=SS0\) is itself an axiom, so its proofs run \(1, 9, 81\): every legal prefix pads into a proof of it. The census counts proofs, not difficulty — and the machine, blind by design, cannot tell a canonical chain from a padded one; that blindness is what makes checking trustworthy (it cannot be talked out of a verdict) and what makes the census honest (it counts what checking accepts, nothing else). (d) Add one non-reflexive axiom to the pool — say \(S0=0\) is ruled out as false, but \(SS0=S0+S0\) or a defining equation like \(x+0=x\) is not reflexive: now R1 and R2 can reach equations that are not axioms, the theorem set grows past the axiom set, and the census of proofs becomes a census of genuine derivations — the exact shape of real arithmetic.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The storage consultant

A colleague must store the triple \((7, 6, 7)\) in one register pair \((b, c)\) and wants the smallest honest pair. (a) Verify that \((907, 8)\) works and is minimal in this order: smallest \(c\), then smallest \(b\). (b) Do the same for the triple \((4, 3, 6)\). (c) The consultant proposes storing \((7, 6, 7)\) as \((b, c) = (7{,}290{,}000{,}000, 1)\) — the whole number itself. What has the proposal missed?

Hint

The moduli \(1 + (i+1)c\) must be pairwise coprime and each exceed the value it stores; then \(b\) is the smallest simultaneous solution of three congruences — search by machine, or by the method of §4's census. For (c): what does the pair have to survive, that a single stored number does not?

Worked answer

(a) The gate is pairwise coprimality: the moduli \(1 + (i+1)c\) must be pairwise coprime for the recovery to be guaranteed in arithmetic, and the difference argument of Example 2 needs \(c\) divisible by every \(j - i\) — here, \(c\) must be even. Smaller even \(c\) fail on size: \(c = 2\) gives moduli \(3, 5, 7\) (3 cannot store 7); \(c = 4\) gives \(5, 9, 13\) (5 cannot store 7); \(c = 6\) gives \(7, 13, 19\) (a remainder mod 7 is at most 6 — 7 is unreachable). So \(c = 8\): moduli \(9, 17, 25\), pairwise coprime, each strictly exceeding its value. Then the smallest \(b\) solving \(b \equiv 7 \pmod 9\), \(b \equiv 6 \pmod{17}\), \(b \equiv 7 \pmod{25}\) is \(907\): \(907 = 9 \cdot 100 + 7 = 17 \cdot 53 + 6 = 25 \cdot 36 + 7\), machine-checked by search up to the product \(9 \cdot 17 \cdot 25\). (b) For \((4, 3, 6)\): smallest even \(c\) with moduli exceeding their values is \(c = 4\) — moduli \(5, 9, 13\), pairwise coprime — and the smallest \(b\) is \(84\): \(84 = 5 \cdot 16 + 4 = 9 \cdot 9 + 3 = 13 \cdot 6 + 6\), machine-checked. (c) The single number \((7{,}290{,}000{,}000, 1)\) does store the triple — it is \(0=0\)'s own number, exponents intact. What it misses is the pair's proof obligation: the \(\beta\)-trick's value is not storage but uniform recoverability inside arithmetic — the moduli \(1+(i+1)c\) are built to be pairwise coprime by a one-line argument about \(c\), so the existence of a decoding is provable in arithmetic itself, for any sequence, by a single construction. Storing "the number itself" works once, for one triple, by inspection; the \(\beta\)-pair works for every finite sequence, by a theorem — and theorems, not inspections, are what the machine can check.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The wall, by hand

(a) Suppose arithmetic had a formula \(\mathrm{True}(x)\) holding exactly of the numbers of true sentences. Use the page's own substitution trick to derive a contradiction. (b) Which relations does arithmetize instead — name three from this page. (c) The open end: why does the two-worlds door (P35) build models rather than truth-predicates, and what does that say about the wall's permanence?

Hint

For (a): the trick of Example 3 builds, from any \(F(x)\), a sentence \(L\) with \(L \leftrightarrow F(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\) — take \(F(x) = \neg\,\mathrm{True}(x)\) and chase the definitions around the loop. For (c): what could a model verify that no formula inside the system can define?

Worked answer

(a) Let \(F(x)\) be \(\neg\,\mathrm{True}(x)\); the substitution trick yields a sentence \(L\) with \(L \leftrightarrow \neg\,\mathrm{True}(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\). Now unfold: if \(\mathrm{True}\) defines truth, then \(\mathrm{True}(\ulcorner L \urcorner)\) holds exactly when \(L\) is true, exactly when \(L\) holds. So \(L \leftrightarrow \neg L\) — both or neither, contradiction. No such \(\mathrm{True}\) exists: truth-in-arithmetic is not arithmetizable; this is Tarski's theorem (1933/1935), the wall of Exhibit C. Note what died: the definition, not the trick — the same substitution built Gödel's \(G\) happily, because \(\mathrm{Prov}\) is a syntax relation and \(\mathrm{True}\) is not. (b) Provable — Example 2's line-checking; refutable — the same census with \(\neg\) prepended; and every rung of Kleene's 1943 hierarchy: decidable relations, then provable-with-existential-witness, and so on — each level arithmetizes, each strictly narrower than truth. (c) Independence proofs cannot define truth inside the system — the wall forbids it — so they build the next best thing: two worlds, each internally consistent, disagreeing about the statement. A model is a witnessed world, not a truth-predicate; the wall stands, and the discipline is to work around it — two worlds where one definition was wanted — never through it. That trade is the P35 door's whole design, and the wall is why the door exists.
