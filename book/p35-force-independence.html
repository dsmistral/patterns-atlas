The Patterns Atlas · P35 · the first move of tier 4

Build two worlds

To show that no proof will ever come, build two worlds that obey every axiom and disagree about the one statement — and the axioms' silence becomes a theorem, witnessed.

New word: independence. A statement \\(S\\) is independent of an axiom list \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) when the axioms prove neither \\(S\\) nor its negation: the question is real, and the axioms are silent. Silence is hard to prove — no census can count every proof that will ever be written. The device that proves it is a pair of worlds: two structures that interpret the same language, each certified to obey every axiom, one making \\(S\\) true and the other making it false. A proof from the axioms is blind to everything the axioms do not say, so it cannot choose between the worlds; if a proof of \\(S\\) existed, it would hold in every world that obeys the axioms, and one of ours does not. This page builds three such pairs — rival multiplication tables, an ordered field with an infinite element, a curved disk where triangles bulge — certifies each world by machine census, and watches a claim about all proofs ever fall to two finite, checkable objects.

1 · Before we begin

P34 ended with a debt. Its machine found a sentence \\(G\\) — true, in the honest reading, and provable in no consistent system containing arithmetic — and the finding was conditional twice over: if the axioms are consistent, and if "unprovable so far" could be promoted to "unprovable ever". The first condition is not dischargeable from inside (that is the second incompleteness theorem, the deeper of P34's two silences). The second is this page's business, and it is harder than it looks: "no proof exists" is a claim about an ocean of finite objects — every proof that could ever be written — and P25's censuses only ever drained ponds. P11 taught refutation: assume the opponent's claim, run the machine, watch it break. But contradiction needs the opponent to show its face, and "a proof exists" wears a thousand masks.

Other pages hold the tools this page assembles. P01: before arguing about independence, say exactly what the axioms are, what the statement says, and what a world formally is — three pins this page drives before any construction. P34: the codebook — axioms, statements, and proofs are finite syntax, so the question "which proofs exist" is a question about listable objects, and the answer can be a construction. P25: when the possibilities are finite, the machine counts them — every world below is certified by a census, not by plausibility. P03: stress-testing with special cases, upgraded — the special case is here promoted to a full counterworld, the stress-test that proves the theorem it was hired to explore. P07: the world nobody asked for is the auxiliary object par excellence, invented purely to carry a witness no given problem supplied. P11: the closing step is a proof by contradiction in miniature — assume a proof of \\(S\\), and the two worlds must agree, but they do not. And P66: the bridge from "these axioms are obeyed" to "no proof exists" is an induction over proof length — the soundness ladder — climbed once here and then trusted, like every ladder this book builds.

Here is the tension this page resolves. A universal negative about proofs seems to demand an infinite inspection; a construction is finite. The resolution is the axioms' own blindness: a proof can use nothing but the axioms and the rules, so whatever difference two worlds carry beyond the axioms is invisible to every proof, forever. The page's three receipts make that blindness concrete at census size — and the honest fine print is printed up front: the worlds are certified, the soundness ladder is cited, and the one thing this page never claims is that either world is the "real" one. That reserve is not modesty; it is a theorem's worth of discipline, and Exhibit C pays it out.

2 · Meet the problem

The oldest unresolved question in mathematics was a postulate. Euclid's fifth — in the form John Playfair standardized in his 1795 Elements edition, the form this page uses for \\(S\\) — says: through a point not on a line, exactly one line runs parallel to it, meeting it never. Proclus, in the fifth century, already recorded the discomfort: the postulate reads like a theorem awaiting its proof, and for two thousand years geometers tried to give it one — by assuming the rival and hunting for the contradiction, the exact shape of P11. Gerolamo Saccheri took the hunt furthest: his 1733 Euclides ab omni naevo vindicatus — Euclid freed of every flaw — proved theorem after theorem inside the rival geometry, each looking false and none contradictory, and concluded, wrongly, that the rival was "repugnant to the nature of the straight line". Johann Lambert got closer in his 1766 Theorie der Parallellinien, published only after his death: he found that the rival geometry's triangles fall short of 180° by an amount that behaves like an area, and he refused to publish, because he could not find the contradiction and would not say there was none. The question was never "are the theorems pretty" — it was "does a contradiction exist", an ocean-sized universal negative about proofs.

Then the creators stopped asking. Nikolai Lobachevsky published "O nachalakh geometrii" — On the principles of geometry — in the Kazanskii vestnik across 1829–1830, building the rival geometry with full deliberateness. János Bolyai, twenty-one, wrote to his father in late 1823: aus dem Nichts habe ich eine neue, andere Welt erschaffen — out of nothing I have created a strange new world. Gauss had the same world in his private notebooks and advised silence. But none of the three owned a certificate that the new world could exist: their tables of theorems were evidence, not proof, and the old scandal stood — maybe the rival was inconsistent, and Euclid's postulate was a theorem whose proof was merely difficult. What was missing was the observation that reframes everything: you do not certify a world by exploring it. You certify it by building it inside a geometry you already trust, and then the two-worlds argument runs: if the trusted world is consistent, so is the strange one, and no proof from the shared axioms can ever choose between them.

Eugenio Beltrami made that observation rigorous in 1868, with a construction — the Saggio di interpretazione della geometria non-euclidea — that built the rival geometry inside ordinary Euclidean geometry, its points living in a region of the plane, its lines in curves, so that "the parallel postulate fails" became an ordinary, consistent fact about a Euclidean object. Felix Klein recast the construction in projective clothes (1871, in the Mathematische Annalen); Henri Poincaré made it famous with the disk of Example 3 (1882, Théorie des groupes fuchsiens, Acta Mathematica 1:1–62). The definition this history distilled, and this page uses:

Definition (independence). A statement \\(S\\) is independent of an axiom list \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) when \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash S\\) and \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash \\neg S\\): no proof from the axioms ends at \\(S\\), and none ends at its negation. The witness pair that certifies it: two worlds — structures interpreting the language of \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) — with \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash \\mathcal{A}\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash S\\), \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\mathcal{A}\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\neg S\\). Then no proof of \\(S\\) from \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) can exist — a proof would hold in \\(\\mathcal W_2\\), which obeys the axioms and denies \\(S\\) — and symmetrically none of \\(\\neg S\\). One direction of the bridge (two worlds ⇒ no proof) is the soundness of the proof rules, an induction over proof length; the converse (no proof ⇒ worlds exist) is Gödel's completeness theorem, P34's companion and Exhibit C's subject.

Three questions, one device. First: what does it take to certify a world — how do you check that a strange structure obeys the axioms, when "obeying" is exactly what is in doubt? The answer below is P25's: obedience is checked instance by instance, and when the world is finite — or the axiom instances form a finite pool — the check is a census. Second: how small can the worlds be? The smallest separating pair below has three elements each, and the machine that finds them is the same one that proves smaller pairs cannot exist. Third: what survives when the axiom list is infinite — arithmetic itself, where P34's \\(G\\) lives? There the worlds exist (they are called nonstandard models), but they cannot be census-certified, and the honest version of the argument is a construction of the literature. Each question gets a receipt below: rival monoid tables, an ordered field, and a disk — two worlds apiece, all machine-checked, and the universal negative falls in each case.

3 · Name the move

§2 met the problem in its oldest clothes. The move, named:

Pattern P35 — Build two worlds (force independence). When a statement \\(S\\) resists every attempt to prove or refute it from axioms \\(\\mathcal{A}\\), stop hunting the proof and put the axioms on trial instead: (1) pin the language, the axioms, and \\(S\\) exactly — P01's discipline, because "worlds" only makes sense for syntax already pinned; (2) build two worlds: structures \\(\\mathcal W_1, \\mathcal W_2\\) interpreting the language, each certified to obey \\(\\mathcal{A}\\), with \\(S\\) true in \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) and false in \\(\\mathcal W_2\\) — P07's auxiliary objects, hired to disagree; (3) close with soundness: a proof from \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) holds in every world that obeys \\(\\mathcal{A}\\), so the disagreement forbids proofs of \\(S\\) and of \\(\\neg S\\) alike — the universal negative "no proof will ever come" becomes a theorem about two exhibited objects. The silence of the axioms is not observed; it is witnessed.

Or, in words: when the asker demands a verdict and the axioms have none to give, do not keep cross-examining the axioms — build the two societies that obey them and disagree, and the verdict's impossibility is on display. One definition was wanted; two worlds are supplied.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the axioms are pinned and listable. A world obeys exactly the pinned list; "obeys standard mathematics" certifies nothing. Jam-mode: the axiom list is vague, open-ended, or second-order in disguise — then no structure can be checked against it, and the question returns to P01: pin the list before building anything. The monoid page below pins two axioms, the field five, the disk a finite slice of Hilbert's list — small on purpose, and the smallness is honest.

Key 2 — the worlds are certifiable. Obedience must be checkable — by finite census for finite worlds, by a construction theorem for built ones. Jam-mode: the world is too large or too vague to certify (a "world" of all intuitions), or certification needs the very axioms in doubt — then the certificate is circular, and the move jams at the door. Box P35.A below makes certification a machine obligation; Example 3's disk shows the built case, certified inside Euclidean geometry, the trick Beltrami invented.

Key 3 — the disagreement is witnessed at \\(S\\) itself. The two worlds must differ on \\(S\\), not on a proxy: \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash S\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\neg S\\), checked at the statement, element by element where elements exist. Jam-mode: worlds that disagree about something near \\(S\\) — the eternal temptation, since near-disagreements are easier to build. The check below is mechanical: for \\(S = \\) "commutativity", the machine reads \\(a \\cdot b\\) and \\(b \\cdot a\\) off the rival table and finds them unequal — the disagreement at \\(S\\), not near it.

Box P35.A — the machine, in three obligations. Every instance of the pattern — the toy tables of Example 1, Beltrami's disk, Cohen's forcing — owes the same three duties, and the first two are checkable by machine at census scale:

1 · The axiom list is finite and decidable per instance. Each axiom, read inside a world, becomes a checkable claim: \\(2 \\cdot 2 = 4\\) identities in a three-element table; \\(27\\) associativity triples; sign rules for a field of fractions; a geodesic construction for the disk. The receipt: every world below is put through a census of its axiom instances — \\(16\\) and \\(19{,}683\\) candidate tables scanned, \\(32{,}768\\) ordered-field triples multiplied four ways — and passes or fails without interpretation. A world that cannot be census-put fails here, whatever it feels like.

2 · Both worlds are certified — by the same machine. The two witnesses owe identical duty: \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) and \\(\\mathcal W_2\\) each run the full axiom census, and \\(S\\) is read off each. The receipt: both tables of Example 1 pass \\(3^3 = 27\\) associativity checks and \\(6\\) identity checks; the field and the disk, the same machine discipline. Symmetry is the honesty: a standard applied to one world and waived for the other is advocacy, not certification.

3 · Soundness turns evidence into theorem. The closing step — "so no proof exists" — is not more census; it is an induction over proof length, the soundness ladder: every axiom is true in every world obeying \\(\\mathcal{A}\\), and every rule preserves truth-in-a-world, so by P66's ladder every proof is. Checked on the toys below at each rule for each world, borrowed as a theorem beyond them — and the borrow is printed every time it happens, because on this pattern the certificate is the argument.

Box P35.B — where the move appears. The birth certificate — Beltrami's 1868 Saggio, the rival geometry built inside Euclid's so its consistency is Euclid's problem now; Klein's 1871 projective recast; Poincaré's 1882 disk, Example 3's world. The algebraic miniature — the three-element nonabelian monoid of Example 1, independence at the smallest scale a census can separate. The arithmetic worlds — Skolem's 1934 nonstandard models and Robinson's 1961 calculus of infinitesimals, Example 2's field grown into a whole theory. The independence of the continuum hypothesis — Gödel's constructible universe \\(L\\) (announced December 1938), where CH holds; Cohen's forcing (1963–64), where it fails: the two-worlds move at the highest court, and the Fields Medal (1966) its only one for logic. The industry — model checkers and SAT solvers, which build small disagreeing worlds on demand: "no counterexample exists in any reachable state" certified exactly when a machine has enumerated the world that would refute it. The signature in the wild: any claim of the form "no proof / no counterexample will ever exist" — reach for the two worlds first; the next-best alternatives (searched hard, resisted long) are Saccheri's, and they took two centuries to fail.

4 · The move at work — three encounters

Example 1 · Two little worlds — commutativity on trial

Moves used: P01 pinning axioms, statement, and world · P25 the census that finds and certifies both worlds · P07 the rival world as the auxiliary object nobody supplied.

Pin everything first — P01's discipline, and Box P35.A's first obligation. The language has one symbol: a binary operation \\(\\cdot\\). A world is a multiplication table on a finite set. The axiom list \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) has two axioms: associativity — \\((u \\cdot v) \\cdot w = u \\cdot (v \\cdot w)\\) for all \\(u, v, w\\) — and identity — there is an element \\(e\\) with \\(e \\cdot u = u \\cdot e = u\\) for all \\(u\\). A world obeying both is a monoid. The statement on trial, \\(S\\): commutativity, \\(a \\cdot b = b \\cdot a\\) for all \\(a, b\\). The question: does \\(\\mathcal{A} \\vdash S\\)? Does associativity, with an identity to lean on, force the table to be symmetric?

The census answers in two movements, both machine-run. At order 2 — two elements — the \\(2^4 = 16\\) candidate tables collapse to \\(4\\) monoids, and all four are abelian: \\(0\\) nonabelian witnesses exist. Order 2 is too small to separate — the census is silent here, and Exhibit A returns to that silence. At order 3 the separation arrives: \\(3^9 = 19{,}683\\) candidate tables collapse to \\(33\\) monoids — \\(27\\) abelian, \\(6\\) nonabelian. The machine has found the pair of worlds; here they are, side by side, with \\(\\mathbb{Z}/3\\) renamed \\(\\{e, a, b\\}\\) for the abelian witness \\(\\mathcal W_1\\), and the "right-pass" monoid for the nonabelian one \\(\\mathcal W_2\\):

Both tables pass the same census — Box P35.A's second obligation, symmetrically applied. \\(\\mathcal W_1\\): identity row and column \\(e\\), all \\(27\\) associativity triples hold (it is \\(\\mathbb{Z}/3\\), addition renamed), and \\(a \\cdot b = e = b \\cdot a\\): abelian, \\(\\mathcal W_1 \\vDash S\\). \\(\\mathcal W_2\\): identity holds (\\(e\\)'s row and column read the element back), all \\(27\\) triples hold — and the disagreement sits at \\(S\\) itself, not near it: \\(a \\cdot b = b\\) while \\(b \\cdot a = a\\). \\(\\mathcal W_2 \\vDash \\neg S\\). Key 3's machine check, done: two entries read off one table, unequal.

Now close the move — and watch the two censuses play their different roles. \\(\\mathcal W_2\\) already proves \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash S\\) forever, by soundness: any proof from \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) would hold in every monoid, and it fails in \\(\\mathcal W_2\\). But the machine can also be pointed at the proofs themselves, \\(P34\\)-style: over the pool of words in \\(\\{a, b\\}\\) of length at most \\(3\\) plus the empty word \\(E\\), the axioms license exactly \\(38\\) instances — \\(30\\) identity steps \\(wE = w\\), \\(Ew = w\\), and \\(8\\) associativity steps \\((uv)w = u(vw)\\) — and every one flattens to a triviality once brackets and \\(E\\) are dropped. One more derivational step, applied to everything: \\(290\\) size-\\(2\\) derivations — \\(38\\) by symmetry, \\(100\\) by transitivity, \\(152\\) by congruence — with \\(31\\) distinct conclusions, and every one of them a triviality too: the derived equations all collapse to word-equalities the bracket-drop already knew, and \\(ab = ba\\) is not among them, nor anything that could carry it — the machine-checked fact that at depth two, the statement on trial never even enters the record. The relation between the two receipts is the whole page in one line: the proof census says \\(S\\) is not provable at size 2; the witness world says it is not provable at any size — P25's pond, and the ocean behind it, certified by one three-element table and the soundness ladder of Box P35.A's third obligation.

Example 2 · An infinite element — Archimedes on trial

Moves used: P03 the special case promoted to a counterworld · P25 the axiom census that certifies the field · P07 the element nobody asked for.

Pin the trial — P01 again, then Box P35.A's first obligation. The language is the language of ordered fields: \\(+\\), \\(\\times\\), \\(0\\), \\(1\\), \\(<\\). The axioms \\(\\mathcal{A}\\): the field axioms, plus the order axioms, plus the two compatibility axioms — sums and products of positive elements are positive. The statement \\(S\\): Archimedes' principle — for every element \\(y\\) there is a natural number \\(n\\) with \\(n \\cdot 1 > y\\); no element outruns counting itself. Every schoolbook field obeys it, which is exactly why it is worth putting on trial.

Build the counterworld \\(\\mathcal W_2\\) — an ordered field of rational functions. Its elements are fractions \\(\\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\\) of polynomials with real coefficients, \\(q \\neq 0\\); its operations are the school ones. The only design decision is the order, and it is the whole trick: \\(\\frac{p}{q} > 0\\) when, far out at \\(+\\infty\\), the fraction is eventually positive — formally, when the leading coefficients of \\(p\\) and \\(q\\) have the same sign. One rule decides everything; nothing else is touched.

Certify the world — the census, Box P35.A's second obligation, symmetrically applied. The machine built the pool of \\(32\\) elements: numerators of degree at most \\(1\\) with coefficients in \\(\\{-1, 0, 1\\}\\), over the four denominators \\((1)\\), \\((x)\\), \\((x-1)\\), \\((x+1)\\). Against that pool it ran every ordered-field axiom it could reach: \\(1{,}024\\) ordered pairs through four checks each, \\(32{,}768\\) ordered triples through four checks each — associativity and commutativity of both operations, distributivity, order-compatibility — and \\(1{,}024\\) positive-product checks. Zero failures. And \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) — the ordinary field \\(\\mathbb{Q}\\), say — passes the same census with the same machine: for \\(t = \\frac{1}{n}\\), the smallest \\(k\\) with \\(kt > 1\\) is exactly \\(n + 1\\) (checked \\(n = 1 \\ldots 12\\), witnesses \\(k = 2 \\ldots 13\\)) — counting always catches up, there.

The disagreement, at \\(S\\) itself. In \\(\\mathcal W_2\\), the element \\(x\\) outruns every natural number: \\(x > n\\) for \\(n = 1, \\ldots, 50\\), machine-checked, and for every \\(n\\) at once, since \\(x - n\\) has leading coefficient \\(1 > 0\\). Its reciprocal \\(\\varepsilon = \\frac{1}{x}\\) is the old dream of infinitesimal analysis: \\(0 < \\varepsilon < \\frac{1}{n}\\) for every natural \\(n\\), and \\(n \\cdot \\varepsilon < 1\\) — positive, smaller than every schoolbook fraction, and never zero. The census collects the stranger receipts: \\(\\varepsilon^2 < \\varepsilon\\) with \\(0 < \\varepsilon^2\\); \\((1-\\varepsilon) \\cdot \\frac{x}{x-1} = 1\\) exactly; and the geometric-series identity \\(\\frac{1}{1-\\varepsilon} - (1 + \\varepsilon + \\varepsilon^2 + \\varepsilon^3 + \\varepsilon^4) = \\frac{\\varepsilon^5 x}{x-1}\\) — the sum stopping one term short of the limit, the residue computed exactly, a P69–B11 receipt in a world school never mentions. Soundness closes both directions: \\(\\mathbb{Q}\\) forbids proofs of \\(\\neg S\\), the field of fractions forbids proofs of \\(S\\), and Archimedes' principle is independent of the ordered-field axioms. Neither world is counterfeit; Exhibit B takes that seriously.

Example 3 · The disk — Euclid's postulate on trial

Moves used: P07 the disk as the auxiliary world Beltrami invented · P25 the angle and geodesic censuses · P01 pinning "parallel" before disagreeing about it.

The original trial, now with the machine watching. The axioms \\(\\mathcal{A}\\): neutral geometry — the finite slice of Euclid and Hilbert that never mentions parallels: points and lines, betweenness, congruence, the plane axioms. The statement \\(S\\), Playfair's form of the fifth postulate: through a point \\(P\\) not on a line \\(\\ell\\), exactly one line runs parallel to \\(\\ell\\). World \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) is the Euclidean plane, which obeys \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) and \\(S\\) both — the two-thousand-year default. World \\(\\mathcal W_2\\) is the Poincaré disk, and it is built, not found: points are the interior of the unit circle; lines are diameters and arcs of circles meeting the boundary at right angles; angles are measured as the Euclidean angles between arcs — the disk is conformal, which is why angle censuses are honest here. Beltrami's transfer owns the consistency risk: the disk is a region of the ordinary plane with curves in it, so if Euclidean geometry is consistent, the disk world exists — Exhibit C keeps that ledger.

Certify the disk — Box P35.A's first two obligations, disk edition. The machine runs the geodesic construction: the circle through two interior points \\(P, Q\\), orthogonal to the boundary, has center \\(C\\) solving \\(2P \\cdot C = |P|^2 + 1\\) and \\(2Q \\cdot C = |Q|^2 + 1\\) — two linear equations, one circle, always a solution inside the geometry. Then the angle census, on triangles \\(T(s)\\) with vertices \\((0,0)\\), \\((s,0)\\), \\((0,s)\\): \\(T(0.5)\\) — Fig P35.1's triangle — has angles \\(90.0000° + 30.9638° + 30.9638° = 151.9275°\\), defect \\(28.0725°\\); and the defect in radians is the area — \\(0.4900\\), the Gauss–Bonnet law at census scale, cited as a theorem, checked as a number. \\(T(0.9)\\): sum \\(101.9851°\\), defect \\(78.0149°\\), area \\(1.3616\\). And the local-flatness receipt, the one that says the disk obeys the local axioms honestly: \\(T(0.25)\\) sums to \\(172.8473°\\), \\(T(0.1)\\) to \\(178.8541°\\), \\(T(0.01)\\) to \\(179.9885°\\) — defect ratios against \\(T(0.5)\\) of \\(0.2548\\), \\(0.0408\\), \\(0.0004\\): halve the triangle, quarter the defect, the signature of a world that is Euclidean in the small and curved in the large, exactly as neutral geometry requires.

The parallel census — the disagreement, at \\(S\\). Fix \\(\\ell\\) as the horizontal diameter and \\(P = (0.2, 0.5)\\). Every geodesic through \\(P\\) is a circle whose center \\(C = (c_x, c_y)\\) lies on the line \\(0.4c_x + c_y = 1.29\\) — the orthogonality condition at \\(P\\), one equation. The machine sampled \\(39\\) centers across it and sorted every circle into two exact piles. \\(19\\) with \\(|c_x| < 1\\) escape — they bend away from \\(\\ell\\) and run to the ideal boundary without meeting it, two witnesses drawn in Fig P35.3. \\(18\\) with \\(|c_x| > 1\\) cross \\(\\ell\\) strictly inside the disk, at \\(x = c_x - \\mathrm{sgn}(c_x)\\sqrt{c_x^2 - 1} \\in (-1, 1)\\) — the sampled center \\(c_x = 1.5\\), for instance, lands its crossing at \\(x = 0.3820\\), the marked point of the figure. And the knife-edge is printed, not hidden: the two remaining circles, with \\(|c_x| = 1\\), graze \\(\\ell\\) exactly at its ideal endpoints \\(x = \\pm 1\\) — the asymptotic parallels, meeting at infinity and nowhere else, the precise boundary between the two piles. Not one circle did anything else. The dichotomy is exact, and Playfair's "exactly one" dies of abundance: through \\(P\\) there are not one but at least nineteen parallels — nineteen escapes, before even counting the two that die trying. The disk obeys \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) and refutes \\(S\\); the plane obeys \\(\\mathcal{A}\\) and asserts it; soundness closes, and the two-thousand-year hunt is over by construction — no proof of the fifth postulate from neutral geometry will ever exist.

Read the receipt against the history of §2. Lambert, refusing to publish, had found that the rival triangles fall short of 180° by an amount behaving like an area; the disk's census hands back the same number twice — defect \\(28.0725°\\), area \\(0.4900\\) — the suspicion now an equation. Bolyai's "strange new world out of nothing" here has coordinates, a construction rule, and a machine-checkable census. And the oldest lesson is printed in the figure: the disk's geodesics are curved because the axioms never said straight — Key 1 again, the blindness of the axioms, now drawn to scale.

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits

Exhibit A · The census is not the certificate

Order 2 said "all four monoids are abelian", and for one seductive moment it looks like evidence for \\(S\\) — four worlds checked, four agreements. It is nothing of the kind, and the page's own funnel (Fig P35.2) is the retraction: a census records what exists in the pool it drained, and order \\(2\\) is a pool with no nonabelian fish in it. The certificate is never "many cases agree"; it is, exactly, Box P35.A's second obligation discharged twice: two worlds, one certificate each, one disagreement between them. Confusing the two is the pattern's signature failure — it re-runs Saccheri's mistake with a machine's confidence. The machine cannot make the distinction; only the move can, and the funnel's \\(0\\) at order 2 is printed in green-less gray precisely so the \\(6\\) at order 3 can be read as what it is: the moment the pool got big enough to separate. Lesson, generalizable at no extra charge: before reading any census as a verdict, ask which side of the funnel it drained — the candidates, the survivors, or the pair that disagrees.

Exhibit B · The worlds are strange — and real

The two-worlds move builds its witnesses from whatever is at hand, and what is at hand is never familiar. The disk's "lines" are circles; its triangles wear holes in their angle sums; its parallels fan out through a single point by the dozen. The field \\(\\mathbb{R}(x)\\) contains an element \\(x\\) bigger than every counting number and an \\(\\varepsilon\\) smaller than every fraction yet bigger than zero — school intuition files both under "impossible", and the census files both under "checked, \\(32{,}768\\) triples, zero failures". Box P35.B's roster says the same of its elders: Skolem built a world in the 1930s whose "real numbers" can be listed one by one — countable — while satisfying every sentence the true reals satisfy. The strangeness is not a bug to be apologized for; it is the load-bearing material. A world close enough to intuition to feel obvious would agree with intuition about \\(S\\) too, and certify nothing. The professional habit the pattern teaches: when a proposed world feels too strange to exist, do not consult feelings — consult the census. Obedience to the pinned axioms is the only citizenship a world needs, and geometry's strangest citizen, the disk, holds it in perfect standing.

Exhibit C · Independence is not truth

The deepest caveat, and the one the move itself cannot discharge. Two worlds certify that the axioms prove neither \\(S\\) nor \\(\\neg S\\). They say nothing about whether \\(S\\) is true — truth is a question about the world, and the move answered a question about the axioms. The continuum hypothesis is the standing reminder: Gödel's constructible universe \\(L\\) (announced December 1938) is a world where CH holds; Cohen's forcing (1963–64) builds worlds where it fails; and after both — the two-worlds certificate complete, the Fields Medal awarded — the plain question "but is it true?" stood exactly where it stood before, which is why it still stands. There is a second caveat underneath the first: the whole argument is conditional. If the axioms themselves are inconsistent, everything is provable and the two worlds were never both certifiable; Beltrami's transfer moves the disk's risk onto Euclid's shoulders but does not eliminate it — consistency is assumed at the bottom of every stack, the honest price of Box P35.A's third obligation. And the converse debt, recorded in §2: this page used soundness (two worlds ⇒ no proof); the mirror statement (no proof ⇒ two worlds exist) is Gödel's completeness theorem, and it belongs to P34's side of the family. The pattern's discipline: independence is a theorem about proofs; it is never a verdict about reality, and the page that forgets this starts selling Exhibit C's silence as an answer.

What is machine-checked on this page: both monoid censuses (\\(16 \\to 4 + 0\\), \\(19{,}683 \\to 33 = 27 + 6\\)) with both witness tables' \\(27 + 6\\) checks; the proof census (\\(38\\) instances, \\(290\\) derivations, \\(31\\) conclusions, \\(ab = ba\\) in none); the ordered-field census (\\(1{,}024\\) pairs, \\(32{,}768\\) triples, \\(1{,}024\\) products, zero failures) with every receipt of Example 2, and the \\(\\mathbb{Q}\\) side \\(n = 1 \\ldots 12\\); the disk's geodesic construction, the angle censuses (\\(151.9275°\\), \\(101.9851°\\), the flatness ratios \\(0.2548, 0.0408, 0.0004\\)), and the \\(39\\)-circle dichotomy with its meet-point \\(x = 0.3820\\). What is borrowed on trust: the soundness ladder (induction over proof length, P66, checked at each rule on the toys, granted as a theorem beyond them); the Gauss–Bonnet area law (checked as two agreeing numbers, cited as a theorem); Beltrami's transfer and the consistency assumptions underneath it; and the literature's independence results (CH, nonstandard arithmetic), dated in §7 and never presented as checked. The theorems here are theorems about pinned toys; the real theorems belong to their builders, and this page's job was to make the device touchable — two small worlds at a time.

6 · The doors this opens

Every pattern page ends by pointing at the doors it passes without opening. Three here, at honest distances.

The working door — P44 · Formal verification. Box P35.B's industry builds small worlds on demand: model checkers enumerate the states of a circuit or protocol and return "no counterexample" — a two-worlds certificate run by machine, where the disagreeing world either exists and is exhibited, or provably cannot. P44 takes the same discipline to full mathematics: proofs refereed line by line by a kernel small enough to trust completely. Distance: one tier step — the census becomes the kernel, the world becomes the witness. What it carries: the method by which "no proof exists" turns from a survey into a checkable claim.

The far door — P49 · Ultraproduct transfer. This page built two worlds; the far machinery builds all worlds at once and votes across them — what holds in almost every world holds in some idealized one, and statements move between worlds along the vote. Distance: far — the bookkeeping is infinitary and has no census this size. What it carries: the model-builder's step past "two worlds": a geometry of worlds themselves.

The widening door — P62 · Sheafify on a site. The two-worlds habit at its widest: when the objects you have refuse to glue, invent the space where they already do — a world built to make a local story global, which is what every witness here secretly was. Distance: widening — it is the same act of construction, one abstraction higher, and the reader who has certified two worlds already owns the move's nerve. What it carries: the right to answer a hard question about given objects by building the setting the question wanted.

Default habit: when a claim of the form "no proof / no counterexample will ever exist" arrives, do not search harder — ask for the two worlds: which pair of certified structures disagrees at the statement itself? If nobody has them, the claim is Saccheri's: two centuries of confidence and no certificate. If they exist, read the certificate like this page does — both censuses, one disagreement, soundness last.

7 · Where the move came from

Non-load-bearing, and honest about it: the page above works if this section is skipped. But the move has a two-thousand-year paper trail, and its early chapters are the best warning label the pattern has.

The struggle before the device. Proclus, writing in the fifth century, already records attempts to purge Euclid's fifth postulate from the Elements — it reads like a theorem, and geometers treated it as one that had merely misplaced its proof. John Playfair's 1795 edition gave the postulate the form this page used for \\(S\\). Girolamo Saccheri's 1733 Euclides ab omni naevo vindicatus mounted the refutation hunt in earnest — assume the rival, find the contradiction — and found, theorem after theorem, a geometry that behaved strangely and contradicted nothing; he published a verdict of "repugnant" that his own lemmas did not support. Johann Lambert's 1766 Theorie der Parallellinien, published posthumously, went further: the rival triangles' angle-shortfall behaved exactly like an area, and Lambert saw what that meant — a world with its own scale — and shelved the manuscript. The device was missing, and without it, the finest geometers could only search harder, which is the one thing this pattern says never settles the question.

The creators, and the missing certificate. Nikolai Lobachevsky built the rival geometry deliberately and published it as "O nachalakh geometrii" — On the principles of geometry — in the Kazanskii vestnik across 1829–1830. János Bolyai, twenty-one, wrote his father in late 1823 with the sentence the whole subject keeps: aus dem Nichts habe ich eine neue, andere Welt erschaffen — out of nothing I have created a strange new world; the world appeared in print in 1832, as an appendix to a book of his father's. Gauss had the same geometry in his notebooks and advised silence. All three had the theorems. None had the certificate — the proof that the strange world could exist — and without it, their geometry stood one undiscovered contradiction from vanishing.

The device, made rigorous. Eugenio Beltrami's 1868 Saggio di interpretazione della geometria non-euclidea — Giornale di Matematiche 6, 284–312 — supplied it: the rival world built inside a region of the ordinary plane, so that its consistency became Euclid's problem, transferred and honest. Felix Klein recast the construction projectively in 1871 (Über die sogenannte Nicht-Euklidische Geometrie, in the Mathematische Annalen), giving the disk its cross-ratio wardrobe. Henri Poincaré's 1882 Théorie des groupes fuchsiens (Acta Mathematica 1, 1–62, December) gave the disk of Example 3 its angles — conformal, census-friendly, and since the most-built world in mathematics. In one decade, independence stopped being a scandal and became a certificate.

The logician's century. Thoralf Skolem in 1934 built the first nonstandard models of arithmetic — worlds satisfying arithmetic's first-order axioms with elements past every counting number, Example 2's idea at arithmetic scale. Abraham Robinson's 1961 paper (Non-standard analysis, KNAW Proceedings A 64, 432–440) made the infinitesimal world a working calculus — Leibniz's \\(dx\\), cleared after two centuries of Berkeley's complaints, by the two-worlds device. And the device went to its highest court: Kurt Gödel announced in December 1938 (PNAS 24, 556–557) the constructible universe \\(L\\), a world of set theory where the continuum hypothesis holds; Paul Cohen, in two 1963–64 papers (PNAS 50, 1143–1148, then Part II in 51), built worlds where it fails — forcing, the two-worlds move industrialized, recognized with the Fields Medal in 1966, the only one logic has received. From Proclus's marginalia to a medal in Paris: the pattern's history is the story of mathematics learning that some silences are theorems, and that the way to prove one is to build.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The diagnosis — cancellation

A colleague claims: "cancellation — \\(a \\cdot b = a \\cdot c\\) implies \\(b = c\\) — follows from associativity and identity; the left side pins the right." You own this page's move. Diagnose the claim: is it provable, refutable, or independent? Hint

Worked answer

Independent — and the smallest worlds suffice, which is the exercise's point. \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash\\) cancellation: take the two-element monoid \\(\\{e, a\\}\\) with \\(a \\cdot a = a\\) (identity and all \\(8\\) associativity triples hold); then \\(a \\cdot e = a = a \\cdot a\\) with \\(e \\neq a\\) — cancellation fails, witnessed at order \\(2\\). \\(\\mathcal{A} \\nvdash \\neg\\) cancellation: \\(\\mathbb{Z}/2\\), the other two-element monoid, is a group and cancels perfectly; so does \\(\\mathcal W_1\\) of Example 1. Soundness closes both directions, and the verdict is in. The lesson Example 1's funnel under-teaches: the order-\\(2\\) census was silent about commutativity but is decisive about cancellation — the two-element monoids separate it, with \\(a \\cdot a = a\\) on one side and \\(a \\cdot a = e\\) on the other. Separating power is a property of the pair (axioms, statement), not of the pool size: a census is silent or eloquent depending on what you ask it, and the diagnosis skill is knowing which questions a given pond can answer. That is why the pattern builds worlds on demand instead of enlarging censuses on faith.

Exercise 5 ◐ · The other failure — the sphere

Playfair's statement fails in two directions: too many parallels (the disk), or none at all. Explore the sphere: points on the surface, "lines" as great circles. (a) Show the angle sum of a spherical triangle exceeds \\(180°\\), and compute one. (b) What breaks when you try to use the sphere as the second world for \\(S\\) — which axioms of neutral geometry does it refuse? Hint

For (a): a great-circle triangle, say with a vertex at the north pole and two right angles there, is already enough. For (b): how many great circles join the north pole to the south pole? Worked answer

(a) Take the triangle with vertices: north pole, \\((1, 0, 0)\\), \\((0, 1, 0)\\) — two meridians from the pole meeting the equator, plus a quarter of the equator. Both pole angles are \\(90°\\), and the equator corner is \\(90°\\): the sum is \\(270°\\), excess \\(90°\\), and the excess is the area — the sphere runs the disk's defect law with the sign flipped, one more letter in Lambert's drawer.
