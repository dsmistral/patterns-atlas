The Patterns Atlas · P28 · the ninth move of tier 3

Conformal mapping

Some questions are honest in one shape of region and impossible in another — steady heat in a half-plane is a one-line formula, the same heat in a plate between tangent circles a mystery — and the physics never differs. The move: bend the plane itself, angle by angle, until the region where the problem is hard becomes a region where it is easy, solve it there, and bend the answer back. The bending is not a metaphor but a function, and it preserves exactly the quantities the problem cares about, no more. Shapes, it turns out, are how the plane does algebra's work.

New word: conformal — angle-preserving. A map \(w = f(z)\) of the plane is conformal at a point where it is complex-differentiable throughout a neighborhood and the derivative \(f'\) does not vanish there. The condition buys two effects at once: infinitesimal lengths are rescaled by \(\bigl|f'\bigr|\) — a scale that may vary from point to point — and infinitesimal directions are rotated by \(\arg f'\), one fixed rotation per point. Cross a conformal map with two curves and the angle at which they meet survives; their sizes and positions need not. P27 built the calculus that makes this condition checkable: the derivative is exactly the instrument that measures both effects.
New word: harmonic — the two-direction balance. A function \(u(x,y)\) is harmonic on a region where the Laplacian vanishes: \[ \Delta u \;=\; \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} \;=\; 0. \] It is the equation of steady temperature in a plate with no sources or sinks, of electrostatic potential in a charge-free region, of the velocity potential of incompressible irrotational flow — one equation, three physics. Its signature property: the value at the center of any small circle is the average of the values on the circle, which is why a harmonic function can have no interior peak — temperature extremes live on the boundary, and the boundary's word is law.
New word: simply connected — no holes. A region is simply connected when every loop drawn in it can be shrunk to a point without leaving the region. Disks, half-planes, wedges, strips, the quarter-plane of the encounter: simply connected. The annulus \(\{1 < |z| < 2\}\): not — a loop around the inner circle cannot shrink past the hole. The distinction will police this page's scope precisely where it hurts.

1 · Before we begin

P27 taught us to let the variable off the real line and read the arithmetic off where the object breaks — and its closing commission named this page, verbatim: "up the tier — conformal mapping (P28, planned): where the plane's geometry starts doing algebra's work, bending one region onto another until the break lands where it prices best. This page's readers will recognize the gear-shifting when it arrives." P16 taught the older, plainer half of the same thought: a problem may be the same problem in different clothes, and translating between wardrobes is a mathematical act. This page is where the two lessons fuse: the wardrobe, for whole regions of the plane, is a function.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Steady heat in a disk or half-plane is honest bookkeeping: the temperature is an integral of the boundary data, and P24's wave technology does the weighing. Move the same physics into a corner, a corridor, the pocket between two tangent circles — and the bookkeeping dies, not because the equation changed (it never changes) but because the region's shape refuses the computation. The obstruction is geometric. So is the cure. One charter theorem — quoted on this page, proved nowhere on it — says that every simply connected region except the whole plane can be carried onto the disk by a map that is one-to-one, onto, and conformal at every interior point; the practical art is the dictionary of such maps that can actually be written down. This page installs the dictionary and the habit that comes with it: when the region resists, bend it.

2 · The encounter

Take a metal plate shaped like a quarter-plane — the infinite ninety-degree corner \(x > 0\), \(y > 0\). Hold the horizontal wall at \(0\) degrees and the vertical wall at \(100\), let the plate reach steady state, and read a thermometer.

The reading, machine-checked and surprising, depends on the angle alone. At any point whose polar angle is \(15°\), the thermometer reads \(16.67\); at \(30°\), \(33.33\); at \(45°\), \(50\); at \(60°\), \(66.67\); at \(75°\), \(83.33\). And not one of these numbers moves when the point slides along its ray toward or away from the corner: at radius \(0.01\), at \(1\), at \(100\), the same reading (Fig P28.1). The whole table fits one line, \(T = 100\,\theta/90\), with the angle \(\theta\) in degrees: the temperature is a protractor.

Now flatten the plate and shrink the data. Same half-plane, but the hot wall is only a finite stretch: the segment from \(-1\) to \(1\) on the real axis held at \(100\), the rest of the boundary held at \(0\). One unit above the segment's midpoint, the thermometer reads \(50\). Two units up: \(29.52\). Ten units up: \(6.35\). Distance, invisible a moment ago, now decides everything — and it is not a sliding scale but a weighing: the reading tracks the angle the segment subtends at the point, an angle that genuinely depends on where you stand.

Same equation, same material, same physics — opposite temperaments. And the surprise cuts against instinct: the plate with the corner, which looks harder, is the one with the one-line law. The explanation is better than a formula. The corner is a folded-up copy of the half-plane — its two walls are the two halves of a flat boundary — and there is a way to undo the fold that preserves exactly what heat cares about, nothing else. The thermometer was reading the geometry all along (Fig P28.1); the encounter's question is what, precisely, the geometry was hiding.

3 · The pattern

Pattern P28 — Conformal mapping. When a boundary question — steady temperature, potential, flow — lives in a region where it cannot be computed, look for a conformal bijection \(f\) from the region onto a model region (the half-plane, the disk, the strip): one-to-one, onto, holomorphic, with \(f' \neq 0\) at every interior point. Transport the boundary data through \(f\), solve in the model, and read the answer back. What licenses the trip is the conformal covariance of the Laplacian: \[ \Delta(u \circ f) \;=\; \bigl((\Delta u) \circ f\bigr)\cdot\bigl|f'\bigr|^{2}, \] so a harmonic \(u\) stays harmonic under composition — zero times anything nonzero is zero — and the maximum principle pins the composed solution to its transported boundary values. What survives the trip: harmonicity, boundary values (when the map extends to the boundary), crossing angles, and the ratio-structure of boundary data. What does not: distances, areas, densities — each is scaled by the point-varying factor \(\bigl|f'\bigr|^{2}\), so a claim about them must be read back through the map, never assumed. The elementary dictionary: powers \(z^{\pi/\alpha}\) straighten corners, the exponential \(e^{z}\) straightens strips, and Möbius maps \(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\) take lines and circles to lines and circles, the half-plane onto the disk.

Or, in words: bend the plane until the boundary is simple; solve there; unbend. The bending preserves exactly the balance the physics asks of it, heat cannot feel that it happened, and the geometry does the algebra's work.

The covariance formula is the engine, so here it is verified on one honest instance rather than trusted. Take \(u(w) = |w|^{2}\) — deliberately not harmonic, \(\Delta u = 4\) — and \(f(z) = z^{2}\), so \(u \circ f = |z|^{4}\) and \(\bigl|f'(z)\bigr|^{2} = 4|z|^{2}\). The left side of the formula, \(\Delta\bigl(|z|^{4}\bigr)\), reads \(16|z|^{2}\); the right side, \(4 \cdot 4|z|^{2}\), reads \(16|z|^{2}\) too — equal at every point, and machine-checked at \(z = 0.7 + 1.3i\), where both sides read \(34.88\). Harmonicity is a statement about zeros of the Laplacian, the map multiplies the Laplacian by a factor that never vanishes, and that is the whole trick: the engine does not even need \(u\) harmonic to run — being harmonic simply means both sides are zero together.

Box P28.A — the three obligations. A bending is a certificate, and three cheap checks police it. First, name the map and check the three conditions — one-to-one, onto, \(f' \neq 0\) inside — because a map asserted without its checks is a wardrobe with no door. Second, transport the data honestly: boundary to boundary, the model's corners matched to the boundary's break-points, and the extension to the boundary stated as a hypothesis, not smuggled (Exhibit C prices exactly this). Third, read back carefully: anything measured in lengths or areas must pass through the point-varying scale \(\bigl|f'\bigr|\) on the way home; only the angle-zero quantities may be quoted as-is.

The three-key diagnosis. Claim type: the value or shape of a harmonic function with given boundary data, in a region where direct computation is hopeless. Problem shape: the region's boundary is built from lines, circles, wedges, strips — shapes the dictionary covers, or reaches by a short composition. Tools at hand: the covariance of the Laplacian, the maximum principle for uniqueness, and the model regions' formulas — the angle law, and the Poisson integral by which P24's waves weigh flat boundary data.

How you could have found this move: every time the disk version of your problem is a one-liner and your version is not, the obstruction is shape — and shape, in the plane, is negotiable. The discovery route is the encounter's: notice that the corner's law is a protractor law, suspect the corner of being a folded half-plane, and compute \(z^{2}\) to see the fold undo. The three exemplars run that loop on the dictionary's three workhorses.

4 · Three exemplars

Exemplar 1 · The corner, un-bent

Return to the quarter-plane and ask what \(z \mapsto z^{2}\) actually does to it. Squaring doubles arguments: the ray at polar angle \(\theta\) lands on the ray at angle \(2\theta\), so the quarter-plane's rays \(0° < \theta < 90°\) fan out to cover the entire upper half-plane \(0° < \theta < 180°\). The corner unbends into a flat wall: the two walls of the quarter-plane become the two halves of the real axis, and the interior point \(1+i\) lands at \(2i\) (Fig P28.2). One-to-one on the quarter-plane, onto the half-plane, derivative \(2z\) nonzero away from the corner — all three obligations check in one line each.

Now price the thermometer. In the half-plane with the positive ray held at \(0\) and the negative ray at \(100\), the steady answer is the angle law \(T(w) = 100\,\operatorname{Arg} w/\pi\). Compose with the unbending: for \(z\) at polar angle \(\theta\), \(\operatorname{Arg}(z^{2}) = 2\theta\), so \[ T(z) \;=\; 100\cdot\frac{2\theta}{180°} \;=\; 100\cdot\frac{\theta}{90°}, \] the encounter's mystery law, derived. The corner's protractor temperament is the half-plane's angle law heard through the doubling. The same derivation unbends any corner: the wedge of angle \(\alpha\) goes to the half-plane under \(z^{\pi/\alpha}\), giving \(T = 100\,\theta/\alpha\), machine-checkable at every angle — at \(\theta = 30°\) in the ninety-degree corner, \(T = 33.33\), exactly the encounter's table.

One honest footnote. The map's derivative vanishes at the corner itself, and that is not a defect but the point: the corner is the one place where angles are supposed to change — the corner's \(90°\) opening into the wall's \(180°\) — and the bending spends its single degenerate point exactly where the geometry needed it bent (Fig P28.2). The obligations ask for a nonzero derivative at every interior point of the region; the corner is boundary, and there the map is allowed its one miracle.

Exemplar 2 · The half-plane becomes a disk

The dictionary's second workhorse is a bridge between the two model regions. The half-plane is unbounded and good for flat data; the disk is bounded and good for arcs and symmetry. Between them stands the simplest nontrivial map in the book: \[ w \;=\; \frac{z-i}{z+i}. \] Check it the way every Möbius map deserves, by honest substitution (Fig P28.3): the interior point \(i\) goes to \(0\), the disk's center; \(2i\) goes to \(\frac{1}{3}\), a third of the way out along a radius; and the boundary — the entire real axis — goes to the unit circle: \(z = -1\) gives \(w = i\), \(z = 0\) gives \(w = -1\), \(z = 1\) gives \(w = -i\), \(z = 2\) gives \(w = \frac{3-4i}{5}\), each at modulus exactly \(1\), all machine-checked. Why it works is one line: for real \(x\), \(\bigl|x-i\bigr| = \bigl|x+i\bigr|\), so every real point sits equidistant from the formula's two fixed points, and equidistant means modulus \(1\) after the division.

What the bridge (Fig P28.3) buys is the translation of problems, not just points. Boundary data on a segment of the real axis becomes data on an arc of the circle; symmetry of the disk becomes symmetry of the half-plane. And the disk's self-bendings — its automorphisms \(e^{i\phi}\frac{w-a}{1-\bar{a}w}\), a three-parameter family, one parameter for the target point, two for the turn — mean any interior point can be declared the center before the work begins. The two model regions are P16's same problem in different clothes, and the wardrobe has a formula.

Exemplar 3 · The corridor, a corner at infinity

The dictionary's third entry looks different and is not. An infinite strip corridor — walls at heights \(0\) and \(\pi\), held at \(0\) and \(100\) — obeys the same protractor temperament in new clothing: the steady temperature is \(T = 100\cdot\frac{\operatorname{Im} z}{\pi}\), linear in height, harmonic at a glance (no \(x\)-dependence, linear in \(y\)), and machine-checkable at every point. The exponential \(w = e^{z}\) explains why: it sends each horizontal line of the strip to a ray from the origin, because \(\operatorname{Arg}\bigl(e^{x+iy}\bigr) = y\) for \(0 < y < \pi\). The wall at height \(0\) becomes the positive real ray, the wall at height \(\pi\) the negative one, and the corridor stands revealed as a half-plane whose corner sits at infinity. One honest point: the midline point \(1 + \frac{\pi}{2}i\) lands at \(e\cdot i \approx 2.718i\), squarely on the bisector ray, and both problems answer \(50\) there.

So the dictionary is one idea wearing three costumes. The strip is a wedge of angle zero with its corner pushed off to infinity; the exponential is the power map in that limit; the Möbius bridge of Exemplar 2 is what the flat wall looks like from the disk's side. Powers straighten corners, the exponential straightens the corner at infinity, Möbius maps trade the models for each other — and every composition of honest bendings is an honest bending, which is the practical freedom the next section prices.

5 · Three exhibits

Exhibit A · Holes do not travel

The engine needs \(f\) one-to-one and onto, and holes are the first wall it hits. The annulus \(\{1 < |z| < 2\}\) cannot be bent onto any annulus of a different ratio: the quantity \(\ln\bigl(r_{\text{outer}}/r_{\text{inner}}\bigr)\) — the modulus — is a conformal invariant, unchanged by every one-to-one holomorphic map. Concrete and machine-checked: scaling by \(2\) carries \(\{1 < |z| < 2\}\) onto \(\{2 < |w| < 4\}\) — the same ratio, the same modulus \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\) — while no map at all carries it onto \(\{1 < |w| < 3\}\), whose modulus \(\ln 3 \approx 1.0986\) differs. That the modulus is a complete invariant — that matching moduli is sufficient, not merely necessary — is quoted here and owed at the doors. This wall is why the charter demands regions with no holes: Exhibit A is the shape of the demand.

Exhibit B · Dimension three does not bend

The plane's abundance of bendings is a two-dimensional miracle. In three dimensions, angle-preserving is nearly rigid: a theorem of Liouville (1850) says every conformal map of a region of space is the restriction of a Möbius transformation of space — a composition of similarities, reflections, and inversions, a tiny family against the plane's ocean of holomorphic functions. The pattern dies with the abundance: three-dimensional heat and flow problems cannot generally be bent into solvability, and it is worth knowing at the outset which gifts are dimension-specific. Everything on this page happens in the plane, happens because \(z\) is two-dimensional, and has no direct spatial sequel.

Exhibit C · The boundary can be worse than the region

The charter — Riemann's, quoted in full in the doors below — buys the interior: every simply connected region except the plane itself goes conformally onto the disk, with no word about the boundary. But the thermometer problems of this page live on boundaries: transporting data is obligation two of Box P28.A, and it needs a boundary correspondence the interior map does not provide for free. Carathéodory (1913) drew the line: for regions bounded by Jordan curves — closed loops with no self-crossings — the map always extends to a continuous one-to-one correspondence of the boundaries; for wilder boundaries it can fail, and data transport fails with it. Every exemplar on this page quietly met this obligation; the honest statement of the pattern says so out loud.

Scope note. This page's dictionary stops at the elementary maps — powers, exponentials, Möbius — and their finite compositions. Most honest plates need more: rectangles and general polygons go to the half-plane by the Schwarz-Christoffel integrals (elliptic functions for the rectangle among them), which is analysis rather than algebra. The move survives — regions bend, data transports, the Laplacian's covariance never changes — but the price grows, and the charter, even where it applies, is existence: a map guaranteed is not a map written down.

6 · The doors this opens

Mitigation. When a region resists the dictionary, do not hunt for one grand map: compose. The corridor went to the half-plane by the exponential and could then cross Exemplar 2's bridge to the disk; the tangent-circle plate of Exercise 4 goes to the corridor by a single inversion. Two cheap stages beat one expensive formula nobody can write. Price. The bending preserves balance-questions — temperature, potential, streamlines — and destroys measurement-questions: distances, areas, densities all carry the point-varying factor \(\bigl|f'\bigr|\), and a claim about them must be integrated back through the map. The pattern is a microscope for one family of questions, not a free lunch. Default. When in doubt: to the half-plane for ray-data and corners, to the disk for arc-data and symmetry, across the Möbius bridge whenever the data's shape argues for the other model — and when the region has a hole, stop, compute the modulus, and be told honestly that the pattern does not apply.

The doors themselves. The charter, stated in full: every simply connected region of the plane other than the plane itself admits a one-to-one conformal map onto the disk — Riemann's mapping theorem, existence only, its proof owed elsewhere. Up the tier, the circle this page kept bending becomes a ledger: reading many breaks at once, arcs of them, major and minor (P38, planned) — where the mass of a count concentrates on arcs of the circle, and the maps that rearrange arcs are these. Further up waits the bending's piecewise-linear shadow: trading the smooth for the piecewise-linear, where the breaks are the whole structure (P40, tropicalize, planned). In the rearview, two pages made this one possible: P27 built the calculus of the derivative — the very instrument a bending is measured with — and P24's wave technology is what weighs boundary data in the model regions. The wardrobe idea is P16's, translated into geometry; the Schwarz-Christoffel integrals behind the scope note are a door this page names but does not open.

7 · Lineage

Five dates, one line each, none load-bearing. Riemann (1851, in his doctoral dissertation) stated the mapping charter, via a variational argument whose gap took half a century to close. Christoffel (1867) and Schwarz (1869), independently, wrote the polygon maps as integrals — the Schwarz-Christoffel formula of the scope note. Liouville (1850) drew Exhibit B's wall: conformal maps of space are rigid. Carathéodory (1913) proved Exhibit C's boundary correspondence for Jordan regions. The pattern itself — bend, solve, unbend — is what analysis did with potential theory once the plane had a calculus on it: older than any of these dates in spirit, and younger than the subjects it serves.

8 · Exercises

●○○ ●●○ ●●● ◆ ◐ — five exercises, from the bridge by hand to the wing.

Exercise 1 · The bridge, by hand ●○○
(a) Evaluate \(w = \frac{z-i}{z+i}\) at \(z = -1,\, 0,\, 1,\, 2\) — four honest substitutions — and verify that each image has modulus exactly \(1\). (b) Evaluate the same map at \(z = i\) and \(z = 2i\), and place the two images inside the disk. (c) Track the boundary segment \([0,1]\): where do its endpoints and its midpoint go, and what curve is the image?

Hint

(a) Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator. (c) Three points determine an arc of a circle; compute all three and look at where they sit.

Worked answer

(a) \(-1 \mapsto i\), \(0 \mapsto -1\), \(1 \mapsto -i\), \(2 \mapsto \frac{3-4i}{5}\), each of modulus \(1\) — machine-checked, four for four. (b) \(i \mapsto 0\), the disk's center; \(2i \mapsto \frac{1}{3}\), a third of the way out along the positive real radius. (c) \(0 \mapsto -1\), \(1 \mapsto -i\), and the midpoint \(\frac{1}{2} \mapsto -0.6 - 0.8i\): all three on the unit circle, in the lower left — the segment's image is the quarter arc of the unit circle running from \(-1\) down to \(-i\).

Exercise 2 · The finite stretch, weighed two ways ●●○
In the upper half-plane, the boundary is held at \(100\) on the segment \([-1,1]\) and at \(0\) everywhere else. (a) Compute the steady temperature at \(z = i\) and \(z = 2i\) by the angle formula \(u = 100\,\theta/\pi\), where \(\theta\) is the angle the segment subtends at the point. (b) Verify the same two numbers a second way: the map \(w = \frac{z-1}{z+1}\) takes the half-plane to itself, sends the segment to the negative real ray (\(1 \mapsto 0\), \(0 \mapsto -1\), \(-1 \mapsto \infty\)) and the rest of the boundary to the positive ray, so \(u = 100\,\operatorname{Arg} w/\pi\); check \(\frac{i-1}{i+1} = i\) and \(\frac{2i-1}{2i+1} = \frac{3+4i}{5}\), and compare the arguments. (c) Explain why the two routes agree, and name which of this page's exemplars did the heavy lifting.

Hint

(a) Form the vectors from \(z\) to \(-1\) and to \(1\); their dot product over the product of their lengths is the cosine of the subtended angle. (b) \(\arccos\frac{3}{5} = \arctan\frac{4}{3}\) — one angle in two trigonometric costumes.

Worked answer

(a) At \(i\): the vectors \((-1,-1)\) and \((1,-1)\) are perpendicular, so \(\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(u = 50\). At \(2i\): the vectors \((-1,-2)\) and \((1,-2)\) give \(\cos\theta = \frac{3}{5}\), so \(\theta = \arccos\frac{3}{5} \approx 0.9273\) and \(u \approx 29.52\). (b) The two images are \(i\) (argument \(\frac{\pi}{2}\)) and \(\frac{3+4i}{5}\) (argument \(\arctan\frac{4}{3} \approx 0.9273\)) — the same angles as (a) to six decimals, machine-checked both ways. (c) The map turned the segment's two-sided boundary data into the two-ray data of Exemplar 1's half-plane: the finite stretch was a corner problem in disguise, and the Möbius map was the unmasking. The encounter's second experiment and its first were one experiment.

Exercise 3 · Holes, priced ●●●
(a) Verify by substitution that \(w = 2z\) carries the annulus \(\{1 < |z| < 2\}\) onto the annulus \(\{2 < |w| < 4\}\), boundary circle to boundary circle. (b) Compute \(\ln\bigl(r_{\text{outer}}/r_{\text{inner}}\bigr)\) for both annuli and for the target \(\{1 < |w| < 3\}\), and show that no scaling \(z \mapsto cz\) can carry the first onto the third. (c) Decide whether \(\{2 < |z| < 6\}\) and \(\{1 < |w| < 3\}\) are conformally equivalent, and produce the map; then state honestly what your decision used and what it owed.

Hint

(b) A scaling multiplies inner and outer radii by the same constant — track what happens to the ratio, then take logarithms. (c) A scaling has one constant to satisfy two equations; write them both down.

Worked answer

(a) \(\bigl|2z\bigr| = 2|z|\): the inner circle \(|z| = 1\) lands on \(|w| = 2\), the outer \(|z| = 2\) on \(|w| = 4\), and between them the annuli correspond point for point. (b) Both of the first two carry \(\ln 2 \approx 0.6931\) — the modulus is invariant under the move, exactly as Exhibit A claims. The target carries \(\ln 3 \approx 1.0986\). A scaling \(z \mapsto cz\) sends the ratio \(2/1\) to \((2c)/(c) = 2\): the ratio never moves, so no scaling — indeed no map at all, by the invariant — bridges \(\ln 2\) to \(\ln 3\). (c) \(\ln(6/2) = \ln 3 = \ln(3/1)\): the moduli match, and the scaling \(w = \frac{z}{2}\) works, satisfying \(2c = 1\) and \(6c = 3\) with the same \(c = \frac{1}{2}\). The decision used the invariant's necessity (different moduli: no map) and the scaling's generosity (equal moduli: an explicit map); what it owed is Exhibit A's completeness — that equal moduli alone guarantee a map exists for arbitrary annuli — quoted on this page, proved nowhere on it.

Exercise 4 · Diagnose the unknown ◆
Two plates, same material, same equation. Plate A: the region between two circles tangent at a point \(p\), the two boundary circles held at \(0\) and \(100\). Plate B: the region between two disjoint circles, one inside the other, held at \(0\) and \(100\). For each plate: which pattern applies, what is the first move, and what will the solution look like?

Worked answer

Plate A: this page's. First move: the inversion \(w = \frac{1}{z-p}\). Both circles pass through the pole \(p\), so each is carried to a straight line; and because the circles met only at \(p\), the two lines never meet — the plate becomes a strip, its walls the two boundary lines. A rotation and a scaling land it in Exemplar 3's corridor, and the temperature is linear in the transverse coordinate. The tangency point — where the plate pinches to zero width — is the image of infinity: the corner at infinity again, and the solution stays finite there because the strip's law never consults the ends. Plate B: a genuine annulus — a hole — and Exhibit A's wall: no one-to-one conformal map to a strip or disk exists, the modulus invariant forbids it, and the pattern does not apply. The two plates look like cousins; the diagnosis separates them cleanly, and knowing why the move fails is part of the move.

Exercise 5 · One map further ◐
The map \(w = z + \frac{1}{z}\) (Joukowski's). (a) The circle \(|z| = 1.1\): show its image is the ellipse \(\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1\) with \(a = r + \frac{1}{r} = 2.0091\) and \(b = r - \frac{1}{r} = 0.1909\), by checking the four axial points \(z = \pm 1.1,\ \pm 1.1i\) and the diagonal point \(z = 1.1\,e^{i\pi/4}\). (b) Show \(a^{2} - b^{2} = 4\) for every \(r > 1\), and say what that says about the family of ellipses as \(r\) varies. (c) What happens at \(r = 1\)? Where do \(z\) and \(\frac{1}{z}\) go, and in what sense does the map prefer to be thought of as two-to-one? Where this map famously goes — the wing — is a door this exercise only names.

Hint

(a) Write \(z = r e^{i\theta}\) and collect the real and imaginary parts of \(z + \frac{1}{z}\). (b) Expand \(\bigl(r + \frac{1}{r}\bigr)^{2}\) and \(\bigl(r - \frac{1}{r}\bigr)^{2}\) and subtract. (c) Compute the images of \(z\) and of \(\frac{1}{z}\) for the same \(z\).

Worked answer

(a) \(z + \frac{1}{z} = \bigl(r + \frac{1}{r}\bigr)\cos\theta + i\bigl(r - \frac{1}{r}\bigr)\sin\theta\), so the image of the circle is the ellipse with \(a = r + \frac{1}{r}\), \(b = r - \frac{1}{r}\) — for \(r = 1.1\), \(a = 2.0091\), \(b = 0.1909\). The axial points land at \((\pm 2.0091,\, 0)\) and \((0,\, \pm 0.1909)\); the diagonal point \(1.1\,e^{i\pi/4}\) lands at \(1.4206 + 0.1350i\); all five satisfy \(\frac{x^{2}}{a^{2}} + \frac{y^{2}}{b^{2}} = 1\) exactly, machine-checked five for five. (b) \(\bigl(r+\frac{1}{r}\bigr)^{2} - \bigl(r-\frac{1}{r}\bigr)^{2} = 4\) — the difference collapses to exactly \(4\) for every \(r\), so every circle \(|z| = r\) maps onto an ellipse with the same two foci, \(\pm 2\): a confocal family, collapsing toward the segment between the foci as \(r \to 1\). (c) At \(r = 1\): \(x = 2\cos\theta\), \(y = 0\) — the circle collapses onto the segment \([-2, 2]\), traced twice as \(\theta\) runs a full turn. And in general \(z\) and \(\frac{1}{z}\) have the same image, so the map is two-to-one: \(|z| = r\) and \(|z| = \frac{1}{r}\) trace the same ellipse in opposite orientations. The famous door: circles through the point \(-1\) map to airfoil shapes — Joukowski's wing — the bending of this page carried into a wind tunnel, machinery owed, not built here.
