The Patterns Atlas · P15 · the fifteenth move of the spine

Squeeze it

How much is it? Some quantities refuse to answer. The rim of a circle is a length that no fraction and no radical ever spells, and P14 just proved a whole world of such numbers exists — too many to list, every one unwritable by formula. The fifteenth move accepts the refusal and changes the game. Build two walls you CAN compute — one proved to sit below the answer, one proved to sit above it — and then drive them together: double the polygons, add the terms, halve the interval, until the gap between the walls is smaller than any precision anyone can demand. The quantity never surrenders a formula. It does not need to. Trapped tightly enough, it is known to the last digit anyone will ever ask for; and when the walls hold for every crank at once, the squeeze turns a computation into a proof without the walls ever touching. It is the oldest move in this book by two millennia — and the one running inside every certified decimal ever printed.

New word: lower bound and upper bound. Given a quantity \(x\), a lower bound is a number \(L\) you can actually compute, with \(L \le x\); an upper bound is a number \(U\) you can actually compute, with \(x \le U\). Together they form a trap: \(L \le x \le U\). The word "compute" is load-bearing — a wall you cannot evaluate is scenery, not a wall. New word: the gap. The gap of a trap is \(U - L\), the trap's slack. The whole move is a campaign against the gap: crank the trap until the gap is smaller than the precision demanded — or, in the exact squeeze, until the gap is forced to be zero. New word: exhaustion. The move's ancestral name: fill the unknown from the inside with computable pieces, enclose it from the outside with computable pieces, and shrink the leftover until it is smaller than anything anyone can name. The name dates from 1647; the machine is far older — §7 tells the story.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on, P13 convicted whole families by manufacturing ever-smaller criminals, and P14 built the one object no list can contain. The ladder arrives here carrying two tools this page leans on hard: P02, because a wall is only as good as the computation that certifies it — a bound you cannot run is a guess wearing brackets — and P11, because the squeeze's closing act, the ε-trap of §4's third example, is a reductio with its coat off.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P14 ended with a departing gift: numbers exist — overwhelmingly many of them — that no enumeration reaches and no formula spells. How do you compute with a number you cannot write? The good news: nothing new needs building. The two walls are computations you already know how to run — areas of polygons, sums of fractions, midpoints of intervals — and P02 taught exactly how to run them honestly, detector by detector. The bad news, paid up front: the trap demands two proofs, one per wall, and a wall you merely believe is a conjecture in costume — §5's gallery shows what each missing half costs. And the trap's honesty has a second price: it certifies values, never formulas. No amount of cranking will turn \(\pi\) into a fraction; precision without a formula is the normal condition of this move, and the move's whole art is making that condition livable.

2 · Meet the problem

Ask the oldest quantitative question there is: how long is the rim of a circle? Take a circle of radius \(1\); the answer, half the circumference, is the number \(\pi\). You cannot write \(\pi\). Not as a fraction — Lambert proved that in the eighteenth century, by a route this book defers — and not through any finite stack of square roots and arithmetic either (Lindemann, 1882; §5's last exhibit prices that wall). But you do not need to write \(\pi\). You need to corner it.

Build the walls from polygons, starting with the cheapest ones. The hexagon inside: join the center to six equally spaced points on the rim. Each of the six triangles has two sides of length \(1\) (radii) meeting at \(60^\circ\), so the third side is forced — equal sides carry equal angles, the base angles sum to \(120^\circ\), every angle is \(60^\circ\), every triangle is equilateral, every side is exactly \(1\). The inscribed hexagon's perimeter is \(6\), half of it is \(3\), and every chord is shorter than the arc it cuts: the first wall reads \(\pi > 3\). The hexagon outside: rest six tangent sides against the circle. Each side touches the rim at its midpoint; the center, the tangent point, and a vertex form a right triangle with its \(30^\circ\) angle at the center and the radius \(1\) as the adjacent leg. The \(30\)-\(60\)-\(90\) triangle is built from \(1\), \(\sqrt 3\), \(2\), so the opposite leg — half the side — is \(1/\sqrt 3\), each side is \(2/\sqrt 3\), the perimeter is \(12/\sqrt 3 = 4\sqrt 3\), and half of it, \(2\sqrt 3\), overhangs the rim it touches: \(\pi < 2\sqrt 3\). The trap closes:

\[3 \;<\; \pi \;<\; 2\sqrt 3 \;\approx\; 3.4641.\]

Hand-check the outer wall without decimals: squaring is fair to positive quantities, and \((2\sqrt 3)^2 = 12 < 12.0409 = 3.47^2\), so \(2\sqrt 3 < 3.47\). The trap reads \(3 < \pi < 3.47\) — honest walls, both proved, and jointly worth exactly one digit: the integer part of \(\pi\) is \(3\). Not much. But the trap has a crank.

Double the polygons. Twelve sides fit tighter than six; twenty-four tighter than twelve. Two lines of Pythagoras — one chase per family, and Exercise 1 runs the first doubling by hand — turn each doubling into bookkeeping. If \(a_n\) is the side of the inscribed \(n\)-gon and \(b_n\) the side of the circumscribed one:

\[a_{2n} = \sqrt{2 - 2\sqrt{1 - a_n^2/4}}, \qquad b_{2n} = \frac{b_n}{1 + \sqrt{1 + b_n^2/4}}.\]

Start from \(a_6 = 1\) and \(b_6 = 2/\sqrt 3\) and turn the crank:

That is exactly where Archimedes stopped — by hand, in rational arithmetic, in the treatise Measurement of a Circle. His walls, rounded outward to convenient fractions (a wall may always be loosened, never tightened), read

\[3 + \frac{10}{71} \;<\; \pi \;<\; 3 + \frac{1}{7}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{223}{71} \;<\; \pi \;<\; \frac{22}{7}.\]

Our \(96\)-gon table brackets him: \(3.14103 > 223/71 = 3.14085\ldots\) and \(3.14271 < 22/7 = 3.14286\ldots\) — his fractions sit just inside the computed walls, the outward rounding visible as slack. He wanted walls a student can verify by dividing integers, and he paid one digit for the convenience.

Notice what the table does not contain: \(\pi\) itself. No polygon's side ever equals the arc; the walls only lean in. What licenses "the \(96\)-gon certifies two decimals" is not that the trap catches its prisoner — it never touches — but that the gap has been driven below the demanded precision. That demand structure — what a trap must prove, and what happens when each part fails — is the move proper, and it gets a name next.

3 · Name the move

P15 · Squeeze it — trap the answer between two computable walls

Shape of the claim. "How much is it?" — asked about a quantity that is well-defined but not directly computable: no formula, no closed form, no finite spelling. The question is quantitative, and it expects a number, not a description.

The move. Do not chase the quantity. Build two families of computable approximations — one proved to sit always below the answer, one always above — and a crank: a parameter (sides \(n\), terms \(k\), steps \(m\)) whose turning provably shrinks the gap between the walls. Then match the crank to the demand: a gap below \(10^{-2}\) certifies two decimals; a gap below every named size at once — the ε-trap — forces the answer exactly.

When to reach for it. When the answer exists but the formula does not — or the formula is beyond your current floor, or too expensive to evaluate. When someone demands a certified value, not an estimate. And when a conjectured value needs walls strong enough to rule out every rival.

The move in words a page long: the quantity is known exactly when it is known to arbitrary precision — because "arbitrary precision" is a statement about the walls, not the prisoner. The two families do all the work; the prisoner never testifies. That inversion — proof without exhibition, knowledge without capture — is the squeeze's signature, and every example below runs on it.

The three demands

A squeeze is legitimate only if all three gates clear, and §5 is a gallery of failures at each:

  • Computable walls. Each wall must be something you can actually evaluate — with rationals, radicals, or an algorithm — and the enclosure must be a theorem. A "wall" that costs more to evaluate than the answer you want is a joke, and a bound with no partner is worse: §5's first exhibit is the one-armed version.
  • Proven enclosure. The inequalities must be proved, not observed. A million agreeing decimals enclose nothing; a wall is a proof, and §5's second exhibit prices the difference between evidence and enclosure.
  • A drivable gap. There must be a crank: a step that provably shrinks the gap, without limit — for every demanded size, some number of cranks gets below it. A trap whose gap has a floor (§5's frozen exhibit) certifies one digit forever, and no amount of cranking changes that.

Where the move appears, and its three-key signature

Appearances. Perimeters and areas cornered by polygons (§2, and §4's first example). Constants certified by their series (§4's second example). Roots cornered by interval halving, and limits pinned exactly by the ε-trap (§4's third example). Every definite integral ever evaluated by sums from below and above. And — least obviously — refutations: an integer trapped strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) cannot exist, and §8's third exercise uses that squeeze to prove \(e\) is not a fraction.

Three keys. The move applies exactly when all three turn:

  1. A quantitative unknown. The target is a number — a length, an area, a value — not a structure or a classification. If the question is not "how much," the trap has nothing to enclose.
  2. Walls in reach. Some computation you already own must sit provably on each side: inscribed and circumscribed polygons, partial sums and tail bounds, test intervals that flip sign.
  3. A crank with no floor. One honest parameter must drive the gap down without limit: doubling, adding terms, halving, refining.

When the first key jams, the question is structural — hand it back to P01 and ask what is actually being demanded. When the second jams, you are not squeezing; you are conjecturing, and §5's second exhibit prices the difference. When the third jams, the walls are real but the trap only closes partway: welcome to one-sided country, where single bounds are theorems in their own right — the frontier where P38 and P56 work.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per family in the catalogue. The first is Archimedes' own: an area cornered by triangles, two thousand years before calculus. The second certifies a constant's digits with nothing but fraction arithmetic. The third builds the move's closing act — the ε-trap — and shows it pin three famous quantities: a decimal, an area, a root.

Example 1 · The parabola's cap, cornered to \(\tfrac{4}{3}\)

Moves used: P07 building the triangle-filling machine · P02 tabulating the covered sums · P15 trapping the area between covered and overhang.

Take the parabola \(y = x^2\) and the chord joining \((-1, 1)\) to \((1, 1)\). The parabolic segment is the cap between chord and curve, and the question is its area. Calculus answers in one line at the class-12 floor; Archimedes answered it around 250 BC with a squeeze — and this page rebuilds his, because it shows the move running where no formula is allowed to help.

The inscribed triangle first: vertex at \((0, 0)\), base the chord. Its area is exactly \(1\) — the shoelace on \((-1,1)\), \((1,1)\), \((0,0)\) gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}\,|{-1}(1-0) + 1(0-1) + 0(1-1)| = \tfrac{1}{2}|-2| = 1\). Call it \(T = 1\). The triangle is inside the cap: each slanted side is a chord of the parabola, a parabola lies below every one of its chords, and the base is the cap's own lid — so the covered area starts at \(T\).

Now the machine, one P07-style construction. Each slanted side leaves a sliver between itself and the curve. In each sliver, inscribe the triangle whose base is the sliver's chord and whose apex sits where the tangent parallel to that chord touches the parabola. The left sliver's chord runs from \((-1,1)\) to \((0,0)\), slope \(-1\); the tangent to \(y = x^2\) has slope \(2x\), which equals \(-1\) at \(x = -\tfrac{1}{2}\), so the apex is \((-\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4})\). The new triangle \((-1,1)\), \((0,0)\), \((-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{4})\) has area exactly \(\tfrac{1}{8}\) — shoelace again — one-eighth of its parent. The mirror triangle on the right contributes the same, and stage 2 covers \(T + 2 \cdot \tfrac{T}{8} = \tfrac{5T}{4}\).

And the machine repeats: each sliver of a parabola cut by a chord is the whole problem in miniature, so every stage's triangles carve exactly one-eighth out of their parent's leftover — doubling the count, quartering the total. Stage \(k\) contributes exactly \(T \cdot 4^{-(k-1)}\), and the covered sums are a geometric series in disguise:

\[\text{covered}_k \;=\; \bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{16} + \cdots + 4^{-(k-1)}\bigr)T \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T\,\bigl(1 - 4^{-k}\bigr).\]

The lower wall is free: every stage's triangles lie inside the cap, so \(\text{covered}_k \le \text{area}\) for every \(k\). The upper wall is the honest part, and it carries an honesty label. Sketch — the missing piece is the enclosure lemma: the leftover after stage \(k\) fits inside a region of area at most \(\tfrac{4}{3} \times\) the total of the stages after \(k\), a tangent-chord chase this book defers. Taken on credit, the lemma delivers the upper wall, and the algebra collapses beautifully:

\[\text{area} \;\le\; \text{covered}_k + \tfrac{4}{3}\,T \cdot 4^{-k} \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T\,\bigl(1 - 4^{-k}\bigr) + \tfrac{4}{3}\,T \cdot 4^{-k} \;=\; \tfrac{4}{3}\,T, \qquad \text{for every } k \text{ at once}.\]

The leftover bound and the series tail cancel exactly, and the trap snaps shut around a constant: \(\text{covered}_k \le \text{area} \le \tfrac{4}{3}T\), for every \(k\). The walls do not touch at any finite stage — the lower one rises forever, the upper one never moves — and yet the prisoner has nowhere to stand: §4's third example names the principle that forces \(\text{area} = \tfrac{4}{3}T\) from exactly this shape. (Archimedes, who had no ε, closed it his own way — twice: once by Eudoxus' double contradiction, once by summing the geometric series directly. His Quadrature of the Parabola runs twenty-four propositions to this one number.)

The class-12 floor can check the constant: \(\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^2)\,dx = \bigl[x - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\bigr]_{-1}^{1} = \tfrac{4}{3}\) — the trap and the integral agree, two millennia apart. The check is a courtesy; the trap above is the proof.

Example 2 · \(e\)'s integer part, certified by fractions

Moves used: P02 tabulating partial sums · P15 trapping a constant between sums and tail bounds.

Define a number by its series (the growth constant wears this costume; why it does is another page's story — here the sum itself is the quarry):

\[e \;=\; 1 + \frac{1}{1!} + \frac{1}{2!} + \frac{1}{3!} + \cdots \;=\; \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{1}{k!}.\]

Each partial sum \(S_k = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \frac{1}{j!}\) is a fraction you can compute exactly — a lower wall, since the omitted tail is positive: \(S_k < e\) for every \(k\). The upper wall needs a bound on that tail, and the crudest one costs a single line: factorials grow at least like powers of two,

\[k! \;=\; 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots k \;\ge\; 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdots 2 \;=\; 2^{k-1} \qquad (k \ge 1),\]

so \(\sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k!} \le \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = 2\), and the crude trap closes: \(e \le 1 + 2 = 3\). From below, \(S_2 = 2.5 < e\). Two proved walls — \(2.5 < e < 3\) — and the integer part of \(e\) is certified: \(\lfloor e \rfloor = 2\). No formula for \(e\) was ever written down, and none was needed; the trap answered a question about \(e\) without ever holding \(e\).

Sharpen the upper wall by trapping the tail where it lives. For \(j > k\), the factorial \(j! = k! \cdot (k{+}1)(k{+}2)\cdots j\) has \(j - k\) factors, each at least \(k{+}1\), so \(\frac{1}{j!} \le \frac{1}{k!}\,(k{+}1)^{-(j-k)}\). Summing the geometric bound:

\[R_k \;=\; \sum_{j > k} \frac{1}{j!} \;\le\; \frac{1}{k!}\sum_{i \ge 1} \frac{1}{(k{+}1)^i} \;=\; \frac{1}{k!} \cdot \frac{1}{k} \;=\; \frac{1}{k \cdot k!}.\]

Now the crank is a table, and the gap meets any demand you can name:

The walls read \(2.718281526 < e < 2.718281832\), and both round to the same five decimals: \(e = 2.71828\), certified. The floor of the trap is worth naming, because it is a wall too: everything above used only the definition's growth — factorials outpacing powers. Nothing about \(e\)'s character was needed for its first five digits. The character came later and costs more: Hermite proved \(e\) transcendental in 1873, and §8's third exercise derives the easier half — that \(e\) is not a fraction — from this very table's walls, by squeezing an integer out of existence.

Example 3 · The ε-trap — walls that meet without touching

Moves used: P11 running the contradiction frame on a size · P02 keeping the interval books · P15 driving the gap to zero.

Both examples above end the same way: a lower wall rising toward an upper wall, neither ever touching, and a conclusion drawn anyway. The principle that licenses it is small enough to hold in one hand:

\[0 \;\le\; \delta \;\le\; 10^{-K} \text{ for every } K \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \delta = 0.\]

Why: if \(\delta > 0\), then some \(K\) has \(10^{-K} < \delta\) — make \(K\) larger than the count of \(\delta\)'s leading zeros — and the assumed trap breaks. A nonnegative quantity smaller than every nameable size is zero. That is P11's frame in miniature — assume \(\delta > 0\), run the machine, watch it break — and it is the squeeze's only touch: the walls never reach the prisoner, but "below every demanded size, forever" is reaching. Call it the ε-trap.

First application, a famous one. Is \(0.999\ldots\) — all nines, forever — equal to \(1\)? Let \(\delta = 1 - 0.999\ldots\). Truncate after \(K\) nines: the truncation is exactly \(1 - 10^{-K}\) (decimal arithmetic, no limits: \(0.9 = 1 - 10^{-1}\), \(0.99 = 1 - 10^{-2}\), and so on), and the full string is at least as large as every truncation. So \(0 \le \delta \le 10^{-K}\) for every \(K\); the ε-trap fires; \(\delta = 0\); \(0.999\ldots = 1\). Two lines — and P14's first exhibit, where the costume \(0.999\ldots\) collided with a diagonal rule, is now a theorem instead of an accident.

Second application, closing Example 1's sketch. The walls there were \(\text{covered}_k \le \text{area} \le \tfrac{4}{3}T\) for every \(k\), with the wall-to-wall gap exactly \(\tfrac{4}{3}T \cdot 4^{-k}\). Name any demanded size, \(10^{-K}\): some \(k\) drives the gap below it, because \(4^{-k}\) eventually beats every power of ten. Then \(\tfrac{4}{3}T - 10^{-K} < \text{covered}_k \le \text{area} \le \tfrac{4}{3}T\) for every \(K\), so the difference \(\tfrac{4}{3}T - \text{area}\) is a \(\delta\), and the ε-trap forces \(\text{area} = \tfrac{4}{3}T = \tfrac{4}{3}\). The sketch is closed — with the enclosure lemma still on credit, a debt §5's second exhibit prices — and the mechanism deserves a second look: no single \(k\) ever finished the job. The conclusion lives in the words "for every \(k\)," and the gap does the arguing.

Third application: the trap as an algorithm. Corner \(\sqrt 2\) on the interval \([1, 2]\) — the endpoints sit on opposite sides, since \(1^2 = 1 < 2 < 4 = 2^2\). The sign of \(x^2 - 2\) is a detector in P02's exact sense — a checkable test — so bisect: test the midpoint, keep the half where the sign flips. Ten steps of bookkeeping, all of it dyadic — every endpoint has the form \(k/2^{n}\), and the test \((k/2^n)^2 < 2\) becomes \(k^2 < 2 \cdot 4^n\) after clearing denominators. Integers only; no decimals, no drift, nothing to round.

One comparison before the move gets its gallery. The Babylonian machine \(x \mapsto \tfrac{1}{2}(x + 2/x)\), started at \(x_0 = 1\), runs the errors \(0.414,\; 0.0858,\; 0.00245,\; 2.1 \times 10^{-6},\; 1.6 \times 10^{-12}\) — the count of correct digits roughly doubles per step, against bisection's steady \(0.301\). The trade is the trap's price structure in miniature: bisection demands only a sign test and works on anything with a sign; the Babylonian machine demands a division contract — and a theory, which P22's page owns. Fast squeezes buy speed with assumptions, and the honest question is never "which is better" but "which demands can I actually meet."

5 · When the move fails

The three demands of §3 are not decoration; each fails in a characteristic way, and the failures are as instructive as the squeezes. Five exhibits.

Exhibit 1 · The one-armed trap — an enclosure failure

A student computes inscribed polygons diligently — through \(n = 96\), lower wall \(\pi > 3.14103\) — and reports: "the polygons say \(\pi\) is at least \(3.14103\), and we can push that as far as we like." Both statements are true and jointly worthless. A lower bound alone is compatible with \(\pi = 3.2\), with \(\pi = 4\), with anything above the wall — it certifies "not below" and nothing else. The mirrored version (circumscribed only: \(\pi < 3.14271\)) is equally blind downward. Two walls is not double the work of one; it is the difference between a bound and a trap. The demand of proven enclosure has two halves, and §2 built both before claiming a single digit.

Exhibit 2 · The conjectured sandwich — a proof-demand failure

Euler's constant \(\gamma \approx 0.5772156\ldots\) is known numerically to vast precision, its digits carried far beyond any hand-check. The numerics whisper \(0.5772 < \gamma < 0.5773\), and every trap-builder wants those walls. But wanting walls is not having them: a printed decimal expansion is a computation, not a proof, and \(\gamma\)'s tight two-sided traps are hard-won theorems rather than table work — a crude trap for \(\gamma\) is a textbook exercise, the tight one a research paper. This is P02's detector lesson in grown-up clothes: evidence, however overwhelming, is not enclosure. The honest squeeze on \(\gamma\) states its demand and stops where its proofs stop — and the discipline is old: §7's history is two millennia of exactly that, squeezing exactly as far as the proofs reached and not one digit further.

Exhibit 3 · The frozen trap — a gap-demand failure

Here is a trap that is true, proved, and stopped: \(3 < \pi < 2\sqrt 3\). Built in §2, never cranked, it certifies exactly one digit, forever. "But it is a valid trap!" — it is a pair of inequalities, which is what a trap is called only when the crank exists. The frozen trap teaches the discipline backwards: the demand comes first — five decimals, ten decimals, exact — and the crank count is read off the gap's shrink rate. §2's gap quarters per doubling, so five decimals costs eight doublings, \(n = 1536\); §4's bisection halves, so ten decimals costs \(34\) steps. A trap without a demand has no stopping rule, and a crank turned without one is a machine burning effort with no licensed conclusion.

Exhibit 4 · Trapping the wrong object — a domain failure

"Every truncation of \(0.999\ldots\) is strictly less than \(1\): \(0.9 < 1\), \(0.99 < 1\), and so on. So \(0.999\ldots < 1\)." The walls are honest — every truncation really is below \(1\) — but they enclose the truncations, and \(0.999\ldots\) is not a truncation. The infinite string is the limit the truncations squeeze toward, and §4's ε-trap caught it sitting exactly on \(1\). This is P14's costume trap in a new costume: there the trap enclosed digit strings while numbers wore two spellings; here the trap encloses finite prefixes while the number is the infinite object. The repair is the same in both worlds: name the prisoner precisely before building walls around it.

Exhibit 5 · Precision is not a formula — the scope boundary

Thirty-four bisection steps certify \(\sqrt 2\) to ten decimals. No number of steps certifies \(\sqrt 2\) as a fraction — that impossibility is P11's to prove and M01's to run, and no trap can overturn it: the walls close on \(\sqrt 2\) itself, which is the entire point. For \(\sqrt 2\) the trap merely races a formula that exists (a radical). For \(\pi\) no formula exists at any depth of radicals and arithmetic — Lindemann, 1882 — so the trap is not a detour on the way to a formula; the trap is the access, forever. And beyond both lies the one-armed country: quantities whose upper walls improve forever without a lower partner in sight, where the single bound stops being scaffolding and becomes the theorem itself — P38's and P56's territory, where this page stops honestly at the border.

Scope, honestly. What P15 gives: certified values to any demanded precision; exact pinning when two proven walls hold for every crank (the ε-trap); and refutations by trap — §8's third exercise proves \(e\) is not a fraction by squeezing an integer out of existence. What it does not give: closed forms (no crank writes a formula; precision and formula are different achievements); explanations (the trap says how much, never why); or any purchase on non-quantitative questions — before the trap can grip, P01 must have produced a quantity to enclose. And the price is structural: two proofs per trap, one per wall, both mandatory. The five exhibits above are the price list for skipping each.

6 · What to try next

Three destinations elsewhere in the book, and a default habit.

  • Catch the limit point (P21). Bisection's intervals are nested and halving, and the ε-trap says they close on exactly one point. P21's move generalizes the crowding: infinitely many attempts trapped in a bounded room must accumulate, and the accumulation point carries the answer. The squeeze's analytic engine, one tier up.
  • Iterate to a fixed point (P22). The Babylonian machine of §4's third example, made into theory: a contraction — each step multiplies the error by a fixed factor below \(1\) — has exactly one place to settle, and the error's squarings beat bisection's halvings. The trade Example 3 noticed (slow squeezes with weak demands, fast ones with contracts) becomes a theorem with a name.
  • Major and minor arcs (P38). The frontier where bounds stop being scaffolding and become load-bearing: split the problem into main roads that carry the weight exactly and side roads that need only bounds. The one-armed traps of Exhibit 5 stop being failures there and become the method — the honest home of every wall that never met its partner.

And the default habit, in one sentence: when a quantity will not yield a formula, do not chase it — build a lower wall and an upper wall that you can prove, state the demand first, and crank until the gap meets it; and if the walls will not close, say so, because a located hardness ("the two-sided trap for this quantity is a research problem") is itself a finding, priced honestly.

7 · Lineage

The squeeze is the oldest move in this book, and its paper trail runs back further than any other pattern's. In the late fifth century BC, Antiphon inscribed polygons in a circle and argued the leftover would eventually be used up — intuition ahead of its rigor. The rigor came from Eudoxus of Cnidus, who built the proportion theory and the double-contradiction frame that let Greek mathematics fill a figure without limit while never once naming an infinity; Euclid's Book XII carried the machine through its first theorems, the areas of circles behaving as the squares on their diameters among them. Archimedes then ran the machine at full power twice. Measurement of a Circle corners \(\pi\) between \(\frac{223}{71}\) and \(\frac{22}{7}\) with \(96\)-gons — §2's table, by hand, in rationals — and the Quadrature of the Parabola runs twenty-four propositions to the \(\tfrac{4}{3}\) of §4's first example, closing the trap twice: once by Eudoxus' double contradiction, once by the geometric series the ancients could sum but would not call a limit.

The move traveled east as far as forward. In the third century AD, Liu Hui, commenting on the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, rebuilt the circle squeeze from scratch — polygons out to \(192\) sides, his own trap \(A_{2N} < A_{\text{circle}} < A_{2N} + D_{2N}\) relating areas and slivers, the value \(3.1416\). Two centuries later Zu Chongzhi pushed the crank to a \(24{,}576\)-gon and certified \(3.1415926 < \pi < 3.1415927\) — a record that stood for roughly nine hundred years — alongside the approximation \(355/113\), six decimals accurate and easier to remember than to beat. The name arrived last and latest: "the method of exhaustion" first appears in 1647, in Gregory of Saint Vincent's geometry — the machine was over two thousand years old when it was christened, just in time for the nineteenth century to rebuild the machine itself. The double contradiction became the ε-trap of §4's third example; the language of "for every demanded size" replaced the Greek dance around infinity; and the squeeze theorem — the sandwich — took its permanent seat in the first calculus course. The pattern's newest face is the one your computer wears when it certifies a result: guaranteed-precision arithmetic, where every certified decimal is a pair of proved walls. The ancients cornered \(\pi\) with polygons; the machine corners everything the same way.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — The hexagon walls, by hand. (a) Show the inscribed hexagon of the unit circle has half-perimeter exactly \(3\). (b) Show the circumscribed hexagon's half-perimeter is \(2\sqrt 3\), and certify \(2\sqrt 3 < 3.47\) without decimals. (c) Turn the crank once: verify \(a_{12} = \sqrt{2 - \sqrt 3}\) and \(b_{12} = 4 - 2\sqrt 3\) from the doubling recurrences, compute the \(12\)-gon walls, and confirm the gap was cut by a factor of about \(4.24\).

Hint

(a) Two radii and an apex angle of \(60^\circ\) force the third side. (b) The \(30\)-\(60\)-\(90\) triangle is built from \(1\), \(\sqrt 3\), \(2\); squaring is fair to positive quantities. (c) Rationalize \(\frac{2}{2+\sqrt 3}\) by multiplying top and bottom by \(2 - \sqrt 3\).

Worked

(a) Each of the six triangles has two sides of length \(1\) (radii) meeting at \(60^\circ\); equal sides carry equal angles, the base angles sum to \(120^\circ\), so all angles are \(60^\circ\) — equilateral, side \(1\), perimeter \(6\), half \(3\). (b) The tangent side touches the rim at its midpoint; the radius to the tangent point is perpendicular to the side, giving a right triangle with \(30^\circ\) at the center and adjacent leg \(1\). Half the side is the opposite leg, \(1/\sqrt 3\), so the side is \(2/\sqrt 3\) and half the perimeter is \(6/\sqrt 3 = 2\sqrt 3\). Squaring: \((2\sqrt 3)^2 = 12 < 12.0409 = 3.47^2\), so \(2\sqrt 3 < 3.47\). (c) From \(a_6 = 1\): \(a_{12} = \sqrt{2 - 2\sqrt{1 - 1/4}} = \sqrt{2 - \sqrt 3} \approx 0.51764\), so the lower wall is \(6\sqrt{2-\sqrt 3} \approx 3.10583\). From \(b_6 = 2/\sqrt 3\): \(b_{12} = \frac{2/\sqrt 3}{1 + \sqrt{1 + 1/3}} = \frac{2/\sqrt 3}{1 + 2/\sqrt 3} = \frac{2}{2 + \sqrt 3} = 2(2 - \sqrt 3) = 4 - 2\sqrt 3 \approx 0.53590\), so the upper wall is \(6(4 - 2\sqrt 3) \approx 3.21539\). The gap is \(0.10956\) against the hexagon's \(0.46410\) — a factor of \(4.24\) — and the ratio tightens toward exactly \(4\) as \(n\) grows.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — \(e\)'s floor and first digits, certified. (a) Prove \(k! \ge 2^{k-1}\) for all \(k \ge 1\). (b) Conclude \(2.5 < e < 3\), and say what the trap certifies about \(\lfloor e \rfloor\). (c) Prove the tail bound \(R_k < \frac{1}{k \cdot k!}\), and certify \(e = 2.71828\) to five decimals using nine terms.

Hint

(a) The first factor is \(1\); each of the remaining \(k-1\) factors is at least \(2\). (b) The floor needs walls that straddle no integer. (c) For \(j > k\), bound \((k{+}1)(k{+}2)\cdots j\) from below and sum a geometric series.

Worked

(a) \(k! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots k\): one factor of \(1 = 2^0\), then \(k-1\) factors each at least \(2 = 2^1\), so \(k! \ge 2^{k-1}\). (b) \(e = 1 + \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k!} \le 1 + \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{2^{k-1}} = 3\), while \(e > S_2 = 2.5\); the walls straddle no integer, so \(\lfloor e \rfloor = 2\), certified. (c) For \(j > k\), \(j! = k!\,(k{+}1)(k{+}2)\cdots j \ge k!\,(k{+}1)^{j-k}\), so \(R_k \le \frac{1}{k!}\bigl(\frac{1}{k+1} + \frac{1}{(k+1)^2} + \cdots\bigr) = \frac{1}{k!} \cdot \frac{1}{k}\) — strict, because the second factor already exceeds \(k{+}1\). At \(k = 9\): \(S_9 = \frac{986410}{362880} = 2.718281526\), and \(R_9 < \frac{1}{9 \cdot 362880} < 3.07 \times 10^{-7}\), so \(2.718281526 < e < 2.718281832\). Both walls round to \(2.71828\): five decimals certified by fraction arithmetic.

Exercise 3 ●●● — \(e\) is not a fraction. Suppose, for contradiction, that \(e = p/q\) in lowest terms with \(q \ge 2\). (a) Show that \(q!\,S_q\) and \(q!\,e\) are integers, so \(q!\,R_q\) is an integer. (b) Show \(0 < q!\,R_q < 1\). (c) Name the pattern moves at work, and run the witness \(q = 2\): what integer does the trap demand, and where does it actually sit?

Hint

(a) Clear denominators: \(q!/j!\) is an integer for \(j \le q\), and \(q! \cdot p/q = (q-1)!\,p\). (b) Exercise 2(c)'s bound. (c) An integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) is a broken machine; name the pattern that breaks it.

Worked

(a) \(q!\,S_q = \sum_{j=0}^{q} \frac{q!}{j!}\) is a sum of integers, hence an integer; \(q!\,e = q!\cdot\frac{p}{q} = (q-1)!\,p\), an integer. So \(q!\,R_q = q!\,e - q!\,S_q\) is an integer. (b) Every tail term is positive, so \(q!\,R_q > 0\); Exercise 2(c) gives \(R_q < \frac{1}{q \cdot q!}\), so \(q!\,R_q < \frac{1}{q} \le \frac{1}{2} < 1\). An integer strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) cannot exist — contradiction, and \(e\) is irrational. (For \(q = 1\): \(e\) an integer, killed by Exercise 2(b)'s walls \(2.5 < e < 3\).) (c) P11 supplies the frame — assume the fraction, run the machine, watch it break — and P15 supplies the walls that break it: partial sums from below, tail bound from above, squeezing an integer out of existence. Witness \(q = 2\): the trap demands that \(2!\,R_2 = 2(e - 2.5)\) be an integer; in fact \(2(e - 2.5) \approx 0.4366\), sitting strictly between \(0\) and \(1\). The "integer" the fraction \(e = p/2\) promised does not exist.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Referee three students. Student A: "Every truncation of \(0.999\ldots\) is below \(1\), so \(0.999\ldots < 1\)." Student B: "Archimedes proved \(\pi = 22/7\) — his book says the circle is trapped by it." Student C: "The inscribed and circumscribed perimeters never touch — they differ at every polygon count — so the squeeze never actually certifies \(\pi\); it just gets close." For each: verdict, the section that settles it, and the demand or principle confused.

Hint

A trapped the wrong object. B read a wall as a prisoner. C is waiting for an event where the license is a rate.

Worked

A — reject: the walls enclose the truncations, and the number itself is the limit the ε-trap pins to exactly \(1\) (Example 3, first application; Exhibit 4 names the confusion). B — reject: Archimedes proved \(\frac{223}{71} < \pi < \frac{22}{7}\); the outer wall is a wall, not a value, and a loosened wall — his convenient fractions — is still a wall. Reading "trapped by \(22/7\)" as "equals \(22/7\)" mistakes the jail for the prisoner (§2; Exhibit 3's cousin, the frozen wall mistaken for content). C — reject: the certification never needed contact; it needs the gap below the demand — for every demanded size, some crank gets under it — which is exactly the ε-trap (Example 3). All three stumbled on the same root: treating the trap's approximations as the thing itself.

Exercise 5 ◐ — The price of certainty, bisection edition. (a) Run the first ten bisection steps for \(\sqrt 2\) from \([1,2]\) with dyadic bookkeeping, and explain why every test is integer arithmetic. (b) How many steps certify ten decimals? Justify \(34\) exactly, with the two powers of two that bracket \(10^{-10}\). (c) The Babylonian machine \(x \mapsto \tfrac{1}{2}(x + 2/x)\) from \(x_0 = 1\): list the errors through \(x_4\), compare the digits-per-step with bisection, and name the trade behind the difference. (d) Write an integer-only certificate for \(1.414 < \sqrt 2 < 1.415\), and say in one sentence what makes it a certificate.

Hint

(a) Every endpoint is \(k/2^n\); clear denominators. (b) Compare \(2^{-33}\) and \(2^{-34}\) with \(10^{-10}\). (c) The errors are tabulated in Example 3; count correct digits, not decimals. (d) Scale by \(1000\) and square integers.

Worked

(a) The intervals are \([1, 1.5]\), \([1.25, 1.5]\), \([1.375, 1.5]\), \([1.375, 1.4375]\), \([1.40625, 1.4375]\), \([1.40625, 1.421875]\), \([1.4140625, 1.421875]\), \([1.4140625, 1.41796875]\), \([1.4140625, 1.416015625]\), \([1.4140625, 1.4150390625]\) — widths \(2^{-1}\) down to \(2^{-10}\). Every endpoint is \(k/2^{10}\) at worst, and \((k/2^n)^2 < 2\) is \(k^2 < 2 \cdot 4^n\): integers only, no rounding anywhere. (b) \(2^{-33} \approx 1.16 \times 10^{-10} > 10^{-10}\), so \(33\) steps do not suffice; \(2^{-34} \approx 5.8 \times 10^{-11} < 10^{-10}\), so \(34\) steps do. The first \(n\) with \(2^{-n} < 10^{-10}\) is \(n = 34\). (c) Errors: \(0.414\), \(0.0858\), \(0.00245\), \(2.1 \times 10^{-6}\), \(1.6 \times 10^{-12}\). Correct digits go \(0, 1, 2, 6, 12\) — roughly doubling per step, against bisection's steady \(0.301\) per step. The trade: bisection needs only a sign test; the Babylonian step needs division and a contraction contract — speed bought with demands, and P22 owns the theorem. (d) \(1414^2 = 1999396 < 2{,}000{,}000 < 2002225 = 1415^2\), so \((1.414)^2 < 2 < (1.415)^2\), and squaring preserves order on positives. What makes it a certificate: anyone can check it with integer multiplication alone — no software, no tables, no trust.
