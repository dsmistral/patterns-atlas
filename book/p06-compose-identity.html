The Patterns Atlas · P06 · the sixth move of the spine

Compose with identity

Stuck on one object? Put it inside a composition with an object you control. The identity does nothing — that is its job. A familiar one manufactures witnesses. And the invariants multiply.

New word: composition. You have been composing all your life without the word. Whenever an operation takes two objects of a kind and returns a third — addition takes two numbers, multiplication takes two numbers, "then" takes two rotations — you can compose the second with the first. And among all the objects, one is special: the identity element, the object that composes without changing anything. Zero for addition (\\(x + 0 = x\\)), one for multiplication (\\(x \cdot 1 = x\\)), the do-nothing rotation for turning. This page is about a move that sounds trivial and is not: when a claim about one object resists you, compose it with something — often with the identity itself — and read the claim off the composition. The identity is not decoration; it is the probe that certifies the operation, and the composition is the machine that manufactures new facts from old ones.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned claims, P02 built tables, P03 attacked them with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, and P05 put every object in its uniform before asking it questions. Then the bridge B12 taught a recipe that none of those moves explains: multiply two pairs by the rule \\((a, b) \odot (c, d) = (ac - bd, ad + bc)\\), and the norms multiply — \\(N(a, b) \cdot N(c, d) = N\\) of the answer, always, with a second route \\((ac + bd, ad - bc)\\) giving a second witness for free.

Here is the tension this page resolves. B12's recipe arrived as a fact about pairs — clever, verified, and isolated. Why should cross-multiply and subtract, cross-multiply and add preserve anything at all? And why did the same shape of trick appear in B01–B02, where a 255-digit power was reduced by repeatedly composing with a small representative? The answer is that both are instances of one move, and the move has a name: compose with identity. The operation must have an identity element (a legality probe), the composition must carry an invariant that multiplies (the arithmetic engine), and the object you compose with is yours to choose (the control). B12 is this move's carrier — this page assembles the move, proves nothing by authority, and points the machine at new game.

2 · Meet the problem

Two problems, one geometric and one trigonometric. No names yet — just watch what solves them.

Problem one. Find every right triangle with whole-number legs and hypotenuse 65. Hunting by hand: the legs \\(x, y\\) must satisfy \\(x^2 + y^2 = 65^2 = 4225\\), so try squares near 4225 and hope the gap is a square: \\(4225 - 63^2 = 256 = 16^2\\) — a hit, \\(63^2 + 16^2 = 4225\\), the \\((16, 63, 65)\\) triangle. But is that all? The hunt gives no completeness, and each further hit would cost another search. Now watch B12's recipe do the same work. The hypotenuse factors as \\(65 = 5 \cdot 13\\), and B12's Lecture 0 already wrote 65 as a sum of two squares two ways: \\(65 = 1^2 + 8^2 = 4^2 + 7^2\\). Compose each representation with itself: \\((1, 8) \odot (1, 8) = (1 - 64, 8 + 8) = (-63, 16)\\) and \\((4, 7) \odot (4, 7) = (16 - 49, 56) = (-33, 56)\\). Both answers carry norm \\(63^2 + 16^2 = 33^2 + 56^2 = 4225 = 65^2\\): two more triangles, \\((63, 16, 65)\\) and \\((33, 56, 65)\\), manufactured — not hunted. Compose the two representations with each other, both routes: \\((4, 7) \odot (1, 8) = (4 - 56, 32 + 7) = (-52, 39)\\) and \\((4, 7) \odot (1, 8)\\) by the sign-flipped route \\(= (4 + 56, 32 - 7) = (60, 25)\\). Norms \\(52^2 + 39^2 = 60^2 + 25^2 = 4225\\): the triangles \\((39, 52, 65)\\) and \\((25, 60, 65)\\). Four compositions, four triangles, zero hunting — and the hand-hunted \\((16, 63)\\) is on the list, because \\((1, 8) \odot (1, 8)\\) produced it (signs are free; the norm only sees squares). A full census confirms: exactly four such triangles exist, and the composition machine produced all of them.

Problem two. What is \\(\cos 75^\circ\\)? No standard table row is memorized at 75 degrees — but 45 and 30 are. Turn the point \\((1, 0)\\) by \\(45^\circ\\) and then by \\(30^\circ\\); the point ends up turned by \\(75^\circ\\). Write each rotation as a pair and compose with B12's rule — the turning pair at angle \\(t\\) is \\((\cos t, \sin t)\\), so compose \\((\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2})\\) with \\((\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})\\):

\[\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4},\ \tfrac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}\right)\]

Numerically that is \\((0.2588190451, 0.9659258263)\\) — and both routes agree to fifteen decimals with the direct evaluation of \\((\cos 75^\circ, \sin 75^\circ)\\), plus the exact check \\(\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{6} - \sqrt{2}}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4}\right)^2 = 1\\). A composed answer, exact and new, from two memorized ones.

Both problems were solved by the same shape of move: the object you cannot crack is placed inside a composition with an object you control — itself, a familiar representation, or a pair on the unit circle — and the answer is read off the composition's invariant. Neither solution stared at the original object. The move has a name, and it is the sixth pattern of the spine.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P06 — Compose with identity. When a claim about one object resists direct attack, place the object inside a composition: an operation on its class that has an identity element and a multiplicative invariant — a quantity \\(I\\) with \\(I(z \text{ composed with } w) = I(z) \cdot I(w)\\). Then compose with an object you control: the identity itself (which must return the object unchanged — this is the legality probe that certifies the operation), a familiar object (whose composition manufactures new witnesses from old ones), or the object again (which squares the invariant). Finally read the claim off in composed coordinates, remembering that the invariant multiplies and that a second route, if the operation offers one, is a second witness for free.

Or, in words: don't interrogate the suspect alone — put them in a room with someone you know, and watch what survives the meeting. The "someone" can be the person who changes nothing (the identity: if the suspect does change in that room, the meeting-room itself is broken); a friend with a clean record (whose file you already have); or the suspect themselves (double trouble). What the meeting multiplies — the norm, the determinant, the angle — is the arithmetic you get out. No multiplicative invariant, no arithmetic: the room is just talk.

Box P06.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. A true identity must exist. The operation needs an object \\(e\\) with \\(z\\) composed with \\(e\\) equal to \\(z\\) for every \\(z\\). For pair multiplication this is \\((1, 0)\\): \\((a, b) \odot (1, 0) = (a \cdot 1 - b \cdot 0,\ a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 1) = (a, b)\\). For rotations it is the \\(0^\circ\\) turn; for square matrices, the identity matrix. An operation without an identity element has no "compose with identity" — and an operation whose claimed identity fails to hold still is not an operation; the probe is cheap, so run it first.
  2. The invariant must be multiplicative. The composition must carry a quantity that multiplies — the norm \\(N\\) for pairs (B12: \\(N(z \odot w) = N(z) \cdot N(w)\\)), the determinant for matrices. This demand is the engine: it is what turns compositions into arithmetic (products of invariants) and routes into witnesses. Term-wise multiplication of pairs \\((a, b) \otimes (c, d) = (ac, bd)\\) has an identity, \\((1, 1)\\), and fails exactly here: norms do not multiply — a hand check refutes \\(N((7,4) \otimes (8,1)) = N(7,4) \cdot N(8,1)\\) with the witness \\(56^2 + 4^2 = 3152 \ne 4225\\) (block 5 runs the story).
  3. The routes must be real. If the same composition can be computed two ways — B12's two routes \\((ac - bd, ad + bc)\\) and \\((ac + bd, ad - bc)\\) — both ways must give the same invariant (they do: both carry \\(N(z)N(w)\\)), and comparing the two outputs is legitimate only for the invariant, never for the coordinates: \\((-10, 11)\\) and \\((14, -5)\\) are different pairs with the same norm \\(221\\). A "composition" whose routes disagree on the invariant was never well-defined; a reader who expects the routes to agree on the coordinates has asked the invariant the wrong question.

Three-key diagnosis. The claim type: produce a witness, verify an identity, or compute an invariant for a composite object built from known ones. The problem shape: one object, stuck, whose factors or building blocks are tractable — the claim is about the assembly, not the parts. The tools at hand: a composition with identity and multiplicative invariant (the catalogue), plus small cases to run the probes. The catalogue grows as the book climbs — coordinates give rotations a full machine at B05–B08, and P16 composes whole problems.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. Each names every pattern used.

Example 1 (numbers) — the application: Brahmagupta–Fibonacci, read as multiplying moduli

From your class-12 shelf, one old friend: the complex number \\(z = a + bi\\), with \\(i^2 = -1\\). Read B12's pairs as complex numbers — the pair \\((a, b)\\) is \\(a + bi\\) — and B12's composition rule becomes plain multiplication:

\[(a + bi)(c + di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i\]

so \\((a, b) \odot (c, d)\\) is exactly the product of the two complex numbers. The modulus, \\(|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\\), is the length of \\(z\\) drawn as an arrow — and the norm of B12 is the squared modulus: \\(N(a, b) = a^2 + b^2 = |z|^2\\). In this language the bridge's identity becomes one clean line, and it is this page's application:

\[|z_1 z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 \, |z_2|^2\]

Why believe it? Box P06.A, demand by demand. Identity: \\((1, 0)\\), the number \\(1\\) itself — probe it: \\((a,b) \odot (1,0) = (a,b)\\) over 169 pairs, zero exceptions. Multiplicative invariant: the norm — check: \\(N(z \odot w) = N(z)N(w)\\) on both routes over all \\(11^4 = 14{,}641\\) quadruples \\(a, b, c, d\\) from 0 to 10, zero mismatches. Routes: B12's two routes give different output pairs with the same invariant — two witnesses per composition. And one lemma pays for itself again and again: the conjugate \\(\bar{z} = a - bi\\) (flip the second coordinate) satisfies \\(z \bar{z} = (a^2 + b^2) + 0i\\) — composing with the conjugate parks the whole norm on the horizontal axis. Check \\(z \bar{z} = (N(z), 0)\\) over all \\(13^4 = 28{,}561\\) quadruples.

Now aim the machine. Composing with the identity manufactures nothing — that is its certification. Composing with \\((1, 1)\\) — the norm-2 pair — manufactures a doubler: \\((a, b) \odot (1, 1) = (a - b,\ a + b)\\), whose norm is \\((a-b)^2 + (a+b)^2 = 2(a^2 + b^2)\\). That is the doubling identity \\(2(a^2 + b^2) = (a+b)^2 + (a-b)^2\\), discovered rather than memorized, and it holds because \\(2 = N(1,1)\\) and norms multiply. Witness: \\((3, 4)\\), norm 25, composes to \\((-1, 7)\\), norm \\(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\\). Fig P06.2 shows both compositions as one machine.

And the witness factory scales. B12's Lecture 0 produced \\(221 = 17 \cdot 13\\) through both routes — \\((-10, 11)\\) and \\((14, -5)\\); the encounter produced four hypotenuse-65 triangles by squaring and cross-composing. Here is a census, done the composed way. Is \\(1025\\) a sum of two squares, and in how many ways? Factor: \\(1025 = 5^2 \cdot 41\\), with \\(5 = 1^2 + 2^2\\) and \\(41 = 4^2 + 5^2\\). Build \\(5^2\\) first: \\((1,2) \odot (1,2)\\) gives the two routes \\((-3, 4)\\) and \\((5, 0)\\) — the second trivial, parked on the axis (composing a pair with itself can park a witness on the axis; the conjugation lemma is the same phenomenon pointed backwards). Compose each with \\((4, 5)\\): from \\((3,4)\\), the routes give \\((-8, 31)\\) and \\((32, -1)\\); from \\((5, 0)\\), both routes give \\((20, 25)\\). So \\(1025 = 1^2 + 32^2 = 8^2 + 31^2 = 20^2 + 25^2\\) — three representations, every one route-produced, one of them needing the trivial rep. A full census confirms three is exactly right (every pair with \\(x, y \le 33\\) scanned) and that all three arise this way. Hunting the same census by hand means testing 32 candidate squares; composing means three multiplications. Moves used: P06, anchored on B12's two routes; the census discipline is P02's; the small probes are P02's detector honesty.

Example 2 (geometry) — rotations: the addition formulas are B12 on the circle

Rotate the plane by \\(\alpha\\), then by \\(\beta\\): the result is a rotation by \\(\alpha + \beta\\). Now write each rotation as what it does to the point \\((1, 0)\\) — it moves it to \\((\cos t, \sin t)\\) — and compose with B12's rule. The composition \\((\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha) \odot (\cos\beta, \sin\beta)\\) is

\[(\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta,\ \ \cos\alpha\sin\beta + \sin\alpha\cos\beta) = (\cos(\alpha+\beta),\ \sin(\alpha+\beta))\]

The angle-addition formulas — usually memorized as separate facts — are one fact: they are B12's recipe applied to the circle. The invariant explains why the circle stays the circle: every rotation pair has norm \\(1\\), so composing two of them multiplies norms \\(1 \cdot 1 = 1\\) — the answer is on the circle again, automatically. Check the composition formula over the full grid \\(\alpha, \beta \in \{-180^\circ, -172.8^\circ, \dots, 180^\circ\}\\) — \\(51 \times 51 = 2{,}601\\) pairs — with worst coordinate error \\(1.221 \times 10^{-15}\\) (and the composed norms never stray more than \\(4.441 \times 10^{-16}\\) from \\(1\\)). The encounter's \\(\cos 75^\circ\\) is one cell of that grid: compose \\(45^\circ\\) with \\(30^\circ\\), both routes agree — the exact value \\(\tfrac{\sqrt{6}-\sqrt{2}}{4} = 0.2588190451\\) against the direct \\(\cos 75^\circ\\) to ten decimals, and \\(\cos 70^\circ = 0.3420201433\\) the same way. Composing an angle with itself doubles it — the double-angle formulas \\(\cos 2t = \cos^2 t - \sin^2 t\\) and \\(\sin 2t = 2\sin t\cos t\\) are the self-composition, verified over 73 angles to \\(2.220 \times 10^{-16}\\). Fig P06.3 shows the composed turn.

One caution belongs here rather than in block 5, because the figure tempts it: the composition adds angles, and angles are not coordinates. What doubles is the angle, never the sine — block 5 works the false line \\(2\sin 35^\circ = 1.147 \ne \sin 70^\circ = 0.940\\) as a caution. Moves used: P06 on the circle pairs; the grid verification is P02's detector discipline; the exact-route-vs-numeric-route comparison is the two-routes demand of Box P06.A.

Example 3 (algebra) — the Fibonacci machine: composing a matrix with itself

From your class-12 shelf, the second old friend: the \\(2 \times 2\\) matrix, and its determinant \\(\det\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc\\). Matrix multiplication is a composition — an operation on matrices with identity \\(I\\) and a multiplicative invariant: \\(\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)\\), verified over \\(20 \times 20 = 400\\) matrix pairs to \\(2.293 \times 10^{-14}\\). Now compose the matrix \\(Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\\) with itself, \\(n\\) times:

\[Q^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\\\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad Q^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\\\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix},\quad Q^4 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\\\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix},\quad \dots\]

The Fibonacci numbers walk in: \\(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \dots\\), and the pattern \\(Q^n = \begin{pmatrix} F(n+1) & F(n) \\\\ F(n) & F(n-1) \end{pmatrix}\\) (check it for every \\(n\\) from 1 to 40, where \\(F(41) = 165{,}580{,}141\\)). Now the invariant does the work. \\(\det Q = 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1\\), so \\(\det Q^n = (-1)^n\\); computed instead from the entries of \\(Q^n\\), the same determinant reads

\[F(n+1)\,F(n-1) - F(n)^2 = (-1)^n\]

Cassini's identity — a real theorem about Fibonacci numbers — manufactured by composing a matrix with itself and reading the invariant. One composition, two routes (expand \\(Q^n\\) versus multiply determinants), one identity for free: that is the whole move. The same machine proves the addition formula \\(F(m+n) = F(m)F(n+1) + F(m-1)F(n)\\), because \\(Q^{m+n} = Q^m Q^n\\) — composing \\(m\\) steps with \\(n\\) steps is \\(m+n\\) steps; check it over the full \\(25 \times 25\\) grid, with the instances \\(F(10) = 55 = 40 + 15\\) (\\(m = n = 5\\)) and \\(F(12) = 144 = 105 + 39\\) (\\(m = 5, n = 7\\)). And the legality probe runs here too: \\(Q^n I = Q^n\\), the identity matrix composing like the identity pair \\((1,0)\\) and the \\(0^\circ\\) turn. Moves used: P06, with the determinant as the multiplicative invariant; the pattern-spotting in \\(Q^2, Q^3, Q^4\\) is P02's table discipline, its proof-by-composition the upgrade this page teaches.

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

The move's failures are worth cataloguing precisely, because most of them are near-misses: operations and pairings that look like compositions and are not. The probes of Box P06.A exist to catch exactly these.

  • An operation can have an identity and still be dead. Term-wise multiplication \\((a, b) \otimes (c, d) = (ac, bd)\\) passes the identity probe: \\((1,1)\\) is its identity, and \\((a,b) \otimes (1,1) = (a,b)\\) holds. It fails the invariant probe: norms do not multiply. Witness, as a false line: \\((7,4) \otimes (8,1) = (56, 4)\\), and \\(56^2 + 4^2 = 3152 \ne 4225 = (49+16)(64+1)\\). Mitigation: probe the invariant on small cases before trusting any offered operation — two lines of arithmetic. Price: nearly every operation you invent will fail the probe; that is what the probe is for. Default: identity probe first, invariant probe second, and only then compose.
  • Averaging is not composing. The encounter's two squaring witnesses \\((33, 56)\\) and \\((63, 16)\\) sit on the same circle, and the "obvious" ways of combining coordinates all fail the invariant. Their midpoint \\((48, 36)\\) has norm \\(48^2 + 36^2 = 3600 = 60^2\\) — a different circle, a 60-triple, not a 65-triple. Worse, the plausible mixed pairing — half-sum and half-difference of the two first coordinates, \\(63\\) and \\(33\\), giving \\((48, 15)\\) — has norm \\(48^2 + 15^2 = 2529 \ne 4225\\), and this is the false line to watch. Only the \\(\odot\\) recipe — cross-multiply, subtract, add — carries the norm. Mitigation: when a combining rule is offered, ask which composition it is the recipe for; if it is none, no invariant is coming. Price: the recipe looks arbitrary from outside (block 7 says where it comes from); from inside, the invariant is the explanation. Default: combine by a licensed route or say honestly that you have no way to combine.
  • Composing angles is not adding coordinates. Rotations compose by adding angles — but a reader who transfers that to coordinates writes the false line \\(2\sin 35^\circ = 1.147\\) against the true \\(\sin 70^\circ = 0.940\\) (both checked to three decimals; the discrepancy is the point). The invariant is multiplicative (norms), the composition acts on angles, and no individual coordinate is additive under the composition. Mitigation: compose in the object that owns the identity — the circle pair \\((\cos t, \sin t)\\) — and read coordinates out only at the end. Default: this is exactly what Example 2 does; imitate it.
  • Some classes never compose at all — and a census can prove the "never". Sums of two squares compose (B12). Sums of four squares compose (Euler's four-square identity — verify it over all \\(65{,}536\\) pairs of quadruples from 0 to 3, and use it to climb: \\(105\\), which is not a sum of two squares at all, is \\(2^2 + 7^2 + 6^2 + 4^2\\), and composition carries it onward). But sums of three squares do not compose, and the reason is a single honest number: \\(15\\). Both \\(3 = 1 + 1 + 1\\) and \\(5 = 4 + 1 + 0\\) are three-square sums, their product is 15, and \\(15\\) is not a three-square sum — so the class is not closed, so no identity-composition on it can exist, no matter how clever the recipe. Three censuses over \\(1\\) to \\(100\\): 43 numbers are sums of two squares, all 100 are sums of four squares, and exactly 15 refuse three squares — \\(7, 15, 23, 28, 31, 39, 47, 55, 60, 63, 71, 79, 87, 92, 95\\) — matching Legendre's criterion (numbers of the form \\(4^a(8b+7)\\)) exactly, in both directions. Honest scope: the full story of which dimensions admit norm-multiplying compositions is a famous theorem (Hurwitz: only 1, 2, 4 and 8) and belongs to a much later tier of this book — an orientation page, forward-referenced, not proved here. What this page certifies is the ground floor: closure is a prerequisite, and a census is how you test it.
  • The identity itself can be misread as "doing something". Composing with \\((1,0)\\) returns the object — \\((2,3) \odot (1,0) = (2,3)\\), \\(4 + 9 = 13\\) unchanged — and a reader expecting the composition to "generate" something new is tempted to skip the identity as useless. The mistake runs the other way: the identity is the certification, the one composition whose output is known in advance, and an operation that fails it (a "composition" that changes the object when composed with its own identity) was never an operation. The 169-pair identity sweep and the four-square identity's \\((1,0,0,0)\\) row are this probe running. Default: run the identity row first, every time, on every new machine.

  • You want the deeper story of what composition preserves → P09 · Find the invariant: the multiplicative invariant of this page is the general invariant discipline's most behaved citizen.

  • Your probes keep failing on a class you care about → that is a result, not a failure: the three-square census of block 5 is the model — record the refuser, name the criterion if one is known, and defer the walls honestly (the Hurwitz 1-2-4-8 story is tier-5 orientation material).

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. The composition of two-square sums is older than its name: Brahmagupta's Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta (628 CE) states the two-route rule for combining differences of squares — his word bhāvanā means exactly "composition" — as a machine for manufacturing new solutions from old ones, which is this page's witness factory verbatim. Fibonacci's Liber Quadratorum (1225) carries the same identity into Europe, and Euler's 1748 letter to Goldbach extends the machine to four squares. The complex-number reading — pairs as arrows, norms as squared lengths — is Gauss's language, and Hamilton's 1843 quaternions are the four-square composition discovered as a number system (he carved them into a bridge; ask block 5's Hurwitz pointer why the story stops at eight). None of this history is load-bearing; it says the machine you just learned to aim has been standard issue for fourteen centuries.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

(a) Compose \\((2, 3)\\) with the identity \\((1, 0)\\), both routes, and confirm the norm is untouched: \\(2^2 + 3^2 = 4 + 9 = 13\\). (b) Compose \\((1, 4)\\) with \\((2, 3)\\) — both routes — and confirm each output carries norm \\(221 = 17 \cdot 13\\).

Hint

The recipe is \\((a, b) \odot (c, d) = (ac - bd,\ ad + bc)\\); the second route swaps the sign: \\((ac + bd,\ ad - bc)\\). With \\(c = 1, d = 0\\) every product term containing \\(d\\) dies.

Worked answer

(a) \\((2,3) \odot (1,0) = (2 \cdot 1 - 3 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 0 + 3 \cdot 1) = (2, 3)\\), and the sign-flipped route gives \\((2 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 0,\ 2 \cdot 0 - 3 \cdot 1) = (2, -3)\\) — same object up to the free sign, norm \\(4 + 9 = 13\\) both times. (b) Route one: \\((1 \cdot 2 - 4 \cdot 3,\ 1 \cdot 3 + 4 \cdot 2) = (-10, 11)\\), norm \\(100 + 121 = 221\\). Route two: \\((1 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3,\ 1 \cdot 3 - 4 \cdot 2) = (14, -5)\\), norm \\(196 + 25 = 221\\). Two witnesses, one composition — B12's Lecture 0, reproduced as a warm-up.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Compose a general pair \\((a, b)\\) with \\((1, 1)\\). Show that the norm of the output is exactly twice the norm of the input, and check the instance \\((3, 4) \odot (1, 1) = (-1, 7)\\): \\(50 = 1 + 49\\). Then answer precisely: why is \\((1, 1)\\) not an identity — and what invariant value must a true identity always carry?

Hint

Expand \\((a-b)^2 + (a+b)^2\\) and watch the cross terms cancel. For the last question: if \\(e\\) is an identity and invariants multiply, compute \\(I(e) \cdot I(z)\\) two ways.

Worked answer

\\((a,b) \odot (1,1) = (a - b,\ a + b)\\), so the output norm is \\((a-b)^2 + (a+b)^2 = 2a^2 + 2b^2 = 2(a^2+b^2)\\) — the doubling identity, discovered rather than memorized. Instance: \\((3,4) \odot (1,1) = (-1, 7)\\), norm \\(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\\). Why not an identity: an identity must return the object, and \\((a-b, a+b) \ne (a,b)\\) (try \\((3,4)\\): you got \\((-1,7)\\)). The invariant value: \\(I(e)I(z) = I(e \odot z) = I(z)\\) for every \\(z\\), so \\(I(e) = 1\\) whenever some object has \\(I(z) \ne 0\\) — a true identity always carries invariant 1, and \\(N(1,1) = 2\\) confesses immediately. The 169-pair identity sweep is this demand running on \\((1,0)\\).

Exercise 3 ●●●

Find every representation of \\(1025\\) as a sum of two squares \\(x^2 + y^2\\) with \\(0 \le x \le y\\), prove your list is complete, and then produce each representation by composition routes from \\(1025 = 5^2 \cdot 41\\) (Example 1 sketches the route; your job is to carry it out and to reconcile it with the census).

Hint

Worked answer

So exactly three: \\((1, 32)\\), \\((8, 31)\\), \\((20, 25)\\). Routes: \\((1,2) \odot (1,2)\\) gives \\((-3, 4)\\) and \\((5, 0)\\), both norm 25 — the second trivial, parked on the axis. Exercise 4 ◆

A student is asked to simplify \\(\tfrac{1}{2 + \sqrt{3}}\\), and writes: multiply numerator and denominator by \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\), giving \\(\tfrac{2 - \sqrt{3}}{(2+\sqrt{3})(2-\sqrt{3})} = \tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{1} = 2 - \sqrt{3}\\). Diagnose the move: name the pattern (or patterns) at work, and say what the fraction \\(\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{2-\sqrt{3}}\\) really is.

Worked answer

The fraction \\(\tfrac{2-\sqrt{3}}{2-\sqrt{3}}\\) is the multiplicative identity \\(1\\), wearing a chosen shape — and multiplying by it is P06, compose with identity, in its purest form: the composition changes nothing (legality, Box P06.A demand 1) while the chosen shape of the identity does all the work, moving the object into the canonical form \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\) with a rational denominator (P05's uniform). The conjugate \\(2 - \sqrt{3}\\) is not decoration: it is the shape whose composition with \\(2 + \sqrt{3}\\) parks the norm on the axis — \\((2+\sqrt{3})(2-\sqrt{3}) = 4 - 3 = 1\\) — the same conjugation-parking that pairs enjoy (Example 1's lemma), now on real surds. One shape of 1 canonicalizes; another shape of 1 would not. That is the whole move: identity guaranteed, shape chosen.
