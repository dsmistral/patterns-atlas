The Patterns Atlas · P31 · the first move of tier 3

The universal property argument

Don't describe the object; describe its job. Say which structure-preserving maps must meet it, and how uniquely — and every applicant for that job turns out to be the same object, linked by a dictionary nobody had to choose.

New word: universal property. You have met objects by their constructions all your life: a fraction is a pair of numbers with a bar, a matrix is a grid, a polygon is a list of corners. A universal property defines an object the other way round — as a job description: which maps exist into or out of it, and how uniquely. The word maps here always means structure-preserving ones (homomorphisms: functions that respect the addition, or the multiplication, or whatever structure the objects carry — the clock-addition rules of B02 were exactly this requirement). "Universal" because one description covers every possible applicant for the job; the sameness it produces is canonical — P05's word — because it involves no choices anywhere.

1 · Before we begin

P01 taught the habit of pinning: say precisely what you are asked, define every word, and never solve a sentence. P05 taught the reward: put the object in its appointed uniform, prove once, translate everywhere. P16 taught the license: build a dictionary between two worlds, make it carry the relations in both directions, and the solved sibling answers for both. This page is what those three habits become when they grow up.

Here is the tension it resolves. P05 appointed its uniforms — the lowest-terms fraction, the canonical surd — but never had to say what makes an appointment canonical rather than merely customary. And P16's dictionaries were built by hand, object by object; that is bearable when the two worlds are card games and grids of symbols, but mathematics constantly builds objects that share no visible parts at all, and whose constructions swim in arbitrary choices — orderings, coordinates, names, bases. Hand-built dictionaries inherit every one of those choices, and "the" in "the answer" quietly goes soft.

The move: describe the object's job, not its parts. A job is stated entirely in maps — which ones must exist, and how uniquely — and maps never mention parts. If the job description is sharp enough, it pins its holder up to a forced, choice-free dictionary, and every question worth asking about "which object" dissolves into a question about the job. This is the first page of tier 3, and it is the language working mathematicians actually use when they say "the product", "the integers", or "the" anything: the job first, the object second, the dictionary for free.

2 · Meet the problem

Try this one cold. In the ring \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) of polynomials with integer coefficients, find the greatest common divisor of \(x^2 - 1\) and \(x^2 + 2x + 1\).

Factor first: \(x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)\) and \(x^2 + 2x + 1 = (x+1)^2\). The common divisors in \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) are easy to list: the constants \(\pm 1\), and \(\pm(x+1)\). Now — greatest? For numbers, \(12\) beats \(6\) because \(12 > 6\). But polynomials do not come with an order, so "greatest" cannot mean biggest. Try the rescue you were taught: rank by degree. The survivors all have degree \(1\) — the word has died again.

And it dies worse. Work in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\), polynomials with rational coefficients, where dividing by any nonzero number is allowed. Then \(2(x+1)\) divides \(x^2 - 1\), because the quotient \((x-1)/2\) lives in \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\); it divides \((x+1)^2\) too, with quotient \((x+1)/2\). So \(2(x+1)\) is a common divisor of the same degree as \(x+1\) — and so are \(\tfrac{1}{2}(x+1)\), \(3(x+1)\), \(-5(x+1)\), … Infinitely many "greatest" common divisors, none greater than the others. The school definition has not stumbled on a hard case; it has stopped meaning anything.

Salvage it the honest way — not by patching "greatest", but by asking what all the survivors share. Take any two of them, say \(x+1\) and \(2(x+1)\): each divides the other, with quotients \(2\) and \(\tfrac{1}{2}\). Take any common divisor at all, say \(3(x+1)\) or \(1\): it divides every survivor. The survivors form a single family of mutual translations, and everything divides and is divided within the family — the family as a whole plays the role that "the greatest one" was supposed to play. Numbers that divide \(1\) — here, every nonzero rational — have an old name, units; multiplying by a unit never changes what divides what, so the whole family is one object wearing unit-multiples as hats.

Now go back to numbers with new eyes. For \(36\) and \(60\): the divisors of \(36\) are \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36\); of \(60\): \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60\); the common ones are \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\). Check what \(12\) does: every common divisor of \(36\) and \(60\) divides \(12\). That is the property that survives the trip to polynomials. And uniqueness comes free in \(\mathbb{Z}\): if two positive numbers each have every common divisor dividing them, they divide each other, and mutual divisibility among positive integers is equality. One survivor, no family — which is why school could afford the sloppy word "greatest" and get away with it.

Notice what just happened. We set out to rank objects by a size and ended up specifying an object by a job: "a common divisor that every common divisor divides". The job is stated in divisibility maps — arrows of the form \(d \mid n\) — and never inspects the object's interior. Holders of one job come in families of harmless translations; the mathematics lives in the job, not in the name. This page is about how far that idea carries: much, much further than common divisors.

3 · Name the move

§2 replaced a size with a job. The move, named:

Pattern P31 — The universal property argument. To pin down an object — or to prove two constructions the same — do not describe its parts. Describe its job: exactly which structure-preserving maps must exist into or out of it, and how uniquely. A job description is a universal property when it has (existence) at least one holder and (uniqueness) the property that any two holders are linked by structure-preserving dictionaries, back and forth, that commute with the job's data and are themselves forced by it. All holders of one job are then one object, canonically isomorphic — and any map you will ever need out of (or into) the object can be defined by naming the corresponding data and letting the job do the transport.

Or, in words: stop asking what it is made of; ask what it does for everything else. If the answer is sharp enough, the object is fixed — not uniquely, but uniquely-up-to-a-forced-translation, which is the honest version of "unique" this book uses from here on. Then prove two constructions the same the lazy way: show they do the same job, and the dictionary builds itself.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the claim is a canonicity claim. The sentence you must prove says "the" ("the greatest common divisor", "the product", "the answer is independent of the ordering"), or says "well-defined" about a construction drowning in choices. Jam-mode: the claim is a plain computation or an existence count — then no job description will help, and P02 or P25 owns the problem.

Key 2 — the objects are given by choice-laden constructions, or the map must be defined without touching internals. Orderings, coordinates, bases, names, representatives of classes; or: "define a map out of the quotient / into the product / from the completion". Jam-mode: the objects come uniquely with their parts visible and comparable — then P16's hand-built dictionary is cheaper.

Key 3 — there is a notion of structure-preserving map for the objects at hand. Group homomorphisms respect the table; ring homomorphisms respect addition, multiplication and \(1\); continuous maps respect closeness. The job is stated in these maps, so without them there is no job to state. Jam-mode: the "structure" is informal — then pin the structure first, which is P01's work, not this page's.

Box P31.A — the two obligations, and the dictionary nobody chooses. A universal-property proof has exactly two obligations, and both are checked in maps, never in parts:

1 · Existence. Exhibit one holder — a construction, a table, anything concrete. A job with no applicant is a description of nothing (§5's Exhibit A meets one).

2 · Uniqueness. Take any two holders with their job-data. The job applied twice produces maps back and forth between them that commute with the data; each such map is itself unique — any competitor doing the same transport must equal it, by the same clause applied one more time. And the two composites, compared against each holder's own transport to itself, must be identities — uniqueness, applied to one holder twice, does not merely say the holders are isomorphic; it says they are isomorphic by the only possible maps. That is what "canonical" means: the dictionary is part of the guarantee, chosen by no one.

The proof never opens either holder. That is the whole trick: the job is stated only in maps, so it can only ever produce maps.

Box P31.B — where the move appears. Products (Example 2) and their mirrors; quotients — the clock \(\mathbb{Z}/12\) of B02 holds the job "recipient of exactly those maps from \(\mathbb{Z}\) that kill \(12\)"; free objects — words over an alphabet, and the polynomials \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) of Example 3; localizations — the fractions \(\mathbb{Q}\), the smallest world where the nonzero integers come undone; completions — the reals, the home every Cauchy sequence was promised; and §2's greatest common divisor. The three-key signature: a "the" claim over choice-laden constructions (write the job first); a map to be defined without internals (name the data, ride the job); two constructions claiming one title (compare jobs, not parts). The failure signatures are in §5: existence can fail (Exhibit A), uniqueness can fail (Exhibit B), and the whole job can be read through the wrong maps (Exhibit C).

4 · The move at work

The move lives on maps, so each working names its maps before it trusts its objects. Three workings, one per family — numbers, geometry, algebra.

Example 1 (numbers) — the greatest common divisor, hired by job

The job. \(d\) holds the gcd-job of \((a, b)\) when: (i) \(d \mid a\) and \(d \mid b\); (ii) every common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) divides \(d\). Clause (ii) is the arrow-collecting condition of §2 — every divisibility map among the common divisors lands on \(d\). For \((36, 60)\): the common divisors are \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\), and each of them divides \(12\) — so \(12\) holds the job.

Existence and uniqueness, by the book. Existence: the job has a holder, and B03's algorithm produces it every time. Uniqueness, Box P31.A's way: if \(d\) and \(d'\) both hold the job, then clause (ii) applied twice gives \(d \mid d'\) and \(d' \mid d\) — the two forced maps, in divisibility language. Among positive integers, mutual divisibility is equality; in general the survivors form exactly one family of unit-multiples, §2's honest shape. Note what was never consulted: the size of anything. "Greatest" survives only as the name of the job.

The receipts. Bézout, from B04: \(12 = 2 \cdot 36 - 1 \cdot 60\). And the mirror job, P17's flip transplanted into arithmetic: \(m\) holds the least-common-multiple job of \((36, 60)\) when \(36 \mid m\), \(60 \mid m\), and \(m\) divides every common multiple. Prime factorizations \(36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2\), \(60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\) read the pair off: \(\gcd = 2^2 \cdot 3 = 12\), \(\operatorname{lcm} = 2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5 = 180\), and the two jobs share one ledger: \(12 \cdot 180 = 2160 = 36 \cdot 60\).

Moves used: P01 pinning "greatest" before answering · B03–B04 the algorithm and the Bézout receipt · P17 the mirrored job · P02 the census habit (the lab checks the job-pair for every \((a,b)\) up to \(40\)).

Example 2 (geometry) — the cylinder, hired as a product

Two builders, one title. Builder A builds "the" product of the circle \(S^1\) and the line \(\mathbb{R}\) as a flat table: pairs \((\theta, t)\), the angle read mod \(2\pi\), the height plain. Builder B builds it as a tube standing in space: the surface of points \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta, t)\). The two objects share no parts — one is a rectangle with a wrapping rule, the other a curved surface in three dimensions. Which one deserves the title "the product"?

The job. A product of \(S^1\) and \(\mathbb{R}\) is an object \(P\) carrying two readings \(\pi_1: P \to S^1\) and \(\pi_2: P \to \mathbb{R}\), such that: to give a map into \(P\) is exactly to give a pair of maps into the factors — for every object \(X\) and every pair \(f: X \to S^1\), \(g: X \to \mathbb{R}\), there is one and only one map \(h: X \to P\) with \(\pi_1 \circ h = f\) and \(\pi_2 \circ h = g\). Read it aloud: a point of the product is a point of the circle and a point of the line, and nothing more — and it is the nothing more, together with the uniqueness, that does the pinning.

Both builders hold the job, so both hold the title. Builder A's readings are \(\pi_1(\theta, t) = \theta\), \(\pi_2(\theta, t) = t\); Builder B's are \(\pi_1 =\) first-two-coordinates, \(\pi_2 =\) height. Each passes the test: the pairs \((f, g)\) through Builder A are assembled as \(h(x) = (f(x), g(x))\), obviously unique (an element is pinned by its readings); through Builder B, \(h(x) = (\cos f(x), \sin f(x), g(x))\) — and this is the same recipe in tube coordinates, unique for the same reason. So Box P31.A produces the dictionary between the holders: \((\theta, t) \mapsto (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, t)\), forced both ways, commuting with the readings. P16's dictionary — but built by the job, not by hand; no step of it was chosen.

A map made by the job. Take \(X = \mathbb{R}\) with \(f(t) = t\) (as an angle) and \(g(t) = t\) (as a height) — the pair of identity readings. The job transports it to the unique map \(h(t) = (\cos t, \sin t, t)\) through Builder B: the helix. The helix is not a new object; it is the pair (id, id) in tube clothes. Its anchor points — \(t = 0, \tfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi, \tfrac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\), at \((1,0,0)\), \((0,1,\tfrac{\pi}{2})\), \((-1,0,\pi)\), \((0,-1,\tfrac{3\pi}{2})\), \((1,0,2\pi)\) — are Fig P31.2's labeled dots, every coordinate recomputed from the formula.

The coordinates echo. B05 called coordinates "readings off a measuring scheme"; the product is what measuring schemes measure into. To give a point of the plane is to give two numbers — the plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\) holds the product-job of \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbb{R}\), with the two coordinate projections as readings. And the failure exhibit waiting in this example — an object where pairs factor but not uniquely — is §5's Exhibit B.

Moves used: P16 the dictionary, here forced rather than built · B05 readings as maps · P05 "canonical" upgraded from custom to guarantee.

Example 3 (algebra) — \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\), hired to hold "a number we haven't chosen yet"

Polynomials with integer coefficients — every school leaver has spent years computing with them, as formulas. The move asks what \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) is for, and the answer is a job: to give a ring homomorphism out of \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) is to say where \(x\) goes — and nothing else is needed. Precisely: for every ring \(R\) and every element \(c \in R\), there is exactly one ring homomorphism \(\mathbb{Z}[x] \to R\) with \(x \mapsto c\). The map is the substitution machine: \(p \mapsto p(c)\).

Uniqueness is the cheap half. A ring homomorphism respects \(+\), \(\cdot\), and \(1\); so it is pinned on every integer (\(n \mapsto n \cdot 1_R\)) and on \(x\) — and therefore on everything built from integers and \(x\) by adding and multiplying, which is all of \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\). One choice of \(c\), one map; the count is part of the job's shadow: \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) has exactly \(2\) homomorphisms into \(\mathbb{Z}/2\), exactly \(3\) into \(\mathbb{Z}/3\), exactly \(12\) into the clock \(\mathbb{Z}/12\) of B02. Existence is the school half: substitution behaves (\((p+q)(c) = p(c) + q(c)\), \((pq)(c) = p(c)q(c)\) — the lab checks both on sample tables), so the machine is always legal. Receipts: at \(x = 5\), the polynomial \(x^2 + 1\) reads \(26\); at the clock with \(x \mapsto 7\), the polynomial \(3x + 4\) reads \(25 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}\).

The map counts expose a scandal the values were hiding. On the five-hour clock \(\mathbb{Z}/5\), the polynomial \(x^5 - x\) evaluates to \(0\) at every one of the five possible \(x\)'s — all five substitution maps send it to zero (this is Fermat's little theorem wearing a formula). Yet \(x^5 - x\) is not the zero polynomial: over \(\mathbb{Z}\) at \(x = 2\) it reads \(32 - 2 = 30\). Two different elements of \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\), the same function on the clock. The free object remembers what the table of values forgets — and this is exactly the boundary of P20: over an infinite domain, agreement at enough points forces identity of polynomials, so there the values determine the element; over \(\mathbb{Z}/5\), all five points can agree while the elements differ. P20's theorem is a statement about which rings' value-tables do the job of the object, and which do not.

Moves used: P01 the job stated before the holder · B02 the clock tables · P20 the identity-power contrast that separates values from elements.

5 · When the move fails

Three exhibits, one per way the job can go wrong: existence can fail, uniqueness can fail, and the whole job can be read through the wrong maps. Each failure is honest — and two of them are productive.

Exhibit A — the job with no applicant (existence fails)

In \(\mathbb{Z}\), B04 proved that the gcd's two descriptions share one holder: the target of all common divisors is also reachable — \(d = ax + by\) for some integers \(x, y\). Carry that expectation to \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\) and take the pair \(2\) and \(x\). The divisibility job still holds: the common divisors are \(\pm 1\) (the divisors of \(2\) are \(\pm 1, \pm 2\); of \(x\), \(\pm 1, \pm x\)), and every common divisor divides \(1\). But the reachability job — a certificate \(1 = 2a(x) + x\,b(x)\) — has no holder: plug in \(x = 0\) and the right side reads \(2a(0)\), an even number, never \(1\). The constant term of \(2a(x) + x\,b(x)\) is \(2a_0\) whatever the polynomials are; a census over all \(a, b\) of degree \(\le 2\) with coefficients in \([-2, 2]\) — \(15{,}625\) pairs — finds an even constant term in every single case (the lab runs it).

Two job descriptions that coincided in \(\mathbb{Z}\) have come apart in \(\mathbb{Z}[x]\); one has a holder, one does not. The warning is exact: existence is a theorem to prove, never a feature of writing a job description. And the failure is a door, not a corpse: the family \(\{2a(x) + x\,b(x)\}\) is worth naming for itself — it is the ideal \((2, x)\), and families like it are P32's entire subject. The same disease kills products of fields: a "product" of two fields would have to be a field containing a copy of each, but the honest pair-ring \(\mathbb{Q} \times \mathbb{Q}\) has \((1,0) \cdot (0,1) = (0,0)\) — zero divisors, disqualifying it — and no field can hold the job at all.

Exhibit B — the clause that carries the load (uniqueness fails)

Sabotage the product job's fine print. Take the honest holder \(A \times B\) and weld on a junk point: \(P = A \times B \sqcup \{\ast\}\), with the readings extended arbitrarily — say \(\ast\) reads as \((0, a)\). Now every pair of maps \(f: X \to A\), \(g: X \to B\) still factors through \(P\): assemble the honest pairs and never touch the junk — the existence clause holds. But take \(X\) to be a single point sent to \((0, a)\): two factorizations now exist — the honest pair \((0, a)\), and \(\ast\). The job's word unique is doing all the work of excluding the junk, and the moment it is dropped from the fine print, every junk-welded object qualifies as "a product" too.

The lab's census makes the break quantitative: with \(k\) inputs whose \((f, g)\)-value is the junk point's reading, the pair factors through \(P\) in exactly \(2^k\) ways — uniqueness fails the moment \(k \ge 1\), while the honest holder gives exactly \(1\) factorization for every pair. The uniqueness clause is not decoration; it is what makes "the" mean anything at all.

Exhibit C — the right job through the wrong maps

The two four-element groups: \(\mathbb{Z}/4\), the clock, and \(\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2\), the double flip (call it \(V\)). Same size, both abelian, a bijection between them easy to write down. Are they the same object? The map-count probe answers without opening either one: count the homomorphisms into \(\mathbb{Z}/2\). From \(\mathbb{Z}/4\): the image of \(\bar{1}\) must be killed by \(4\), and on \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) everything is killed by \(4\) — so \(2\) homomorphisms. From \(V\): each of the two generators is free to go to either element — \(4\) homomorphisms. \(2 \ne 4\): different objects, with no tables inspected and no bijections audited. The census, brute-forced over all functions by the lab (P25's machine doing this page's arithmetic):

homomorphisms→ \(\mathbb{Z}/2\)→ \(\mathbb{Z}/3\)→ \(\mathbb{Z}/4\)→ \(V\)
from \(\mathbb{Z}/2\)2124
from \(\mathbb{Z}/3\)1311
from \(\mathbb{Z}/4\)2144
from \(V\)41416

Two honest footnotes live in that table. First, a single probe can tie: into \(\mathbb{Z}/4\), both four-element groups give exactly \(4\) — one census column proves nothing. It is the family of all probes that separates — here the vectors \((2, 1, 4, 4)\) and \((4, 1, 4, 16)\) differ in two coordinates. Second, the probe only works because the maps were chosen rightly: as sets, with all functions allowed, \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) and \(V\) are indistinguishable — \(4\) elements each, \(4^4 = 256\) functions between them either way — and the bijection certifies nothing. A job is always a job in a category — a chosen class of objects and of the maps that count — and reading it through the wrong maps proves the wrong thing. (P16's Exhibit 1 — the bijection that carried only four of the eight winning lines — was this trap with cards; the same-size lure, the same cure: ask what the pairing preserves, and here the answer is nothing.)

Scope, honestly. What P31 gives: canonicity verdicts ("the" made precise), forced dictionaries between holders, and maps defined without touching internals. What it never gives: existence for free (Exhibit A — a job posting may have no applicant, and fields have no products), uniqueness for free (Exhibit B — drop the clause and junk qualifies), verdicts outside its category (Exhibit C — count the wrong maps, certify the wrong thing), or answers at all: the property transports uniqueness, not numbers — to compute, exhibit a holder and compute in it, then let the job certify that the number never depended on the choices. The default habit: write the job first anyway; it costs a paragraph and buys the fine print.

6 · What to try next

Doors from this page:

The nearest, and the reason this page exists on the ladder: P33 — the sheaf cohomology bridge. P33's whole subject is local answers that fail to glue, and "gluing" is a job description: maps defined on overlapping patches assemble into one map on the union exactly when they agree on the overlaps — one more universal property, this time with an obstruction waiting inside it. B09 and B10 already built the arithmetic instance: edge differences that come from vertex numbers exactly when every loop test passes.

The pattern's own sequel: P48 — the representable functor trick. This page counted maps to tell objects apart; P48 upgrades the trick into an identification: an object is its map-counts, and every question about the object can be moved into a question about the counts.

Two more consumers: P60 — group completion — formal differences, the universal way to make cancellation legal (the integers' construction, run on anything) — and P32 — ideal arithmetic, where Exhibit A's failed applicant \((2, x)\) becomes a first-class object. P30 — localization at a prime is the same move as \(\mathbb{Q}\)'s construction, run one prime at a time.

The default habit. When a construction smells of choices — orderings, names, coordinates, representatives — or a claim says "the": write the job description first (which maps, between which objects, existence and uniqueness); exhibit one holder; let every further map ride the job. When you need a number, compute in the holder — but keep the job in reserve: it is what proves your number never cared about the choices. That reserve is the difference between "the answer came out" and "the answer was determined".

7 · Lineage

The idea is younger than it feels. Euclid's Elements, Book VII, computes greatest common measures by mutual division — the job-holder's arithmetic, more than two millennia before anyone stated the job. Cayley in 1854 made a group its own table — structure as behavior, not substance — the first step toward objects known by what they do. The language arrived in the 1940s: Eilenberg and Mac Lane's 1945 paper "General Theory of Natural Equivalences" (Transactions of the AMS 58) put maps first and named the arena; Pierre Samuel's 1948 "On Universal Mappings and Free Topological Groups" (Bulletin of the AMS 54) gave the universal mapping its name. Yoneda's 1954 module-homology paper contains the lemma that turns this page's uniqueness argument into an identity — an object is its map-counts; Grothendieck's 1957 Tôhoku paper made universal properties the organizing principle of a whole field; Kan in 1958 named the adjoint functors, the machine that pairs every construction-job with a restriction-job. The dates are receipts, not load-bearing: every claim above stands on this page's own mathematics, and the sources are in the page log.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Two jobs, verified. For \(84\) and \(120\): (a) list the common divisors and check that each divides \(12\); (b) show that every common multiple of \(84\) and \(120\) is a multiple of \(840\), by comparing prime factorizations exponent by exponent; (c) verify the shared ledger \(\gcd \cdot \operatorname{lcm} = 84 \cdot 120\); (d) produce a Bézout certificate \(12 = u \cdot 84 + v \cdot 120\) with integer \(u, v\), and check it.

Hint

\(84 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 7\) and \(120 = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5\). For (d), run Euclid: \(120 = 1 \cdot 84 + 36\), \(84 = 2 \cdot 36 + 12\), and back-substitute.

Worked answer

(a) Divisors of \(84\): \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 12, 14, 21, 28, 42, 84\); of \(120\): \(1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20, 24, 30, 40, 60, 120\); common: \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\) — and \(1, 2, 3, 4, 6\) each divide \(12\) ✓. (b) A common multiple \(m\) must carry at least the larger exponent of each prime: \(v_2(m) \ge 3\), \(v_3(m) \ge 1\), \(v_5(m) \ge 1\), \(v_7(m) \ge 1\) — so \(2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 840\) divides \(m\). (c) \(\gcd = 2^2 \cdot 3 = 12\), \(\operatorname{lcm} = 2^3 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 840\), and \(12 \cdot 840 = 10{,}080 = 84 \cdot 120\) ✓. (d) From \(12 = 84 - 2 \cdot 36 = 84 - 2(120 - 84) = 3 \cdot 84 - 2 \cdot 120\): check \(3 \cdot 84 = 252\), \(2 \cdot 120 = 240\), \(252 - 240 = 12\) ✓. The lab's census verifies the job-pair for every \((a, b)\) with \(2 \le a, b \le 40\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The product job, by hand and by census. Let \(A = \{0, 1\}\), \(B = \{a, b\}\), \(X = \{p, q, r\}\), with \(f(p) = 0, f(q) = 1, f(r) = 0\) and \(g(p) = a, g(q) = a, g(r) = b\). (a) Build the honest holder's factorization: the unique \(h: X \to A \times B\) with \(\pi_1 \circ h = f\), \(\pi_2 \circ h = g\). (b) Prove uniqueness pointwise: any \(h'\) with the same property equals \(h\). (c) Now weld on the junk point: \(P = A \times B \sqcup \{\ast\}\) with \(\ast\) reading as \((0, a)\); count all factorizations of the same pair \((f, g)\) through \(P\), name both when the count is \(2\), and verify the \(2^k\) law on this instance.

Hint

(b) An element of a product is pinned by its two readings. (c) Only inputs whose \((f, g)\)-value equals the junk point's reading have anywhere to choose.

Worked answer

(a) \(h(p) = (0, a), h(q) = (1, a), h(r) = (0, b)\) — the pair itself, paired up. (b) For any input \(x\): \(\pi_1(h'(x)) = f(x)\) and \(\pi_2(h'(x)) = g(x)\), and a pair with first reading \(f(x)\) and second reading \(g(x)\) is \((f(x), g(x))\) — so \(h'(x) = h(x)\) for all three inputs. (c) The honest factorization survives; additionally every input \(x\) with \((f(x), g(x)) = (0, a)\) may instead be sent to \(\ast\), which reads the same. Here only \(p\) qualifies (\(q\)'s value is \((1, a)\), \(r\)'s is \((0, b)\)): \(k = 1\), and the count is \(2^1 = 2\) — the honest factorization and the one redirecting \(p \mapsto \ast\). The lab's census verifies the \(2^k\) law for all sets \(A, B, X\) with \(|A|, |B| \le 2\), \(|X| \le 3\), and all \(f, g\) — uniqueness fails exactly when \(k \ge 1\).

Exercise 3 ●●● — The probe that separates. (a) Compute by hand the number of group homomorphisms \(\mathbb{Z}/4 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\), by pinning the image of \(\bar{1}\). (b) Same for \(V = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2 \to \mathbb{Z}/2\). (c) Same for both groups into \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) — observe the tie, and explain why a tie does not acquit. (d) Conclude that \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) and \(V\) are not isomorphic as groups, citing only map-counts — no tables, no element-by-element comparison. (e) Sanity: why does the same argument fail to separate \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) from itself?

Hint

A homomorphism from \(\mathbb{Z}/n\) is its value on \(\bar{1}\), and that value must be killed by \(n\). A homomorphism from \(V\) is a pair of values, one per generator, each killed by \(2\).

Worked answer

(a) \(y = f(\bar{1})\) must satisfy \(4y \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) — both elements of \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) qualify: \(2\) homomorphisms. (b) \(f(1,0)\) and \(f(0,1)\) each free in \(\mathbb{Z}/2\): \(2 \times 2 = 4\). (c) Into \(\mathbb{Z}/4\): from \(\mathbb{Z}/4\), \(\bar{1}\) may go anywhere (\(4y \equiv 0\) always): \(4\); from \(V\), each generator must land in the elements killed by \(2\), namely \(\{0, 2\}\): \(2 \times 2 = 4\). Tie — but a single census column was never a verdict: an isomorphism would have to match the entire vector of probes, and \((2, 1, 4, 4) \neq (4, 1, 4, 16)\). (d) If \(\phi: \mathbb{Z}/4 \to V\) were an isomorphism, then composing with it would pair up the homomorphisms into any fixed target one-for-one — impossible for \(\mathbb{Z}/2\), where the counts are \(2\) and \(4\). (e) It doesn't fail — it separates nothing from itself precisely because the counts are trivially identical; the argument is symmetric, and self-probes always agree.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the claim (pattern-unknown). A student writes: "\(\mathbb{Z}/4\) and \(\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2\) both have four elements, so they're basically the same group. Also there are homomorphisms both ways — send \(\bar{1} \mapsto (1,1)\), and send \((1,0) \mapsto \bar{1}\), \((0,1) \mapsto \bar{0}\) — and a bijection besides. Same size, maps both ways: same object." Diagnose: which pattern's questions expose each broken step, and what, precisely, would have to be checked for the claim to stand?

Hint

Check the alleged maps really are homomorphisms. Ask what "same object" must mean, and what was never written down before the claim was made.

Worked answer

Step 1 — \(\bar{1} \mapsto (1,1)\): legal; \((1,1)\) has order \(2\), which divides \(4\), and the map \(k\bar{1} \mapsto k(1,1)\) is a genuine homomorphism. Step 2 — \((1,0) \mapsto \bar{1}\): illegal; \((1,0)\) has order \(2\), \(\bar{1}\) has order \(4\), and a homomorphism must send \(2 \cdot (1,0) = (0,0)\) to \(2 \cdot \bar{1} = \bar{2} \neq \bar{0}\) — P09's invariant (order) kills the map before any counting starts. Step 3 — the bijection: carries nothing; this is P16's Exhibit-1 lesson, a pairing that preserves none of the structure the question runs on. Step 4 — "same object": the claim is a canonicity claim, and no job description was ever written. Had it been — even informally, "the product of \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) with itself" — the obligations would have demanded: readings \(\pi_1, \pi_2\) out of the candidate, and unique factorization of every pair. \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) has only two homomorphisms into \(\mathbb{Z}/2\), so it cannot even supply the readings' role that the product's \(4\)-probe exhibits; and the lab's census finds, for every choice of readings, a pair of maps that fails to factor. Verdict: the sentence borrowed three patterns' vocabulary — bijection (P16), maps both ways, sameness (P31) — and checked none of their gates. The honest check that stands: Exhibit C's, map-counts first, conclusions after.

Exercise 5 ◐ One object, three map-counts (exploration). Count the structure-preserving maps \(\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/6\) under three different notions of "map": (a) arbitrary functions of sets — describe, at least, why nothing about the clock's structure constrains them at all; (b) group homomorphisms — pinned by the image of \(1\), which is free: verify that each of the six choices really builds a homomorphism; (c) unital ring homomorphisms — forced by \(1 \mapsto \bar{1}\): verify the forced map is a homomorphism on the clock's tables, and count. Then the punchline: \(\mathbb{Z}\)'s job as a ring is "exactly one homomorphism into every ring" — the initial ring; verify the count is again exactly \(1\) for \(\mathbb{Z}/12\), and say what Box P31.A's uniqueness clause then guarantees about any other object doing \(\mathbb{Z}\)'s job. Finally, the door this opens: if objects are known by their map-counts, what would it take to count the maps out of an object you have never seen the inside of — and does anything about the counting still depend on which maps are declared legal? (Where this leads is P48.)

Hint

(b) \(f(k) = k \cdot \overline{y}\) where \(\overline{y} = f(1)\); check \(f(k + l) = f(k) + f(l)\) on residues. (c) \(1 \mapsto \bar{1}\) forces \(n \mapsto \bar{n}\); check both tables of \(\mathbb{Z}/6\).

Worked answer

(a) A function of sets may send \(1\) to \(\bar{3}\), \(2\) to \(\bar{0}\), \(3\) to \(\bar{5}\) — anything to anything; no addition table is respected, and there is nothing to check because no structure was promised. (b) Six maps, one per element \(\overline{y}\): \(f(k) = k\overline{y} \bmod 6\); \(f(k+l) = (k+l)\overline{y} = k\overline{y} + l\overline{y}\) — a homomorphism for each of the six choices, and every group homomorphism arises this way (it is pinned by \(f(1)\)). (c) Unital ring homomorphisms must send \(1 \mapsto \bar{1}\), so \(n \mapsto \overline{n}\); on the tables: \(\overline{a + b}\) maps to \(\overline{a+b}\) ✓ and \(\overline{ab}\) to \(\overline{ab}\) ✓ — exactly one. And the door: every count above changed when the class of legal maps changed — \(6\) versus \(1\), free versus forced — so map-counting inherits the category's fine print exactly as Exhibit C warned; P48's first move will be to make that fine print itself an object of study.
