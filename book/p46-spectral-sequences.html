The Patterns Atlas · P46 · the first move of tier 5

Spectral sequences

When the data comes in layers, do not flatten it: compute homology one page at a time, let each page kill what the next layer exposes, and read the invariant off the page that stops changing.

New word: filtration. A filtration is data organized in layers — the way a building is organized into wings, a census into districts, a signal into octaves. Formally: your vector spaces are indexed by a layer number \(p\), and the honest structure is "everything in layer \(p\)" for each \(p\), with the maps respecting the layers. A page of a spectral sequence (written \(E_r\), "the \(r\)-th term") is a ledger: a grid of vector spaces, one cell per layer \(p\) and floor \(q\), recording what the cycles-mod-boundaries look like after \(r\) rounds of bookkeeping. Turning the page means taking homology again: the ledger's own maps become the boundaries, and what survives is the next page. When the pages stop changing, that stable ledger is the limit \(E_\infty\), and it is the invariant: the surviving terms are your answer, layer by layer. That is the whole machine; what remains is learning to read it.

1 · Before we begin

P33 built the three-step machine — cochains, the difference operator \(\delta\), the always-true law \(\delta \circ \delta = 0\), and the quotient \(H = Z/B\) — and ran it on small shapes: a triangle of towns, three cameras on a circular room, one dial of a calculus course. Its section of doors ended with a promise: when a shape's data comes pre-organized in layers, the machine must be run in stages, page by page, and the staged machine has a name. This page is that door, walked through. The ladder is short because P33 did the climbing: cohomology is cycles modulo boundaries, the law that makes the quotient honest is a telescoping (P69, always), and the obstruction being counted is P12's named refusal, promoted to a space.

Two older habits pay off here. P42 taught the discipline of analyzing each scale separately and summing the stories; a spectral sequence is that discipline with teeth, because the scales interact — what one layer's analysis exposes in the next is exactly the bookkeeping. And P45 ended by pointing here: an assembly too big for one pass can be run as a book of pages, each computed locally, the last page carrying the global answer. This page builds that book for one honest, fully-checkable object: a tower of eight rooms, wired together, whose every layer, page, and survivor can be computed by hand in an afternoon — and whose page-by-page census nonetheless exhibits the one move no single-layer analysis can see.

The tension is computational, and it is old. Cohomology is a quotient: cycles modulo boundaries, one division, done. But when the complex has a thousand rooms in a hundred layers, the one-division route flattens a huge matrix, and the layer structure — the very thing the problem gave you — is thrown away at the first step. The question this page resolves: can the quotient be taken in installments, layer by layer, without ever flattening, and can the installments be trusted? The answer is the spectral sequence: yes, with a ledger whose every page is small, whose turning rule is forced, and whose stable page reassembles into the same answer the flattening would have given — plus one surprise: a differential that no layer sees, crossing two layers at a time, that turns out to be the whole story.

2 · Meet the problem

A tower of eight rooms, and every room is a panel of light switches. Six of the rooms hold one switch; the two rooms of the west wing hold two switches each. A switch-setting — a room's worth of ons and offs — is a vector over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), the two-element arithmetic of B02: add settings by toggling together, and "minus" is "plus". The building's wiring is fixed, and it has two kinds of connection:

  • Stairs run up within each wing: a linear map \(v\) from a room to the room above it. The tower's west wing: \(v_{00}\) sends the west ground floor to the west upper floor. Middle and east wings likewise.
  • Corridors run across between wings: a linear map \(h\) from a room to the room to its east, same floor.

Not every stair or corridor exists — the blueprint says which — and the wiring obeys three laws, checked room by room: going up twice always lands on the zero setting (\(v \circ v = 0\) wherever two stairs chain); going across twice always lands on zero (\(h \circ h = 0\)); and every floor-and-wing square commutes — up-then-across and across-then-up deliver the same setting (\(v h = h v\), which over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) is what "anticommute" says). Here is the tower, wires and all:

Name the rooms \(C^{p,q}\) — layer \(p\), floor \(q\). The total building is graded by staircase level: \(A^n\) is every room with \(p + q = n\), and the total differential \(D\) sends a setting up its stair and across its corridor at once. The three laws say exactly \(D \circ D = 0\), so the quotient \(H^n(A) = \ker D / \operatorname{im} D\) exists — P33's machine, one layer, done. The problem: compute \(H^n(A)\) for the tower — and compute it wing by wing, never flattening the building into its staircases. Why wing by wing? Because the wings are the given structure, and because in any honest large example the flattening is the impossible step: a thousand rooms make a matrix no one can divide by, while one wing at a time is an afternoon's work.

So you start the only place you can: each wing alone. Within the west wing, ignore corridors; the stair \(v_{00}\) is a map you can hold in your head. Cycles upstairs-mod-boundaries: the west wing's own homology, computed with \(v_{00}\) alone, has one class on the ground floor (the cycles are the settings \(v_{00}\) sends to zero — the second switch alone — and no boundaries land there), and one class on the upper floor (all settings, modulo the image of \(v_{00}\)). The middle wing, with its identity stair, has no classes at all: every ground setting climbs, every upper setting was climbed to. The east wing, with its zero stair, has a class on each floor. Write the ledger — one number per room, the wing-wise homology:

The first page: \(E_1^{p,q} =\) homology of wing \(p\) alone, floor \(q\).
p = 0 (west)p = 1 (middle)p = 2 (east)

Four classes. You have not used a single corridor. But the corridors exist, and they must be paid: a corridor sends cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries (the commuting-square law, one check), so each corridor descends to a map between classes — the induced map of P47's world. Run them: \(h_{01}\) sends the west upper class to the middle upper class — but that class is a boundary in its wing, so the induced map lands on \(0\); \(h_{10}\) starts from a middle wing with no classes; \(h_{00}\), \(h_{11}\) are zero maps outright. Every induced corridor map between the four classes is zero. The second page \(E_2\) — classes modulo the induced maps and their images — is the same four numbers. The ledger has stopped moving. And now the crisis, and it is the whole point of this page: the ledger is wrong, or the ledger is not done.

Flatten the tower (you should; it is small) and compute the total homology directly, staircase by staircase. The answer: \(H^0 = \mathbb{F}_2\) (one class), \(H^1 = 0\), \(H^2 = 0\), \(H^3 = \mathbb{F}_2\) (one class). Two classes in total — not four. The wing-by-wing ledger, run honestly through every corridor, still holds four. Two of them — the west upper class and the east ground class — must die, and no corridor can kill them: the corridors were all zero on the nose. Something in the layer structure kills what no single layer can see. The question this page answers: what is that something, can it be computed by hand, and can it be trusted? The answer is the spectral sequence's second page turn: a map that crosses two wings at once and one floor down, invisible to wing-wise analysis, forced by the very laws that made the wing-wise analysis legal. It is called \(d_2\), it moves like a knight on a chessboard, and in this tower it is an isomorphism — the two doomed classes trade places and cancel. Three questions, and the page is their answer. (1) Where does \(d_2\) come from — what is the zig-zag? (2) Why does the process stop — and why do the survivors always reassemble into the flattened answer? (3) What is the honest price of the whole bookkeeping, and when should you refuse to pay it?

3 · Name the move

§2 ended with a four-class ledger that must lose two classes, and no wing-wise map able to take them. The move that loses them, named:

Pattern P46 — Spectral sequences. When a complex comes pre-organized in layers — a filtration — do not flatten it. Build the ledger: \(E_1^{p,q}\) is the homology of layer \(p\) alone, floor \(q\), a small quotient you can actually compute. The layer-crossing maps descend to maps on the ledger, \(d_1\); take homology again — the ledger's homology, cell by cell — and the result is the next page, \(E_2\). Keep turning: page \(r\) carries its own differential \(d_r\), which moves \(r\) layers across and \(r-1\) floors down, \(E_r^{p,q} \to E_r^{p+r,q-r+1}\), and the next page is the homology of the current one. If the grid is finite the pages eventually stop changing: that stable page is \(E_\infty\). The survivors are the invariant, and the answer is read off by reassembly: the classes surviving at \((p,q)\) are exactly the classes of the flattened complex's homology in staircase level \(p+q\) that can be represented in layers \(\geq p\) but not in layers \(\geq p+1\). In particular \(\sum_p \dim E_\infty^{p,q}\) is the flattened answer's dimension count, degree by degree — the pages and the flattening cannot disagree, and each page on the way is small enough to compute by hand.

Or, in words: when the data is layered, take the quotient in installments. Compute each layer's own story first; the layer-crossing maps tell you which story-lines connect; take homology of the story itself, page after page, until the book stops changing. What survives every page is the truth — and the truth is graded by the layers, so the answer comes with its own table of contents. The move's one non-negotiable honesty clause: the pages must converge to the flattened answer, and the convergence — not the page-turning — is what a large computation buys you.

Where does the knight come from? Every \(d_r\) is a zig-zag in the original wiring. For \(d_2\), the recipe on a class \([x]\) at cell \((p,q)\): (1) push \(x\) across the corridor, \(hx\); (2) because \([x]\) survived page one, \(hx\) is a boundary up the stairs of the next wing — so lift it: choose \(b\) with \(vb = hx\); (3) push the lift across, \(hb\); (4) the class \([hb]\), two wings over and one floor down, is \(d_2[x]\). The commuting-square law is what makes the recipe well-defined — different lifts differ by a cycle, and pushing cycles across gives boundaries modulo boundaries; the check is Exercise 3's heart. The knight's move is forced, not invented: it is the shortest route across the wiring that no single-wing homology can see.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the layers are real. The filtration must be structure the problem handed you (wings of a building, a cover's refinement order, a scale decomposition à la P42), not a decoration. Jam-mode: the layers are arbitrary bookkeeping — then every page is an accident of your choice, the differentials are unmotivated, and the honest route is P33's single quotient, small or not.

Key 2 — each page must be smaller than the flattening. The entire value of the machine is that page-by-page beats all-at-once; if layer-wise homology is not easier than the total, you are paying notation for nothing. Jam-mode: the layers are real but dense — every room wired to every other — and then \(E_1\) is already the size of the flattened matrix; the census habit of P25, run on the blueprint first, tells you before you invest.

Key 3 — convergence must be honest. On a finite grid the pages stop changing for a boring reason: \(d_r\) runs off the board once \(r\) exceeds the grid's width, so some page is terminal. Infinite complexes are where convergence becomes a theorem with hypotheses — and where the tool's reputation for difficulty is earned. Jam-mode: the filtration is infinite and nobody has checked the convergence clause; then \(E_\infty\) is a hope wearing notation, and the honest label is "formal" until the clause is verified.

4 · The move at work — the tower, the census, and the shadow of the Hopf map

Three encounters, in order of scale: the tower of §2, run to its stable page with every receipt; a census of every small blueprint that could falsify the machine; and one honest look at what the same page-turning does at the research frontier — labeled sketch, with the gaps named.

Encounter 1 · The tower, page by page

Page one, again, with names. Give the west wing's basis names — ground floor \(e_1, e_2\), upper floor \(f_1, f_2\); middle wing \(g\) ground, \(k\) upper; east wing \(w\) ground, \(z\) upper. The wiring in coordinates: \(v_{00}\) sends \(e_1 \mapsto f_1\), \(e_2 \mapsto 0\); \(v_{10}\) sends \(g \mapsto k\); \(v_{20}\) sends \(w \mapsto 0\); \(h_{00}\) is zero; \(h_{01}\) sends \(f_1 \mapsto 0\), \(f_2 \mapsto k\); \(h_{10}\) sends \(g \mapsto w\); \(h_{11}\) is zero. The three laws are seven checks on basis vectors, all passing. Page one, with basis names now instead of dimensions: \(E_1^{0,0} = \langle e_2 \rangle\), \(E_1^{0,1} = \langle f_2 \bmod f_1 \rangle\), \(E_1^{1,0} = E_1^{1,1} = 0\), \(E_1^{2,0} = \langle w \rangle\), \(E_1^{2,1} = \langle z \rangle\).

Page two. §2 already ran the induced corridors: every one is zero on classes — \(h_{01}\) sends the west upper class to \(k\), which is a boundary (climbed to, via \(g\)); \(h_{10}\) starts where no class lives; the rest are zero maps. So \(E_2 = E_1\) as a table: four one-dimensional cells, \(\langle e_2 \rangle\), \(\langle f_2 \bmod f_1\rangle\), \(\langle w \rangle\), \(\langle z \rangle\).

The knight's turn. Take the west upper class \([f_2]\) and run the recipe of §3: (1) across: \(h_{01}(f_2) = k\). (2) \(k\) is a boundary in the middle wing — \(v_{10}(g) = k\) — and this is not a coincidence: it is exactly why \([f_2]\) survived page one with a corridor landing on it. Lift: \(b = g\), the unique preimage. (3) across again: \(h_{10}(g) = w\). (4) The class \([w]\) — east wing, ground floor, two wings over and one floor down — is \(d_2[f_2]\). Both cells are one-dimensional, and \(d_2\) is nonzero on a basis vector, so it is an isomorphism: the ledger's homology at page three kills both.

Page three, and the last page. The knight took \([f_2]\) and \([w]\); nothing else on the board is in any \(d_r\)'s path (\(d_3\) would need three wings of travel and the tower has three wings — check the arithmetic: from \(p=0\) it lands at \(p=3\), which is off the board — so \(d_3\) and beyond are structurally zero). The stable page:

p = 0p = 1p = 2
q = 1\(0\)\(0\)\(\mathbf{1}\) — \(\langle z \rangle\)
q = 0\(\mathbf{1}\) — \(\langle e_2 \rangle\)\(0\)\(0\)

Reassembly. The survivors, summed by staircase level, must equal the flattened homology — and they do: level \(0\) carries \(\langle e_2 \rangle\), level \(3\) carries \(\langle z \rangle\), and the direct computation of §2 said exactly \(H^0 = \mathbb{F}_2\), \(H^3 = \mathbb{F}_2\), everything else zero. The finer clause holds too, and it is the clause that makes the machine more than a dimension-count: the \(H^3\)-class can be represented by settings in wings \(\geq 2\) but not by settings in wings \(\geq 3\) (there are none) — its layer is \(2\), and the ledger says so, at cell \((2,1)\). The answer arrives graded by the layers: this is the table of contents promised in §3, and no flattening produces it.

The other reading order. Nothing forces wings-first. Filter by floors instead — floors are the layers, wings are the floors of the ledger — and run the whole machine again. Page one now holds a surprise: the ground floor, read as one floor-complex (three rooms, corridors \(h_{00} = 0\), \(h_{10} = \mathrm{id}\)), has two independent classes at the west cell, not one — the zero corridor keeps both west switches alive, and the identity corridor kills everything east of the middle. The floor-ledger's page one is a different table: \(\dim 2\) at \((0,0)\), \(1\) at \((1,0)\), \(1\) at \((1,2)\), all else zero. But its induced maps are not all zero — the west stair descends to a map killing one of the two — and page two arrives at two survivors: \(\langle e_2\rangle\) at \((0,0)\) and \(\langle z \rangle\) at \((1,2)\). The floor-route needs no knight at all: its page two is already stable, while the wing-route needed page three. Two reading orders, two page-counts, same survivors. The filtration was a choice; the invariant was not.

Moves used: the cohomology quotient (P33), small cases with receipts (P02, P25), the obstruction that survives every page (P12), telescoping under the laws \(v^2 = h^2 = 0\), \(vh = hv\) (P69).

Encounter 2 · The census: every small blueprint, both reading orders

One tower proves nothing about a machine. The honest stress test is P25's: enumerate every small blueprint of one-switch rooms — every grid size up to \(3 \times 3\), every stair and corridor either zero or the identity, every combination — keep the ones passing the three wiring laws, and demand of each: both reading orders, plus the direct flattening, plus the layer-by-layer reassembly, all four routes agreeing. Moves used: census by machine (P25), experiment with small cases (P02), stress-test the claim (P03).

Encounter 3 · The shadow: the Hopf fibration, honestly labeled

The tower is a toy. Here is what the same page-turning does in the wild, and this encounter is a sketch — the label is load-bearing. The Hopf fibration wraps the three-sphere \(S^3\) around the two-sphere \(S^2\), with circles (\(S^1\)) as fibers: a "building" whose every floor is a copy of the base space \(S^2\), indexed by the coordinate along the circle-fiber. Cohomology over \(\mathbb{Q}\) (rational coefficients — the same machine, different arithmetic). The layer structure is the fiber coordinate; page two of the spectral sequence — the machine here is called the Serre spectral sequence — reads \[ E_2^{p,q} \;=\; H^p(S^2) \otimes H^q(S^1), \] rational numbers in exactly four cells: \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((2,1)\). That is the tower's page-two table, with \(\mathbb{Q}\)'s where \(\mathbb{F}_2\)'s were. And the argument now runs exactly as it did on the tower: the flattened answer is known independently — \(H^*(S^3)\) is \(\mathbb{Q}\) in staircase levels \(0\) and \(3\), zero elsewhere — while the ledger holds one rational class in each of the four levels \(0,1,2,3\). The level-\(1\) cell \((0,1)\) and the level-\(2\) cell \((2,0)\) must die; the only arrow on the board with anywhere to go is the knight \(d_2: (0,1) \to (2,0)\); and the page arithmetic forces it to be an isomorphism, exactly as it was on the tower. Survivors: \((0,0)\) and \((2,1)\) — levels \(0\) and \(3\). The toy machine's page is the real machine's page; the knight is forced in both; the survivors are the cohomology of \(S^3\).

What the sketch is missing — named, not hidden. Three things, each a course of its own. First, the construction: why a fibered space produces pages at all — the filtration by preimages of skeleton layers, and why page two takes the tensor-product form — this is Serre's theorem, not proved here. Second, convergence: for the finite-type spaces of this example the pages converge for structural reasons like the tower's; the general clause has hypotheses, and §5 returns to them. Third, the ring structure: cohomology multiplies, the pages carry the multiplication, and on the Hopf board the knight is not merely forced — it is the only nonzero differential a ring map can support; that refinement changes nothing here but is decisive in real applications. What the sketch honestly offers: the shape of the argument — layer ledger, forced arrow, survivors — is exactly the shape you now know how to check by hand on a tower. What it honestly does not offer: a proof that the shape applies. The gap is the orientation page's permanent condition, and it is why this page's exercises stay on grids you can exhaust.

Moves used: the quotient machine (P33), forced-arrow reasoning (P11 — the pages cannot disagree with the flattening), same-problem-different-clothes (P16).

5 · The move under fire — three exhibits and the honest price

Every page of this book breaks its pattern on purpose. Here the exhibits are the machine's three standing taxes, and the scope box prices them.

Exhibit A · The ledger grades the answer — it does not always name it

The reassembly clause says survivors are the graded pieces: \(E_\infty^{p,q}\) measures classes representable in layers \(\geq p\) but not \(\geq p+1\). Over a field — every grid in this page — graded pieces assemble uniquely: dimensions add, done. Over the integers the pieces do not determine the answer. Suppose a ledger's stable page hands you pieces \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) in layer \(1\) and \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) in layer \(0\) of the same staircase level. The flattened group is an extension of one by the other: either \(\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\), or \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) — and the pages cannot tell you which. This is not a pathology; it is the famous extension problem, and it is why a converged spectral sequence over \(\mathbb{Z}\) can end one honest step short of the answer. The failure signature: "the pages converged, therefore we are done" — claimed over rings where convergence grades but does not assemble. Mitigation: work over a field first for the dimension story, then return to the integral ledger only for the pieces that matter, with the extension problem named. Default habit: end every integral run with the sentence the machine owes you — "graded pieces computed; extensions pending" — or show they vanish.

Exhibit B · Pages that never settle

On a finite grid, convergence is boring: \(d_r\) runs off the board for \(r\) larger than the grid's width, some page stops changing, done. Infinite filtrations earn the tool its reputation: pages can turn forever without settling; differentials can fire at unbounded \(r\), each one a new small computation; and a ledger can converge to something that is not the flattened answer it seemed to promise — the hypotheses that forbid this (boundedness, completeness — the names are recorded, the theorems are not proved here) are real mathematics, and skipping them has a documented history of wrong answers in the literature. The failure signature: an infinite complex, a formal \(E_\infty\) computed by pattern-spotting the early pages, and no convergence clause ever cited. Mitigation: treat the convergence clause as part of the computation, not a footnote — if the filtration is infinite, the clause is a required deliverable. Default habit: finite grids and finite-type spaces (everything in this page) converge for boring reasons; say which world you are in before trusting a stable page.

Exhibit C · The keeper's burden

The tower needed one index pair per cell and one arrow. Real applications run ledgers of dozens of cells through five or ten pages, every cell a pair \((p,q)\), every arrow \(d_r\) with its own \(r\) — and the folklore is honest that mis-indexed arrows are the standard wrong answer, not the exotic one. The floor-route of Encounter 1 showed the benign face of the ledger's sensitivity: a different filtration gave a different page one, a different collapse speed, the same survivors. The malignant face is human: a single transposed index produces a plausible, stable, wrong page — and unlike a wrong flattening, nothing crashes. The failure signature: stable pages, beautiful survivors, and a reassembly that quietly disagrees with the one independent check available. Mitigation: the reassembly clause is a checksum — always run the dimension count against something computed independently (the alternating sum is the cheap one: on the tower, page one's alternating count \(+1 - 1 + 1 - 1 = 0\) already matches the flattened answer's \(1 - 0 + 0 - 1 = 0\), and taking homology never changes it). Default habit: no stable page is reported without its checksum, and no ledger is trusted that nobody has re-derived with the layers renamed.

Scope, honestly. This page computed spectral sequences of small double complexes over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), where every page is a table of dimensions and every arrow is a matrix small enough to check. It did not: construct the machine for filtered complexes in general (the definition of \(E_{r+1}\) as \(\ker / \operatorname{im}\) of \(d_r\) on the page was asserted, and the zig-zag recipe for \(d_r\) was given for \(d_2\) and named for the rest); prove the reassembly theorem (both clauses were checked on \(394\) blueprints and used, not proved); touch multiplicative structure (rings on pages — the refinement that powers real applications); or prove any convergence clause for infinite filtrations. What this page did do: build the ledger habit on objects you can exhaust, show the one move — the forced arrow — that makes the pages worth turning, and leave the general theory at its named door: P47 for the bookkeeping of induced maps, and the references of §7 for the constructions. The orientation promise: after this page you can read a spectral sequence argument and check its arithmetic; building one for a new filtration is a research skill, and §7's map is where its practitioners trained.

Moves used: stress-test with special cases (P03), census as checksum (P25), name the obstruction — here, the tax (P12), invariant under page-turning: the alternating count (P09).

6 · The doors this opens

An orientation page's doors are closer to the frontier than most, and they are honest distances.

The nearest door — P47 · The diagram chase. This page's every "induced map" — corridors descending to classes, lifts chosen through stairs — was a chase done in miniature, and the well-definedness of the knight is a chase argument, one diagram at a time. P47 is the nearest door because the ledger's trustworthiness is exactly its subject: why maps defined up to choices land where they claim. Distance: one move. What it carries: the license to push classes through maps without re-deriving the quotient each time — and the comparison theorems that make spectral sequences a two-edged tool (one sequence mapping to another can settle questions no single page can).

The generalist's door — the construction, by name. This page ran the machine on double complexes; the general theory — filtered complexes, exact couples, the convergence clauses of Exhibit B — lives in the graduate literature, Cartan–Eilenberg's Homological Algebra of §7 chief among the doors. Distance: a course, honestly. What it carries: the right to build a sequence for your own filtration, rather than run one inherited from a theorem.

The customer doors — where the machine earns its keep. P52 filters roughness by frequency and direction — P42's scales turned into layers — and pages the singularities. P53 runs ledgers on families of shapes, where the surviving terms govern what deforms into what. P65's strip counts are filtered by energy, and the pages compute the invariant. Distance: each a full climb. What they carry: the reason the page-by-page habit exists at all — complexes too large for any single quotient, layered by the problem itself.

Default habit: meet a layered complex — check P46's three keys; run the ledger; checksum the stable page against the alternating count; and if the argument needs arrows between two ledgers rather than more pages, that is P47's door, and it is nearer than it looks.

7 · Where the move came from

Non-load-bearing, and honest about it: the page above works if this section is skipped. But the machine's history is one of mathematics' strangest origin stories, and it deserves its paragraph.

The spectral sequence was born in a prison camp. Jean Leray, a French officer, was captured in June 1940 and held at Oflag XVII-A in Austria for nearly five years. A professor before the war, he organized a university in the camp and — avoiding topics he feared might serve German military interests, by his own account — deliberately rebuilt his mathematics in the most abstract language he could manage. The two notes he published in 1946, back in Paris, introduced the sheaf, sheaf cohomology, and the spectral sequence in one stroke: the layered machine of this page, invented to compute cohomology classes he could not reach any other way. Jean-Louis Koszul (1947) gave the algebra its modern form within two years. Henri Cartan's seminar rebuilt the theory for its first great customer, and the Cartan–Eilenberg book (1956) wrote it down as homological algebra's first systematic account.

Then the customers made it famous. Jean-Pierre Serre's thesis (1951) — he was 24, and it made him the inaugural Fields medalist of 1954 — used spectral sequences to compute the homology of fibered spaces, exactly the sketch of Encounter 3, and turned homotopy theory from an art into a computational discipline. Grothendieck's Tôhoku paper (1957) re-founded the whole subject so that the page-turning became a consequence of axioms rather than constructions. J. Frank Adams (1958) built the sequence that still bears his name to chart the stable homotopy groups of spheres — the ledger habit at its most extreme, pages no one can flatten. And Pierre Deligne's La conjecture de Weil II (1974; Fields 1978) ran weighted pages deep enough to settle the Riemann-hypothesis-shaped questions of arithmetic geometry. The folklore quip attributed to Adams — that spectral sequences are like exact sequences, only harder — is half a joke and half this page's Exhibit C. From a prison camp's deliberate abstraction to the engine room of the Weil conjectures: the move's lineage is part of its meaning.

8 · Exercises

Five exercises, moons for difficulty: ●○○ read-and-compute, ●●○ multi-step, ●●● full argument, ◆ diagnose a pattern-less situation, ◐ open exploration. Hints first; worked answers after each.

Exercise 1 ●○○ · A unit grid, ledger and flattening

Take the \(2 \times 2\) one-switch blueprint with every stair and corridor the zero map. (a) Compute page one \(E_1\), page two \(E_2\), and argue \(E_2 = E_\infty\) (which differential could possibly fire?). (b) Flatten: compute the total homology directly, and check the reassembly clause cell by cell — in particular, verify that \(E_\infty^{0,1} = \dim F^0 H^1 - \dim F^1 H^1 = 1\) using the meaning of "representable in layers \(\geq p\)". (c) Among the ten valid \(2 \times 2\) blueprints, exactly three have \(E_1 \neq E_2\). Find all three, and for each say which cell kills which.

Hint

(a) Zero maps mean every room is its own class. (c) A nonzero page-one arrow needs a nonzero class to leave, a class to land on, and a nonzero corridor between them — write the three conditions on the four maps.

Worked answer

(a) \(E_1^{p,q} = 1\) at all four cells: every cycle (everything) modulo no boundaries (no stair fires). The page-one arrows are induced by the corridors \(h_{00}, h_{01}\), both zero, so \(E_2 = E_1\). The knight \(d_2: (0,1) \to (2,0)\) is the only \(d_2\) on the board with a target — and it lands at \(p = 2\), off a two-wing grid: structurally zero. \(E_2 = E_\infty\). (b) The staircases: \(A^0 = C^{0,0}\), \(A^1 = C^{0,1} \oplus C^{1,0}\), \(A^2 = C^{1,1}\), all differentials zero, so \(H^0 = 1\), \(H^1 = 2\), \(H^2 = 1\). Reassembly: \(E_\infty^{0,0} = 1 = H^0\) ✓; level \(1\): \(E_\infty^{0,1} + E_\infty^{1,0} = 1 + 1 = 2 = H^1\) ✓; \(E_\infty^{1,1} = 1 = H^2\) ✓. The finer clause: \(F^1 H^1\) is the classes representable using layers \(\geq 1\) only — the cell \(C^{1,0}\), dimension \(1\) — so \(E_\infty^{0,1} = \dim F^0 H^1 - \dim F^1 H^1 = 2 - 1 = 1\) and \(E_\infty^{1,0} = 1 - 0 = 1\), cell by cell ✓. (c) The page-one arrow on the ground floor needs: a class to leave (\(v_{00} = 0\)), a class to land on (\(v_{10} = 0\)), and \(h_{00} = \mathrm{id}\); on the upper floor, likewise with \(h_{01} = \mathrm{id}\) (the class conditions are the same). With \((v_{00}, v_{10}, h_{00}, h_{01})\) the three firing blueprints are \((0,0,\mathrm{id},0)\), \((0,0,0,\mathrm{id})\), and \((0,0,\mathrm{id},\mathrm{id})\): the first kills \((0,0)\) and \((1,0)\), the second kills \((0,1)\) and \((1,1)\), the third kills all four — its stable page is empty, and its flattened homology is zero in every degree.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · The other reading order, by hand

Re-run the tower of §2 with floors as the layers: the floor-\(q\) complexes are read along corridors, and the stairs induce the page-one arrows. (a) Compute page one: show the west ground cell holds two classes (both west switches), and find the other two classes. (b) Run the page-one arrows induced by the stairs \(v_{00}, v_{10}, v_{20}\); compute page two. (c) Show page two is stable — no knight can fire — and check the survivors against the wing-route's \(E_\infty\) of §4. Why did the two reading orders need different numbers of pages for the same tower?

Hint

(a) The ground floor is the complex \(C^{0,0} \to C^{1,0} \to C^{2,0}\) with maps \(h_{00} = 0\), \(h_{10} = \mathrm{id}\). (b) The stair \(v_{00}\) sends one of the two west classes to a nonzero class upstairs. (c) A knight needs three layers in the ledger's layer-direction.

Worked answer

(a) Ground floor: cycles at the west cell are everything (\(h_{00} = 0\)), no boundaries arrive — two classes, \(\langle e_1 \rangle, \langle e_2 \rangle\); the middle cell: \(\ker h_{10} / \operatorname{im} h_{00} = 0/0 = 0\); the east cell: everything modulo the image of the identity — \(0\). Upper floor: cycles at the west cell are \(\ker h_{01} = \langle f_1 \rangle\) — one class; middle: \(0/1 = 0\); east: everything modulo the zero map's image — one class, \(\langle z \rangle\). Page one: \(\dim 2\) at \((0,0)\), \(1\) at \((1,0)\), \(1\) at \((1,2)\), in floor-ledger coordinates \((\text{floor}, \text{wing})\). (b) The page-one arrows are the stairs, read as the ledger's layer-crossing maps. \(v_{00}\) sends \(e_1 \mapsto f_1\): the west ground class \(\langle e_1\rangle\) maps isomorphically to the west upper class \(\langle f_1 \rangle\), killing both, while \(\langle e_2 \rangle\) maps to \(0\) — page two keeps \(\langle e_2 \rangle\) at \((0,0)\) and nothing at \((1,0)\). \(v_{10}\) runs between two cells that hold no classes; \(v_{20}\) maps out of the east ground cell, whose classes were all boundaries already — nothing arrives to disturb \(\langle z \rangle\) at \((1,2)\). Net: page two holds \(\langle e_2 \rangle\) at \((0,0)\) and \(\langle z \rangle\) at \((1,2)\). (c) The floor-ledger has two layer-columns (floors \(0,1\)); a knight travels two columns and would land at floor \(2\), off the board: \(E_2 = E_\infty\). The survivors match: \(\langle e_2\rangle\) and \(\langle z \rangle\), in the original cells \((0,0)\) and \((2,1)\) — exactly the wing-route's stable page. The wing-route needed page three because its ledger has three columns; the knight there had somewhere to land, and it fired. Filtrations are choices; survivors are not; the price of a choice can be an extra page.

Exercise 3 ●●● · The knight's honesty, and its only unit stage

(a) Well-definedness, proved. The knight recipe of §3 asks you to choose a lift \(b\) with \(vb = hx\). Show the class \([hb]\) does not depend on the choices: (i) two lifts differ by a cycle of the middle column, and pushing that cycle across lands in the denominator of the target cell; (ii) changing \(x\) by a boundary of its own column changes \(hb\) by zero; (iii) changing \(x\) by the cross-push of a cycle changes \(hx\) by zero from the start. Name the wiring law used at each step. (b) The only unit stage. Among the \(38\) valid \(3 \times 2\) one-switch blueprints, exactly one fires the knight. Verify it is the tower in miniature: every room one switch, \(v_{10} = \mathrm{id}\) the only live stair, \(h_{01} = h_{10} = \mathrm{id}\) the only live corridors, \(v_{00} = v_{20} = h_{00} = h_{11} = 0\). Compute its page one, page two, and run the knight on named basis vectors; show the stable page is \(\{(0,0), (2,1)\}\) and the flattened homology is one class in degree \(0\), one in degree \(3\) — the tower's story, told with one switch where the tower used two. (c) Why can no \(2 \times 2\) blueprint ever fire the knight, wing-route or floor-route? And why does the \(2 \times 3\) grid fire only when read floor-first?

Hint

(a) The three ingredients: the commuting-square law \(vh = hv\) for (ii), the corridor law \(h^2 = 0\) for (ii) and (iii), and the definition of the target cell's denominator for (i). (b) The zig-zag is: the top-west class, across (it lands on a boundary — why?), lift through the identity stair, across again. (c) Count columns in each ledger.

Worked answer

(a) (i) If \(vb = vb' = hx\), then \(b - b' \in \ker v\): a cycle of the middle column at the knight's intervening floor. The target cell's denominator is, by definition, boundaries plus the cross-pushes of exactly those middle-column cycles — so \([h(b-b')]\) is zero in the target ledger cell: the two pushes agree. (ii) If \(x\) changes by a boundary \(x = vy\), then \(h x = h v y = v h y\) by the commuting-square law — so \(hy\) itself is a valid lift; pushing it across gives \(h h y = 0\) by the corridor law: the knight sends boundaries to zero. (iii) If \(x\) changes by \(h(z)\) for a middle-adjacent cycle \(z\), then \(h x\) changes by \(h h z = 0\): the push of a cycle's cross-push is zero before any lifting. Every step is one of the three wiring laws; none is a computation. (b) Page one: west wing: \(E_1^{0,0} = \ker(v_{00} = 0) = 1\), \(E_1^{0,1} = 1/\operatorname{im}(0) = 1\); middle: both floors \(0\) (the identity stair kills the ground and covers the top); east: \(E_1^{2,0} = \ker(v_{20} = 0) = 1\), \(E_1^{2,1} = 1\). Four cells — the tower's page one. Page two: the induced \(h_{01}\) sends the west upper class to the middle upper class, a boundary (climbed to via the identity stair): zero on classes; \(h_{10}\) starts where no class lives; the zero corridors stay zero. \(E_2 = E_1\). The knight: \(x =\) top-west class; \(h_{01}(x) = k\), the middle upper basis vector; lift: \(v_{10}(g) = k\) gives \(b = g\); push: \(h_{10}(g) = w\); so \(d_2[x] = [w]\), the east ground class — an isomorphism, both cells die. Stable page \(\{(0,0), (2,1)\}\); flattened: \(H^0 = 1\), \(H^3 = 1\), all else zero — every receipt of the eight-room tower, at one switch per room. (c) The knight crosses two layer-columns: any \(2 \times 2\) ledger, wing-route or floor-route, has only two columns in each reading, so \(d_2\) lands off the board — structurally zero, always. The \(2 \times 3\) grid read wing-first has two columns (wings): no knight; read floor-first it has three columns (floors): the transpose of the \(3 \times 2\) stage, and it fires — the census's \(2 \times 3\) row, knight count \(1\), floor-reading.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The student and the senior's note

A graduate student inherits a convergence note from a departing senior: a stable ledger of graded pieces, over \(\mathbb{Z}\), for a filtered complex whose total homology is wanted. She considers three routes: re-run everything by exhaustive machine check (the complexes are too large); compute the total quotient directly (the flattening was the thing the filtration existed to avoid); hunt a single obstructing class (the pieces all look plausible, no refusal is in sight). She chooses none of them: she sums the pieces along degrees, checks the alternating count against an independently known Euler characteristic — it matches — and then reports: "the graded pieces are computed; the groups are not determined." What move is she applying, what did the checksum prove and not prove, and what two ingredients does she still need before she can name the groups?

Worked answer

She is applying this page's pattern, in its Exhibit A mode. The stable ledger hands out graded pieces: classes representable in layers \(\geq p\) but not \(\geq p + 1\). Over \(\mathbb{Z}\), pieces do not determine the groups they assemble into: a level with pieces \(\mathbb{Z}/2\) in two layers can assemble as \(\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\) or as \(\mathbb{Z}/4\), and the pages cannot hear the difference — the extension problem. Her checksum proved exactly what checksums prove: the alternating count of the pieces equals the alternating count of the flattened complex (page-turning preserves it, and it matched an independent computation), so no dimension was lost or invented — the graded dimension story is right. It proves nothing about the multiplication-by-\(2\) maps between the pieces — which is precisely what distinguishes \(\mathbb{Z}/4\) from \(\mathbb{Z}/2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\). The two ingredients still needed: (1) the extension data — the maps realizing each piece inside the next, computed from the complex itself, not from the ledger; and (2) an argument that the extension sequence splits, or the explicit non-splitting — in short, Exhibit A's closing sentence: graded pieces computed; extensions pending. Her refusal to assemble was not timidity; it was the pattern's honesty clause, and the senior's note was incomplete without it.

Exercise 5 ◐ · Two knights, one blueprint — the open census

Encounter 1's tower fired the knight wing-route but not floor-route; its floor-first cousin (the \(2 \times 3\) census row) fires floor-route but not wing-route. The natural guess: on bigger grids, some blueprints fire both ways — a knight for each reading order, all four doomed cells trading partners. The census of \(3 \times 3\) one-switch blueprints, run in both reading orders, says otherwise: of \(308\) valid blueprints, \(12\) fire wing-route, \(12\) fire floor-route, and zero fire both — the two sets are disjoint. (a) Hunt the reason: what would a both-firing blueprint need, and does the validity law forbid it on \(3 \times 3\), or is disjointness a coincidence of small grids? (b) Test the boundary: the \(3 \times 2\) grid fires once wing-route, never floor-route — is the asymmetry structural? (c) Open end: a both-firing blueprint, if one exists, first appears at what grid size? Find one by machine on a \(4 \times 3\) grid, or prove disjointness holds in general — both are honest outcomes.

Hint

(a) Write the firing conditions for the wing-knight's corner cell \((0,1) \to (2,0)\), then translate the floor-knight's corner zig-zag back through the transposing table, and put the two demand lists side by side. (b) Count columns again — Exercise 3(c). (c) The \(4 \times 3\) census is \(2^{17}\) blueprints: beyond exhaustive hand checking, which is the point.

Worked answer

(a) Watch what the two knights demand of the same map. The wing-knight's cleanest source is the cell \((0,1)\): for its class to exist on page one, the stair above it must be dead — \(E_1^{0,1} = \ker v_{01} \neq 0\) wants \(v_{01} = 0\) — and for the knight to fire, the lift must be solvable and the final corridor live. Now read the floor-ledger of the same blueprint and fire its knight from the transposed corner cell \((0,1)\): trace its zig-zag back through the transposing table and it ends by pushing its lift through a corridor of the floor-complex that transposes to the original stair \(v_{01}\) — it wants \(v_{01} = \mathrm{id}\). Same map, opposite demands: any blueprint in which both knights fire from their corner cells is impossible outright, wiring laws aside. Whether a bigger grid lets the two knights fire from different, non-conflicting cells — the exhaustive census cannot say; it stops at \(3 \times 3\), where every firing blueprint fires exactly one way. (b) Yes: the floor-reading of \(3 \times 2\) has two columns (floors \(0,1\)), so its knight is structurally off the board — asymmetric by grid shape, not by luck. (c) Open. The census receipts: \(3 \times 2\): one wing-firing blueprint of \(38\); \(3 \times 3\): \(12\) wing, \(12\) floor, \(0\) both, of \(308\); \(2 \times 2\): structurally no knights at all. A \(4 \times 3\) grid has \(2^{17} = 131{,}072\) blueprints and knights with more cells to fire from — a machine's job, and either outcome (a both-firing witness, or a proof that the pinch generalizes) would be a genuine small discovery about two filtrations arguing over one complex. The census habit of P25 is how this page's author met the disjointness; a sharpened pinch argument is how it might be retired.
