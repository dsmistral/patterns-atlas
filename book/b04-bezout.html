The Patterns Atlas · B04 · bridge page, group A · before P13

Bézout's identity via descent

Run the Euclidean dance backwards and the gcd stops being a number you found — it becomes a combination you built: whole stacks of \(a\), whole anti-stacks of \(b\), adding to \(\gcd(a, b) = ax + by\).

What this page is. This is a bridge page: it teaches a concept, not a problem-solving pattern, and it directly extends B03 (the Euclidean algorithm). B03's dance computes \(\gcd(a, b)\) — but three of group A's open debts need more than the dance's answer; they need the gcd's address, its expression as \(ax + by\) for computable whole numbers \(x, y\). This page builds that expression — the climb — proves it by descent, and spends it at once: the inverse 1321 that B03 brute-forced gets produced instead, P05's fraction-uniqueness debt gets Euclid's lemma, and B02's scope-box promise about dividing congruences gets its precise condition. Take a pencil; you will hunt near-misses between multiples before you read.

1 · Lecture 0 — the phenomenon, before any notation

Task one. List the multiples of 25 up to 100, and the multiples of 11 up to 99. Hunt for the closest pair across the two lists. How close can you get — and can you get down to 1?

Do it. The multiples of 25 are \(25, 50, 75, 100\); the multiples of 11 run \(11, 22, 33, \dots\) up to \(99\). Within the drawn range the two lists never meet — 25 and 11 share no factor — but they come within one unit of meeting: \(100\) and \(99\) (Fig B04.1). And one unit apart means one subtraction away from a statement about stacks:

\[4 \times 25 - 9 \times 11 \;=\; 100 - 99 \;=\; 1.\]

Neither 25 nor 11 divides 1 — no single kind of stack lands on it — and yet 1 is a combination of them: four stacks of 25, nine anti-stacks of 11, nothing else. The smallest positive thing these two numbers can build together is 1. That is the whole subject of this page, in pencil.

Task two. B03 closed its (2026, 1491) story with a brute force: \(2026 \times 1321 = 2{,}676{,}346\), and \(2{,}676{,}346 = 1795 \times 1491 + 1\), so 1321 is the inverse of the class of 2026 mod 1491. Check both claims with pencil multiplication — two of them. Then ask the question the brute force cannot answer: is 1321 an accident — a witness found by luck — or was it sitting somewhere, waiting to be found? Task one says combinations of 25 and 11 can reach 1 without any luck at all. The quotients of the (2026, 1491) dance are \(1, 2, 1, 3, 1, 2, 3, 1, 8\) — and section 3 runs that dance backwards to produce whole numbers \(x, y\) with \(2026x + 1491y = 1\). Reduce \(x\) into the class range and 1321 appears. Nothing accidental about it.

Notice what the two tasks share. Both found the number 1 as a combination — the first by hunting, the second (in section 3) by a mechanical climb back up B03's chain of divisions. The hunt does not scale: for bigger pairs, the near-miss lies too deep in the multiples to see. The climb does — it reuses the dance B03 already ran. We now name what the climb produces.

2 · The idea, named

Box B04 — Bézout's identity. For whole numbers \(a\) and \(b\), not both zero, whole numbers \(x\) and \(y\) always exist with

\[ax + by \;=\; \gcd(a, b).\]

The dance finds them: run the divisions to the bottom (B03), then climb back up the chain, turning each leftover into a combination of \(a\) and \(b\), until the gcd itself arrives carrying its coefficients.

In words: the greatest common divisor is not just the biggest shared part of the two numbers — it is buildable from them alone. Stacks of \(a\), stacks of \(b\), and negative stacks: an anti-stack removes instead of adds. Four stacks of 25 minus nine stacks of 11 lands exactly on 1; the climb finds such landings mechanically, for every pair, every time.

Box B04.A — the reachable set is exactly the multiples of the gcd. For \(a, b\) not both zero,

\[\{\, ax + by \;:\; x, y \text{ whole} \,\} \;=\; \{\, \text{multiples of } \gcd(a, b) \,\}.\]

Every combination is a multiple of the gcd: the gcd divides \(a\) and \(b\), so it divides every stack and anti-stack of them, hence every sum of such stacks (B03's hand-me-down arithmetic). And every multiple is a combination: by Box B04 some combination \(ax_0 + by_0\) equals the gcd, and multiplying through by \(k\) gives \(a(kx_0) + b(ky_0) = k \gcd(a, b)\). Nothing else is reachable. In particular, 1 is reachable exactly from coprime pairs — that one line is the engine of everything section 4 does with inverses.

Three words get defined here, at first use:

  • New word: linear combination. A number of the form \(ax + by\), for whole \(x, y\). Task one built 1 as a linear combination of 25 and 11; this page's combinations are all linear combinations of exactly two numbers.
  • New word: Bézout coefficients. The \(x, y\) of the identity — the two entries of a gcd's certificate. For (25, 11): \(x = 4\), \(y = -9\). They are not unique: \(4 \times 25 - 9 \times 11\) and \((-7) \times 25 + 16 \times 11\) both equal 1. Exercise 3 builds the full family of them.
  • New word: certificate. A checkable object that settles a claim outright — here, a spelled-out \(ax + by\) that anyone can verify with one multiplication and one addition, without re-running anything. A certificate for \(\gcd(a, b) = g\) is stronger than the bare claim: it carries its own evidence.

Examples and non-examples:

  • Yes, coprime: \(4 \times 25 - 9 \times 11 = 1\) — task one's hunt, again.
  • Yes, gcd above 1: \(7 \times 462 - 3 \times 1071 = 3{,}234 - 3{,}213 = 21 = \gcd(1071, 462)\) — B03's task-one pair, its 21 now with an address, not just a value. (Check both products.)
  • No, wrong target: no combination of 4 and 6 is odd: \(4x + 6y\) is even whatever \(x, y\) are. Box B04.A says so structurally — \(\gcd(4, 6) = 2\), so the reachable set is the even numbers, exactly. A sweep over all \(x, y\) from \(-40\) to \(40\) finds only evens, and every even number from \(-396\) to \(396\) turns up in it.
  • Yes, with zero: \(\gcd(a, 0) = a = a \times 1 + 0 \times 0\) — the identity holds at the edge: one stack, and nothing.

The identity carries Étienne Bézout's name (1730–1783), from his 1779 treatise, which handled the general case and — more famously — the same statement for polynomials. But the coprime case was in print long before: Claude-Gaspard Bachet stated it in 1612, in the same book that carries the famous weights puzzle. Names in mathematics drift to the generalizers; the idea itself is as old as the dance it reverses. The claim that such \(x, y\) always exist — and the climb that finds them — is proved in section 4; nothing on this page rests on either name's authority.

3 · Worked by hand

Three workings, simplest first, each carried to the end. Under all of them runs Box B04.

(a) 25 and 11 — the climb, in the small

The dance (B03): \(25 = 2 \times 11 + 3\), then \(11 = 3 \times 3 + 2\), then \(3 = 1 \times 2 + 1\), then \(2 = 2 \times 1 + 0\). Last nonzero leftover: 1. To climb, read the chain from the bottom and substitute each leftover away. The bottom division says \(1 = 3 - 1 \times 2\): true, but the 2 is not yet a combination. The division above it says \(2 = 11 - 3 \times 3\), so

\[1 \;=\; 3 - (11 - 3 \times 3) \;=\; 4 \times 3 - 11.\]

Still a leftover on stage: the 3. The top division says \(3 = 25 - 2 \times 11\), so

\[1 \;=\; 4 \times (25 - 2 \times 11) - 11 \;=\; 4 \times 25 - 9 \times 11.\]

Task one's hunt, produced by three substitutions instead of luck. Check it: \(100 - 99 = 1\).

(b) 2026 and 1491 — the certificate of 1321

The same climb, seven rungs instead of two. The dance, from B03's section 3(c):

\[2026 = 1 \times 1491 + 535, \;\; 1491 = 2 \times 535 + 421, \;\; 535 = 1 \times 421 + 114, \;\; 421 = 3 \times 114 + 79,\]

\[114 = 1 \times 79 + 35, \;\; 79 = 2 \times 35 + 9, \;\; 35 = 3 \times 9 + 8, \;\; 9 = 1 \times 8 + 1, \;\; 8 = 8 \times 1 + 0.\]

Now climb from the bottom, substituting one division per line — and check every line by pencil: on each of them, the two products differ by exactly 1.

\[1 = 9 - 1 \times 8\]

\[1 = 9 - (35 - 3 \times 9) = 4 \times 9 - 35\]

\[1 = 4 \times (79 - 2 \times 35) - 35 = 4 \times 79 - 9 \times 35\]

\[1 = 4 \times 79 - 9 \times (114 - 79) = 13 \times 79 - 9 \times 114\]

\[1 = 13 \times (421 - 3 \times 114) - 9 \times 114 = 13 \times 421 - 48 \times 114\]

\[1 = 13 \times 421 - 48 \times (535 - 421) = 61 \times 421 - 48 \times 535\]

\[1 = 61 \times (1491 - 2 \times 535) - 48 \times 535 = 61 \times 1491 - 170 \times 535\]

\[1 = 61 \times 1491 - 170 \times (2026 - 1491) = 231 \times 1491 - 170 \times 2026\]

Eight lines, and the last one is the identity: \(231 \times 1491 - 170 \times 2026 = 344{,}421 - 344{,}420 = 1\). Fig B04.2 compresses the whole climb into one view.

Two readings of the last line. As an equation over the whole numbers: \(231 \times 1491 - 170 \times 2026 = 1\). Reduced mod 1491: the term \(231 \times 1491\) vanishes, leaving \(-170 \times 2026 \equiv 1 \pmod{1491}\) — the class of \(-170\) is the inverse of the class of 2026. Dress it in census range \(0\) to \(1490\): \(-170 + 1491 = 1321\). And B03's brute-force witness falls out of the same line: \(2026 \times 1321 = 2026 \times (1491 - 170) = 2026 \times 1491 - 170 \times 2026 = (2026 - 231) \times 1491 + 1 = 1795 \times 1491 + 1\). The equation the scan found by testing classes — now with its reason attached.

(c) Linear congruences — solved by certificate

Bézout certificates turn congruence equations into arithmetic. Three cases, carried to the end.

Coprime coefficient: \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\). The dance on (13, 5): \(13 = 2 \times 5 + 3\), then \(5 = 1 \times 3 + 2\), then \(3 = 1 \times 2 + 1\), then \(2 = 2 \times 1 + 0\). Climb: \(1 = 3 - 2 = 3 - (5 - 3) = 2 \times 3 - 5 = 2 \times (13 - 2 \times 5) - 5 = 2 \times 13 - 5 \times 5\). So \(-5 \times 5 \equiv 1 \pmod{13}\): the class of 5 has inverse \(-5\), dressed as 8. Multiply the congruence through by 8: \(x \equiv 8 \times 3 = 24 \equiv 11 \pmod{13}\). One class, and it checks: \(5 \times 11 = 55 = 4 \times 13 + 3\). B02's Exercise 3 solved this same congruence by scanning thirteen candidates; the certificate route never scanned at all.

Shared factor: \(6x \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\). Here \(\gcd(6, 15) = 3\), and 3 divides 9 — so combinations of 6 and 15 can reach 9 at all (Box B04.A: they reach exactly the multiples of 3). Write the congruence as \(3 \times 2x \equiv 3 \times 3 \pmod{15}\): a shared factor sits on both sides, and the honest move divides it out including from the modulus — Corollary 3 below is the full law; here it gives \(2x \equiv 3 \pmod 5\). The class of 2 mod 5 has inverse 3, by the one-line certificate \(2 \times 3 = 6 \equiv 1 \pmod 5\). So \(x \equiv 3 \times 3 = 9 \equiv 4 \pmod 5\): one class mod 5, which is exactly three classes mod 15 — \(\gcd(6, 15) = 3\) of them: \(x \in \{4, 9, 14\}\). Check all three: \(6 \times 4 = 24 = 15 + 9\); \(6 \times 9 = 54 = 45 + 9\); \(6 \times 14 = 84 = 75 + 9\).

No solution: \(4x \equiv 7 \pmod{10}\). \(\gcd(4, 10) = 2\), and 2 does not divide 7. Box B04.A: the combinations of 4 and 10 are exactly the even numbers — and any class congruent to 7 mod 10 is odd. No solution exists, and no pencil is needed to know it; a scan of all ten classes agrees: none. The gcd settles solvability before any search begins.

4 · Why it is true

Three claims carry this page — the identity (Box B04), the reachable set (Box B04.A), and the climb's correctness — and all three get complete proofs. The onramp's debts then fall out as corollaries, each with its proof too.

The identity, proved by descent. Let \(S\) be the set of positive combinations: \(S = \{\, ax + by > 0 \;:\; x, y \text{ whole} \,\}\), for \(a, b\) not both zero. \(S\) is nonempty — \(a \times a + b \times b\) is a combination (a stack of \(a\) copies of \(a\) plus a stack of \(b\) copies of \(b\)), and it is positive because \(a\) and \(b\) are not both zero. By B01's well-ordering principle, \(S\) has a smallest member; call it \(d = ax_0 + by_0\). Claim: \(d\) divides \(a\). Divide: \(a = qd + r\) with \(0 \le r < d\) (B01's division algorithm). Then

\[r \;=\; a - qd \;=\; a(1 - qx_0) + b(-qy_0),\]

a combination of \(a\) and \(b\), nonnegative and strictly smaller than \(d\). If \(r\) were positive, it would be a member of \(S\) smaller than the smallest member of \(S\) — impossible. So \(r = 0\): \(d \mid a\). The same division applied to \(b\) gives \(d \mid b\). So \(d\) is a common divisor. Finally, let \(c\) be any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\): then \(c\) divides every stack of \(a\) and every stack of \(b\), so \(c\) divides every combination — in particular \(c \mid d\). A positive number that every common divisor divides, and that is itself a common divisor, is the greatest common divisor: \(d = \gcd(a, b)\). And \(d = ax_0 + by_0\) is the identity. Complete.

Notice what powered the proof: a smallest member, a would-be smaller trespasser, and the impossibility of both. That is descent — the move P13's page builds into a whole method. This proof is its first full appearance on the spine; B03's termination argument (leftovers strictly shrink, and well-ordering forbids an endless fall) ran on the same engine, in the small.

The climb, proved. Every leftover in the dance is a combination of \(a\) and \(b\). The first two members trivially are: \(a = 1 \times a + 0 \times b\) and \(b = 0 \times a + 1 \times b\). And each new leftover is \(r = s - q \times t\), where \(s\) and \(t\) are the two members above it — a difference of two combinations with a quotient multiple, which is again a combination, since combinations add, subtract and scale. So the combination property carries down the chain — and the chain is finite (B03's termination), so the propagation is a finite list of identical steps, whose formal name is induction, P66 — named and deferred, exactly as B02 and B03 deferred it. The last nonzero leftover is the gcd (B03), so the gcd arrives carrying a certificate: Box B04, made mechanical. Section 3's climbs were this proof run on real numbers.

Box B04.A, proved. Both directions were the box's own two sentences. The gcd divides \(a\) and \(b\), so it divides every stack and anti-stack, so it divides every combination: every combination is a multiple of the gcd. And Box B04 exhibits the gcd as a combination, so scaling that one certificate by \(k\) reaches every multiple of the gcd. Together: the reachable set is exactly the multiples of the gcd — nothing more, nothing fewer.

Corollary 1 — B02's conjecture, hard half: coprime classes are invertible. Suppose \(\gcd(a, n) = 1\). By the identity, \(1 = ax + ny\) for some whole \(x, y\). Reduce mod \(n\): the \(ny\) term vanishes, leaving \(ax \equiv 1 \pmod n\) — the class of \(x\) is the inverse of the class of \(a\). Together with B03's easy half (invertible classes are coprime), B02's Exercise 4 census is now a theorem: the invertible classes mod \(n\) are exactly the classes coprime to \(n\). And the (2026, 1491) story tells itself once more: the certificate \(1 = -170 \times 2026 + 231 \times 1491\) was the inverse; \(-170 + 1491 = 1321\) only dressed it in census range. Check the theorem over full censuses — mod 7, 10, 12, 13 and 31: every coprime class has a certificate inverse, it agrees with the scan's inverse every time, and no other class has an inverse at all. The pattern B02 could only observe is now a consequence, both halves proved.

Corollary 2 — Euclid's lemma, and P05's debt paid. If \(n \mid ab\) and \(\gcd(n, a) = 1\), then \(n \mid b\). Proof: by the identity, \(1 = nx + ay\); multiply through by \(b\): \(b = nbx + aby\). The first term is a multiple of \(n\); the second is a multiple of \(ab\), and \(n\) divides \(ab\); their sum is a multiple of \(n\). So \(n \mid b\). Complete — three lines, and the oldest lemma about divisibility is a corollary of reachability. When \(n\) is prime it reads: \(p \mid ab\) forces \(p \mid a\) or \(p \mid b\) (if \(p\) misses \(a\), then \(\gcd(p, a) = 1\), and the lemma forces \(p \mid b\)). That prime case opens the road to unique factorization — which this page deliberately does not claim; the scope box below holds the line.

With it, the debt P05's page named when it reduced fractions: lowest terms are unique. Suppose \(\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}\), both fractions reduced, so \(\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(c, d) = 1\). Cross-multiply: \(ad = bc\). Then \(b \mid ad\) with \(\gcd(a, b) = 1\), so Euclid's lemma gives \(b \mid d\); symmetrically \(d \mid bc\) with \(\gcd(c, d) = 1\) gives \(d \mid b\). Two positive numbers that divide each other are equal — \(b = d\) — and then \(ad = bc\) becomes \(ad = dc\), so \(a = c\). The two reduced forms are the same fraction. A lowest-terms fraction has no rival: the uniqueness half of canonical form, which P05's page honestly could not prove, proved here in four lines.

Corollary 3 — B02's scope-box promise: dividing congruences, precisely. For \(c\) a positive whole number,

\[ac \equiv bc \pmod n \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; a \equiv b \pmod{n / g}, \qquad g = \gcd(c, n).\]

Proof: \(ac \equiv bc\) means \(n \mid c(a - b)\). Write \(c = gc'\) and \(n = gn'\) with \(\gcd(c', n') = 1\); then \(n' \mid c'(a - b)\), and Euclid's lemma — applied to \(n'\) dividing a product while coprime to one factor — gives \(n' \mid (a - b)\), which is \(a \equiv b \pmod{n'}\) with \(n' = n/g\). Every step reverses, so the equivalence is exact. B02's promise was the special case: cancel freely exactly when the canceled factor shares nothing with the modulus (\(g = 1\), nothing shrinks).

B02's counterexample, reconciled at last: \(2 \times 3 \equiv 2 \times 8 \pmod{10}\) (both products land on the class of 6), yet \(3 \not\equiv 8 \pmod{10}\) — so cancellation seemed to fail. The condition says why: \(g = \gcd(2, 10) = 2\), so the honest conclusion is \(3 \equiv 8 \pmod 5\) — and that is true; both are 3 mod 5. The modulus shrank with the cancellation; nothing failed. And when \(g = 1\) nothing shrinks: \(5 \times 2 \equiv 5 \times 9 \pmod 7\) (both land on the class of 3) cancels freely to \(2 \equiv 9 \pmod 7\) — sound, because 5 and 7 share nothing.

Corollary 4 — linear congruences, counted. \(ax \equiv c \pmod n\) is solvable exactly when \(g = \gcd(a, n)\) divides \(c\); and then it has exactly \(g\) classes of solutions mod \(n\). Solvability is Box B04.A read aloud: solving means some combination \(ax - nk = c\), and the combinations of \(a\) and \(n\) are exactly the multiples of \(g\). Counting: divide through by \(g\) — Corollary 3 licenses it, sides and modulus both — to get \(a'x \equiv c' \pmod{n'}\) with \(\gcd(a', n') = 1\); Corollary 1 hands the certificate inverse of \(a'\), so there is exactly one solution class mod \(n'\), and each class mod \(n'\) splits into exactly \(g\) classes mod \(n\). Section 3(c) was this corollary running live: \(5x \equiv 3 \pmod{13}\) — \(g = 1\), one class, \(\{11\}\); \(6x \equiv 9 \pmod{15}\) — \(g = 3\) divides 9, three classes, \(\{4, 9, 14\}\); \(4x \equiv 7 \pmod{10}\) — \(g = 2\) misses 7, none. A census over every coefficient \(a\) and target \(c\) for every \(n\) from 5 to 16 agrees exactly with the count — \(g\) classes when \(g \mid c\), zero when not, no exceptions anywhere in the grid.

Scope, honestly. Three boundaries. (i) This page handles pairs. For three numbers, apply the identity twice — reach \(\gcd(a, b)\), then combine with \(c\), using the associativity B03's Exercise 3 proved — done on demand, not built here. (ii) Unique factorization — every whole number above 1 factors into primes in exactly one way — is not claimed on this page. Corollary 2's prime case opens the road, but the assembly needs induction, P66's page; default until then: the road is open, the theorem is not. (iii) Exercise 4's stamp conjecture — largest unmakeable \(ab - a - b\) for coprime \(a, b\) — is verified on two cases and conjectured beyond them; its general proof is genuinely harder than anything here, and stays open. What this page closes, it closes completely; what it defers, it names.

5 · Practice

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◐ open exploration. Hints and worked answers are collapsible. No ◆ exercise here — there is no pattern to diagnose on a concept page yet.

Exercise 1 ●○○

B03's Exercise 1 reduced \(\frac{322}{1386}\) to \(\frac{23}{99}\) by the dance. Run the climb on the same pair (1386, 322): find whole numbers \(x, y\) with \(1386x + 322y = 14\). Then divide the identity by 14 and say what the result certifies about the reduced fraction.

Hint

The dance is four divisions; the climb is three substitutions. Divide the final identity through by the gcd — every coefficient stays whole.

Worked answer

Dance: \(1386 = 4 \times 322 + 98\), \(322 = 3 \times 98 + 28\), \(98 = 3 \times 28 + 14\), \(28 = 2 \times 14 + 0\). Climb: \(14 = 98 - 3 \times 28\); then \(28 = 322 - 3 \times 98\) gives \(14 = 98 - 3 \times (322 - 3 \times 98) = 10 \times 98 - 3 \times 322\); then \(98 = 1386 - 4 \times 322\) gives \(14 = 10 \times 1386 - 43 \times 322\). Check: \(13{,}860 - 13{,}846 = 14\). Divided by 14: \(10 \times 99 - 43 \times 23 = 1\) — check: \(990 - 989\) — a certificate that \(\gcd(99, 23) = 1\). B03's reduced fraction was in lowest terms "as far as the dance could see"; this is the same fact with its reason attached: the parts of a fully reduced fraction are genuinely coprime, and the certificate proves it without factoring 99 or 23.

Exercise 2 ●●○

(a) Run dance and climb on (31, 7) to find the inverse of the class of 7 mod 31. (b) Solve \(7x \equiv 12 \pmod{31}\). (c) Reconcile with B02's Exercise 4 census: mod 10, find 3's inverse by a two-division dance, and say why the climb beats the scan as moduli grow.

Hint

(a) The dance on (31, 7) is two divisions. (b) Multiply both sides by the inverse. (c) The scan tests classes one at a time; the climb's length grows with the number of digits, not the size — B03's halving engine.

Worked answer

(a) \(31 = 4 \times 7 + 3\), then \(7 = 2 \times 3 + 1\), then \(3 = 3 \times 1 + 0\). Climb: \(1 = 7 - 2 \times 3 = 7 - 2 \times (31 - 4 \times 7) = 9 \times 7 - 2 \times 31\) — check: \(63 - 62\). So the class of 7 is invertible mod 31, with inverse the class of 9 (already in census range). (b) Multiply through by 9: \(x \equiv 9 \times 12 = 108 \equiv 15 \pmod{31}\), since \(108 = 3 \times 31 + 15\). Check: \(7 \times 15 = 105 = 3 \times 31 + 12\). One class, as Corollary 4 counts: \(g = \gcd(7, 31) = 1\). (c) Mod 10: \(10 = 3 \times 3 + 1\), so \(1 = 10 - 3 \times 3\): the class of 3 has inverse \(-3\), dressed as 7 — matching the census row \(3 \times 7 = 21 \equiv 1\). The scan tests up to ten classes; the climb here was one division. For a 100-digit modulus the scan is hopeless, while the climb stays a few hundred divisions — the certificate route scales, the search route does not.

Exercise 3 ●●●

For positive whole \(a, b\): if \((x_0, y_0)\) is one solution of \(ax + by = c\), prove that every solution has the form \(x = x_0 + (b/g)t\), \(y = y_0 - (a/g)t\) for a whole number \(t\), where \(g = \gcd(a, b)\) — and that every pair of that form is a solution. Then apply: one solution of \(4x + 6y = 2\) is \((-1, 1)\); write the full family and check four members, including two with negative entries.

Hint

For one direction, add the family's extra terms and watch them cancel — both \(a \cdot (b/g)\) and \(b \cdot (a/g)\) equal \(ab/g\). For the other, subtract two solutions and apply Euclid's lemma (Corollary 2) after dividing out \(g\); remember \(\gcd(a/g, b/g) = 1\).

Worked answer

Every pair of the form works: \(a\left(x_0 + \frac{b}{g}t\right) + b\left(y_0 - \frac{a}{g}t\right) = ax_0 + by_0 + \frac{ab}{g}t - \frac{ab}{g}t = c\). The added terms cancel because both \(a \cdot \frac{b}{g}\) and \(b \cdot \frac{a}{g}\) equal \(\frac{ab}{g}\). Every solution has the form: let \((x_1, y_1)\) be any other solution and subtract the equations: \(a(x_0 - x_1) = -b(y_0 - y_1)\). Write \(a = ga'\), \(b = gb'\); then \(a'(x_0 - x_1) = -b'(y_0 - y_1)\), and \(\gcd(a', b') = 1\) — if some \(d > 1\) divided both \(a'\) and \(b'\), then \(gd\) would divide both \(a\) and \(b\), past the gcd \(g\), impossible. So \(b' \mid a'(x_0 - x_1)\) with \(\gcd(a', b') = 1\), and Euclid's lemma — this page's own Corollary 2 — gives \(b' \mid (x_0 - x_1)\): write \(x_0 - x_1 = b't\). Substituting back, \(a'b't = -b'(y_0 - y_1)\), so \(y_0 - y_1 = -a't\). Both differences match the family's form, with the same \(t\). Complete. Application: \(g = \gcd(4, 6) = 2\), base \((-1, 1)\) checks \((-4) + 6 = 2\), so the family is \(x = -1 + 3t\), \(y = 1 - 2t\). Members: \(t = 0\): \((-1, 1)\); \(t = 1\): \((2, -1)\), with \(8 - 6 = 2\); \(t = 2\): \((5, -3)\), with \(20 - 18 = 2\); \(t = -1\): \((-4, 3)\), with \(-16 + 18 = 2\). Negative entries are legal — an anti-stack is a stack reversed. A window check over all \(|x|, |y| \le 30\) finds 20 solutions; the family captures all 20, and nothing else in the window solves the equation.

Exercise 4 ◐

A post office sells stamps of ₹4 and ₹7 only, and requires exact postage. An amount is makeable if some combination \(4x + 7y\) hits it exactly. (a) Scan the amounts ₹1 to ₹25 and sort them into makeable and never-makeable. (b) Prove that every amount from ₹18 on is makeable. (c) The (4, 7) data suggests a conjecture: for stamp values \(a\) and \(b\) sharing no factor, the largest never-makeable amount is \(ab - a - b\). Test it on (3, 5) as well, then state honestly what has been proved and what has not.

What you should find

(a) Makeable up to 25: \(4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25\). Never-makeable: \(1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, 10, 13, 17\). (b) ₹18 \(= 4 + 2 \times 7\), ₹19 \(= 3 \times 4 + 7\), ₹20 \(= 5 \times 4\), ₹21 \(= 3 \times 7\) — and any makeable amount stays makeable when one more ₹4 stamp joins its combination. So the block 18, 19, 20, 21 covers every amount above it: from ₹22 on, keep adding ₹4 stamps to a base in the block. That is a forced-forever block — the same shape B03 used for its worst-case census, and the formal name for such repetition is induction (P66, named and deferred). (c) For (4, 7): \(4 \times 7 - 4 - 7 = 17\), the largest never-makeable, on the nose. For (3, 5): never-makeable \(1, 2, 4, 7\); largest 7; and \(3 \times 5 - 3 - 5 = 7\) — again on the nose, with every amount from ₹8 on makeable. Two confirmations, zero refutations: a conjecture standing on evidence. What has not been proved: the general claim for arbitrary stamp values sharing no factor. It is a real theorem with a real proof, harder than anything on this page — the scope box said so — and until it is proved somewhere, \(ab - a - b\) is a pattern, not a fact.
