The Patterns Atlas · P55 · the twelfth move of tier 5

Current mass minimization

The cheapest object a problem allows is often too rough to exist among the shapes you can draw: competitors shatter, halve, and slide toward the boundary as they cheapen, and the floor they chase may be nothing they can land on. The move: widen the world on purpose — admit currents, bookkeeping objects that add, cancel, and take limits — so a cheapening sequence acquires a limit object; bound the mass before you chase it, so the limit is not zero; then prove how much geometry the winner wins back. Existence stops being a hope and becomes a certificate — and the certificate names exactly where it does not reach.

New word: current. Start where school already is. Walk a polygonal path: it has a direction, and a length; walk an edge twice and the length counts twice. A chain is that observation promoted to algebra — a formal sum \(T = \sum_i m_i \sigma_i\) of oriented pieces with integer multiplicities: a piece can run backwards (\(m_i = -1\)), repeat (\(m_i = 2\)), or cancel against its reverse (\(1 - 1 = 0\)). Chains add like sums; the boundary \(\partial \sigma\) of an oriented piece is its oriented edge-list, so the boundary of a filled region is the region's rim, walked with orientation; boundaries of boundaries vanish, \(\partial\partial = 0\), because every rim-edge is entered once and left once. The mass \(M(T)\) is the price tag: total length counted with multiplicity — the thing a minimizer must be small in. The flat norm \(F(T)\) forgives what can be explained as a fill: if \(T = P + \partial R\) then \(F(T) \le M(P) + M(R)\), so mass that is merely the rim of a cheap filler does not count against you — two chains whose difference is the boundary of a sliver are flat-close even when their masses are far apart. A current is de Rham's word for a chain taught to take limits: an oriented bookkeeping object that pairs with smooth forms, \(\langle T, \omega \rangle\), pricing each form by feeding it the chain — and a form's comass \(\|\omega\|\) is the most it can charge per unit mass, so the pairing buys inequalities: \(M(T) \ge \langle T, \omega\rangle\) whenever \(\|\omega\| \le 1\). Nothing on this page leaves the chain level — polygons, finite sums, and limits of both; the limits are the whole point, and the widening is exactly the permission to keep what the sequences converge to. P29 taught the one-way ledger; this page builds the world the ledger's limits must live in.

1 · Before we begin

Tier 5 keeps one thing and discards another, honestly. P43 kept the descent and repaired its tears: let the object evolve, cut where the flow breaks, and read the verdict off the pieces. This page refuses the journey and asks for the destination outright: among all competitors, which one is cheapest — and does it exist at all? The discard is symmetry with the process: no evolution, no time, no tears to schedule. What replaces them is a world built on purpose.

What you need from earlier pages is small and named. From P29: the one-way ledger — a quantity the dynamics push one way — read here in a new role: the a priori bound, the number that keeps a cheapening sequence from leaking away to nothing. From P41: the habit of hunting for a critical object without a closed form — but P41's hunt still ran inside a world of honest functions, where the shapes were never in question. From P43: the recognition that the descent's destination — the round shape the flow was heading for — is itself the object worth seeking directly; this page seeks it, and the seeking is the whole method.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Every minimization met so far found its answer among objects you could already see: the circle, the tree, the settled state. But the honest general question — minimize over all competitors, no smoothness assumed — admits competitors that shatter into pieces, double back on themselves, and cheapen by degenerating. The infimum they chase may be approached by ever-cheaper objects whose limit, in the world of drawable shapes, is nothing. So the minimum might not exist — and a minimization whose minimum does not exist is a funnel, not a floor. The direct method resolves this in three obligations, and the page's whole architecture is those obligations: widen the world so limits exist, bound the mass so limits are not zero, and win back the geometry so the limit is still an object you can almost see. Each obligation has a jam-mode, each jam-mode is run below as an exhibit, and the singular minimizer — the geometry that does not fully come back — is shown honestly, as a sketch with its missing parts named.

2 · Meet the problem

The staircase that cheapens by collapsing. Connect \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\) two ways: the diagonal, one segment of mass \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.414214\); and the staircase of \(N\) steps — horizontal, vertical, horizontal, vertical — of mass exactly \(2\), because \(N\) horizontals and \(N\) verticals each of length \(1/N\) sum to \(2\) no matter the \(N\). Run the family: \(N = 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\); the mass reads \(2\), \(2\), \(2\), \(2\), \(2\). The staircase never gets cheaper — and yet it converges: the triangular sliver between the staircase and the diagonal has total area \(N \cdot \frac{1}{2N^2} = \frac{1}{2N}\), a fill of \(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{16}, \tfrac{1}{32}, \tfrac{1}{64}\) — halving with each doubling, gone at the limit. As chains, the difference \(T - T'\) between staircase and diagonal is exactly \(\partial\rho\), the boundary of the sliver-region \(\rho\) — an integer-coefficient identity, checked edge by edge below — so the flat norm obeys \(F(T - T') \le M(\rho) = \frac{1}{2N}\), which is \(\frac{1}{16} = 0.0625\) at \(N = 8\) and zero at the limit. The staircases flat-converge to the diagonal (Fig P55.1).

And the limit is cheaper than every approximant: the diagonal's mass \(\sqrt{2}\) sits a full \(2 - \sqrt{2} \approx 0.585786\) below every staircase's \(2\), forever. That is not a paradox; it is the load-bearing fact of the entire subject, and it has a name: mass is lower semicontinuous under flat convergence — the limit's mass is at most the liminf of the masses, \(2 \ge \sqrt{2}\), and it may drop. A world that closes under limits must expect its price tags to behave this way: if limits could only inherit their approximants' masses, no cheapening sequence could ever settle. The drop is the feature that lets the minimum exist at all; the price of the feature is that the winner may be rougher than anything in the sequence that found it.

The surface that will not stay a surface. Now the problem that made the widening necessary. Among all surfaces spanning a given boundary wire, find the least area — posed by Lagrange in 1760, named for Plateau's soap-film experiments of 1873, and solved in the 1930s by Douglas and Radó under topological restrictions: the competing surfaces were assumed to be discs of a fixed shape-of-connection (Douglas's solution earned the first Fields Medal). Remove the restrictions and let the competitors be honest — any surface, any topology, any number of pieces — and the direct question jams. Competitors can split into two pieces connected by a thinner and thinner neck, cheapening toward a limit that is not a surface but a pair of surfaces; competitors can grow infinitely many ripples of vanishing area; the infimum is approached, but in the world of smooth single surfaces, nothing attains it. The historical resolution is the widened world: De Giorgi's sets of finite perimeter, Reifenberg's compactness, and — the move this page names — Federer and Fleming's integral currents (1960), in which the orientable Plateau problem was solved without topological restrictions, by proving the limit object exists in the bookkeeping world and then winning back its geometry. The staircase in miniature is that whole story: the family converges, the limit is cheaper, and only a world that admits the limit as an object can say so.

3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P55 — Current mass minimization. When the cheapest object a problem allows is too rough to exist among the shapes you can draw — competitors shatter, halve, and degenerate as they cheapen — do not descend toward the floor (P43) and do not deform over the landscape (P41): widen the world on purpose. Embed the competitors in a bookkeeping world — currents, chains with limits, objects that add, cancel, and take limits — so that a cheapening sequence acquires a limit object: closure under limits is existence, bought structurally. Then bound the mass a priori: a calibration form, a one-way book, a hard inequality — named before the minimization runs, because a sequence with no bound leaks to zero and its limit is nothing. Then win the geometry back: rectifiability says the winner is built from countably many honest pieces with tangent directions, regularity says it is smooth outside a small singular set — and the singular set must be discussed honestly, because sometimes it is nonempty. The floor sought directly is the limit the widening caught.

Or, in words: widen the world, bound the mass, win the geometry back. The widening is what makes the minimum exist; the bound is what makes it matter; the winning-back is what makes it geometry. Every one of the three is an obligation with a proof duty, and the exhibits below are the three ways the move fails when an obligation is skipped.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the world must close. The widened world has to be closed under the limits that cheapening sequences perform — that is the entire point of widening it. Jam-mode: the naive world of smooth shapes fails this exactly where it matters: §2's staircases converge to the diagonal, a limit that no further staircase is; the unrestricted Plateau competitors converge to objects that are not surfaces. Compactness is the formal name of the key: bounded mass and bounded support must force a convergent subsequence, or the infimum is chased by ghosts.

Key 2 — the mass must be bounded before it is minimized. The a priori bound must be named with its source: a calibration (a form of comass at most 1 that the competitors all price), a one-way book in the sense of P29, or a hard inequality proved before the run. Jam-mode: §4's third encounter — winding-two chains near the puncture have no positive floor: masses \(16, 1.6, 0.16, 0.016\), chasing zero, attaining nothing; the infimum exists, the minimum does not, and the missing bound is the whole story.

Key 3 — the geometry comes back only partially. The winner is rectifiable and regular — smooth outside a singular set that is small in a precise sense — and the singular set may be genuinely nonempty. Jam-mode: §5's third exhibit, the Simons cone: an area-minimizing current with a real singularity at its vertex, in eight dimensions, named as a sketch with its missing parts named. Regularity is a theorem about where the geometry returns, never a promise of everywhere; quoting it as everywhere is the move's most common forgery.

Box P55.A — the direct method, in three obligations. Every honest application of the move is earned the same three-step way, and each step carries its own proof duty:

1 · Widen into currents; close the world. Replace "surface" or "curve" by the chain-level bookkeeping object — oriented pieces, integer multiplicities, finite mass, finite support — and prove the closure: a sequence of uniformly bounded mass and support has a flat-convergent subsequence, and mass is lower semicontinuous along it, \(M(\lim T_j) \le \liminf M(T_j)\). Duty: exhibit the convergence on your own family — §2's staircase does exactly this, \(2 \ge \sqrt{2}\) — because a claimed closure that no member of the family demonstrates is a definition in costume. The limit's mass may drop below every approximant's; that is the mechanism, not a leak.

2 · Bound the mass; name the bound's source. Before minimizing, produce the number the mass cannot sink below — from a calibration form \(\omega\) with comass \(\|\omega\| \le 1\), whose pairing \(M(T) \ge \langle T, \omega \rangle\) prices every competitor at once; or from a one-way book; or from a combinatorial certificate. Duty: say where the bound lives — the calibration's domain is part of the claim. The form \(d\theta\) has comass \(1/r\), which is at most 1 only outside the unit disk; inside, the pentagon of §5's Exhibit A lives happily at \(1/r = \sqrt{5} - 1 > 1\), and the bound honestly does not reach it.

3 · Win the geometry back; say where it does not return. The limit object is a priori only bookkeeping; rectifiability wins back pieces with tangents, and regularity wins back smoothness — outside a singular set whose size and structure must be stated. Duty: name the singular set, even when it is empty; §5's Exhibit C names the cone's vertex, and the naming is the difference between an honest page and a poster.

Box P55.B — where the move appears. The Plateau problem: the founding instance — Federer and Fleming's integral currents (1960) solved the orientable case without topological restrictions, by widening exactly where Douglas and Radó had to restrict. The isoperimetric inequality: the circle's optimality certified by the calibration \(d\theta\), no enumeration of shapes required — §4's first encounter runs it at desk size. Minimal cones and Bernstein's problem: the singular minimizers that bound what regularity can promise — Simons (1968), Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti (1969), sketched in §4. Reifenberg's epiperimetric route: a rival widening — compact sets with finite measure — solving the problem for boundaries homeomorphic to spheres. Almgren's regularity machine: the winning-back at research scale, multi-valued functions and all. The discretist's version: §4's grid — Steiner-style connection problems where the chains are mod-2 edge sets, the mass is the edge count, and the same three obligations (close the world, bound by stars, win back the tree) run on 4096 subsets of a lattice.

4 · Three encounters

Three workings, one per face of Box P55.B: the plane, where a calibration prices every competitor at once; the grid, where the whole move runs discretely, censused by machine; and the punctured plane, where the bound's domain decides whether a minimizer exists at all. Every number below is machine work — exact integer witnesses where integers suffice, interval arithmetic where irrationals enter, honest tolerance disclosures where floats stand in — and each is re-derivable by hand where a line of algebra suffices.

Encounter 1 (the plane) — Dido, certified

The question. Among closed curves enclosing a fixed area, the circle has the least perimeter — the isoperimetric inequality, queen of calibration arguments. The direct method does not enumerate shapes: it prices them all at once. Circumscribe a regular \(n\)-gon around the unit circle: perimeter \(L(n) = 2n\tan(\pi/n)\), and \(L(n) \ge 2\pi\) is exactly the inequality \(\tan x \ge x\) at \(x = \pi/n\). Inscribe one: area \(A(n) = \frac{n}{2}\sin(2\pi/n)\), perimeter \(2n\sin(\pi/n)\), and the isoperimetric ratio works out to the same inequality — \(\frac{L(n)^2}{4\pi A(n)} = \frac{\tan(\pi/n)}{\pi/n} \ge 1\) — one thread, two classical facts.

The census, squeezed. Run both families by machine: circumscribed perimeters \(L(3) = 10.392305\), \(L(4) = 8\), \(L(6) = 6.928203\), \(L(12) = 6.430781\), \(L(24) = 6.319320\), \(L(48) = 6.292172\), closing down on \(2\pi = 6.283185\) from above; inscribed ratios \(1.653987, 1.273240, 1.102658, 1.023491, 1.005751, 1.001430\) closing on \(1\) from above. And the squeeze needs no floating \(\pi\): Archimedes' own doubling, run by machine on exact dyadic intervals — the circumscribed \(96\)-gon's half-perimeter brackets \(\pi\) in \([3.1427078,\; 3.1427193]\), strictly below \(22/7 = 3.1428571\) — certifies \(\pi < 22/7\), and the witnesses chain in integers: \(27 \ge \pi^2\) for the triangle (\(484 < 1323\)); \(4 \ge \pi\) for the square (\(28 \ge 22\)); \(108 \ge \pi^2\) for the hexagon (\(484 < 5292\)); and for the \(12\)-gon, \(\sqrt{3} \le 73/42\) (\(5329 \ge 5292\)), so \(12(2 - \sqrt{3}) \ge 22/7 > \pi\) (\(924 = 924\)).

The gem. The inscribed \(12\)-gon in the unit circle has area exactly \(3\) — six copies of \(\sin(\pi/6) = 1/2\) — and its perimeter obeys \(L^2 = 144(2 - \sqrt{3}) \ge 264/7 > 12\pi = 4\pi A\), where both bounds are the same two integers as before: \(144(2 - 73/42) = 264/7\) because \(1584 \cdot 7 = 11088 = 264 \cdot 42\), and \(12\pi < 12 \cdot 22/7 = 264/7\). The circumscribed bound and the inscribed bound meet at \(264/7\) — two inequalities, one witness pair, no decimals anywhere in the proof.

The calibration. Why does the circle win, not merely tie a limit? Pair every competitor with the form \(d\theta\): its comass is \(\|d\theta\| = 1/r\), at most \(1\) everywhere outside the unit disk, so any closed curve winding once around the origin and supported there obeys \(M \ge \langle T, d\theta\rangle = 2\pi\) — a floor priced before any competitor is named. The unit circle pays exactly \(2\pi\): floor met, minimizer certified, no enumeration and no limit argument. That is the direct method's signature: the bound is a form, the pairing is one line, and the winner is whoever meets the bound. §5's Exhibit A shows what happens to a competitor that lives where the calibration's comass exceeds \(1\).

Moves used: P17 the form \(d\theta\), the dual object that prices every competitor at once — the pairing is the whole proof · P15 the polygon censuses, squeezing \(\pi\) between inscribed and circumscribed · P25 the doubling run, the ratio table, and the integer witnesses, checked by machine.

Encounter 2 (the grid) — the census and the certificate

The question, discretely. The direct method runs at desk size on a \(3\times 3\) lattice: twelve unit edges, and two connection problems. First: connect the four side-midpoints — the midpoints of the square's four sides — with the fewest edges. Second: connect the four corners. The competitors are mod-2 edge sets — chains with coefficients in \(\{0,1\}\), the discrete face of the widened world — and the mass is the edge count.

The census. There are \(2^{12} = 4096\) edge subsets; the machine tries them all. Midpoints: the minimum is \(4\), and it is attained by exactly one subgraph — the cross, the four edges through the center. Corners: the minimum is \(6\), attained by six different trees, the plainest being the U — left column, right column, bottom row, edges \(\{e_0, e_1, e_4, e_5, e_6, e_7\}\).

The certificate. The machine's census is ground truth, but the direct method wants a reason, priced before the enumeration. Here it is: each of the four midpoint-terminals owns exactly three incident edges, and the four stars — the three edges around each terminal — are pairwise disjoint, partitioning all twelve edges. Any connected subgraph spanning the terminals must cross each star at least once (a terminal must connect to the rest), so it has at least \(4\) edges: four disjoint boxes, each crossed — the pigeonhole, promoted to a mass bound. And the certificate is tight at the midpoints: the cross crosses each star exactly once, meeting the floor. For the corners the same style of certificate — four disjoint corner-pairs of edges — proves only \(\ge 4\), and the truth is \(6\): the certificate is honest but not tight, and the gap is closed by the census, not by more certificate (Fig P55.3).

The lesson. A star certificate is a discrete calibration: it prices every competitor at once, from structure alone, and when it meets its floor it certifies the winner outright — the cross. When it does not meet its floor, the honest page says so: the corners keep a gap of two edges that only enumeration closes, and P25's detector honesty applies verbatim — the bound is valid, the bound is not the truth, and the difference between them is information about the problem, not embarrassment about the method.

Moves used: P25 the \(4096\)-subset censuses, both problems, counted exhaustively · P10 the four disjoint stars, four boxes each crossed at least once — the pigeonhole as a lower bound · P17 the star/cut certificate, the dual object that prices all competitors at once.

Encounter 3 (the punctured plane) — the bound's domain

The world. Puncture the plane at the origin and ask for the cheapest closed chain of winding two. Winding is a class invariant — an integer every competitor in the class carries, unchanged by any deformation that does not cross the puncture; it is P09's invariant wearing geometry's clothes. Two questions a minimizer must face: does a floor exist, and does anything attain it? The answers live on the two sides of the unit circle.

Outside the disk: the floor is met. The form \(d\theta\) has comass \(1/r \le 1\) on and outside the unit circle, so every winding-two chain supported there obeys \(M \ge \langle T, d\theta\rangle = 4\pi\) — the floor, priced by calibration before any competitor is named. And it is attained: the doubly-traversed unit circle, \(2\cdot[\text{circle}]\), carries mass exactly \(4\pi = 12.566371\) — floor met, minimizer certified, and the integer multiplicity is forced: no winding-one chain can carry the class, so the bookkeeping world's own arithmetic — multiplicity two — is what makes the winner a single honest object rather than a limit of pairs of loops.

Inside the disk: the floor is gone. Move the class inward and the calibration dies first: comass \(1/r\) exceeds \(1\) at every \(r < 1\). Then the floor itself dies: take the doubly-traversed \(\varepsilon\)-square at the puncture, vertices \((\pm\varepsilon, \pm\varepsilon)\). Its winding is exactly \(4\pi\) — eight right-angle turns — regardless of \(\varepsilon\); its mass is \(16\varepsilon\), eight segments of length \(2\varepsilon\) counted with multiplicity. Run \(\varepsilon = 1,\; 0.1,\; 0.01,\; 0.001\): masses \(16,\; 1.6,\; 0.16,\; 0.016\), winding steady at \(4\pi\), mass chasing zero. The infimum is \(0\), and nothing attains it: a chain of mass \(0\) is the zero chain, whose winding is \(0\). The class has no minimizer — a funnel, not a floor (Fig P55.2).

The two obligations, read together. Outside, the bound exists and the minimizer exists; inside, neither. The a priori mass bound of Box P55.A's second obligation is not decoration — it is the difference between a minimization and a hole. And compactness alone does not save it: the \(\varepsilon\)-squares flat-converge beautifully, to the zero chain; closure under limits catches the limit and hands you nothing. What the direct method requires, and what this encounter prices, is closure plus a bound: the first catches a limit object, the second makes it nonzero. Widen without bounding, and you have widened the world of the zero chain.

Moves used: P09 the winding number, the class invariant every deformation preserves · P21 the compactness key — bounded mass, bounded support, a convergent subsequence forced · P29 the one-way book, read here as the a priori mass bound that keeps the limit nonzero.

SKETCH — the research theorems behind this page, honestly labeled: the compactness-and-existence core is the Federer–Fleming theory of normal and integral currents (Annals of Mathematics 72, 1960), which this page runs at chain level without building — the slicing, deformation, and closure theorems that make "bounded mass forces a convergent subsequence" a theorem rather than an optimism are named, not proved. The regularity line — De Giorgi and Reifenberg's smoothness for perimeter-minimizers, Simons's extension to dimension six (1968), Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti's minimal cone in \(\mathbb{R}^8\) (1969), and Almgren's multi-valued machine (2000) — is sketched in §5's Exhibit C with its missing parts named: the epiperimetric inequality and the \(Q\)-valued machinery. The cone's minimality is quoted as consistent with the literature, not re-derived on this page. The parts this page does build, it builds by exact integer certificates: every number in §2, §4 and the exhibits below is machine-checked, and the machine record travels with the book.

5 · Exhibits: when the move fails

Every pattern earns its keep by failing. Three exhibits — a calibration that dies where the competitor lives, a class with no floor, and a minimizer whose geometry does not fully come back — with the mitigation, the price, and the default for each.

Exhibit A — the calibration that dies where the competitor lives. Claim: "the circle is the shortest closed curve winding once around the origin." False — and the falsification is a five-decimal machine check: the regular pentagon inscribed in the unit circle winds once around the origin (five exterior angles of \(72^\circ\), summing to \(360^\circ\) exactly) with perimeter \(10\sin(\pi/5) = 5.877853\), comfortably under the circle's \(2\pi = 6.283185\). The claim survives only with its regional fine print: among curves supported on and outside the unit circle — the domain where the calibration \(d\theta\) has comass at most \(1\). The pentagon's chords dip to \(r = \cos(\pi/5) = (1+\sqrt{5})/4 = 0.809017\), and there the comass reads \(1/r = \sqrt{5} - 1 = 1.236068 > 1\) — an exact identity, since \(4 = (\sqrt{5}+1)(\sqrt{5}-1)\), with the perimeter certified without floats too: \(100\sin^2(\pi/5) = \tfrac{25}{4}(10 - 2\sqrt{5}) < 36 < 4\pi^2\) because \(106 < 50\sqrt{5}\) (\(11236 < 12500\)) and \(\pi > 3\). The calibration does not merely weaken inside the disk; it stops being a bound at all.

Mitigation: state the calibration's domain as part of the claim — every time, in the same breath. A bound that lives only on a region must be quoted with its region, and the honest global statements need different machinery (the isoperimetric calibration of Encounter 1 prices the pentagon correctly; the winding form does not).

Price: the regional fine print is real mathematical content, not boilerplate — the difference between the two regimes of Fig P55.2 is the whole subject of Encounter 3, and mispricing it hands competitors a discount the books never offered.

Default: when a calibration proves a floor, ask immediately: where does its comass stay at most one? The answer is the theorem's address; a floor without an address is a claim wearing a proof's clothes.

Exhibit B — the class with no floor. Return to the winding-two chains near the puncture and watch the direct method fail its second obligation. The world is closed — the \(\varepsilon\)-squares flat-converge, to the zero chain, with textbook obedience. The class is honest — winding exactly \(4\pi\), invariant under everything that respects the puncture. And the infimum is \(0\), approached by masses \(16, 1.6, 0.16, 0.016, \ldots\), attained by nothing: the zero chain has winding \(0\), and every chain of positive mass can be shrunk further. No bound was named before the minimization ran, so no minimizer exists to be found — the widened world did its job, closed its limits, and handed back the zero chain. Compactness without a bound is a bucket with no bottom.

Mitigation: the a priori bound, first — Box P55.A's second obligation is not optional equipment. Restrict the class to mass \(\le B\) for a named \(B\), or move the class outside the unit disk where the calibration supplies \(4\pi\); either way the cheapening sequence now lives in a bounded world, its limit is nonzero, and the direct method proceeds.

Price: a bound excludes competitors — that is what a bound is for. The excluded cheap shrimps were never going to be the answer; they were going to be the reason no answer existed.

Default: before minimizing, ask: what number does the mass obey, and who says so? If the answer is "nothing", the honest next move is not a better competitor — it is a bound, or a smaller class, or a confession.

Exhibit C — the geometry that does not fully come back. The third obligation promises rectifiability and regularity: the winning current is built from countably many pieces with tangent directions, smooth outside a small singular set. The honest end of that sentence is outside a small singular set — and the singular set can be genuinely, structurally nonempty. The named sketch: the Simons cone, \(S = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^8 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 + x_4^2 = x_5^2 + x_6^2 + x_7^2 + x_8^2\}\), seven-dimensional, minimal — its mean curvature vanishes at every point except the vertex — and, by Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti (1969), genuinely area-minimizing: the widened world's own winner, with a singularity no smoothing can remove, because the singular object is the cheapest one there is. Simons (1968) had shown that for hypersurfaces of dimension at most \(6\), this cannot happen — regularity holds; dimension \(7\) is the threshold, and the cone crosses it. What regularity does win, stated honestly: a minimizing current is smooth outside a singular set that is small in a precise, dimension-graded sense; what it does not win: everywhere-smoothness, ever, in general. The parts of this story this page does not build — the epiperimetric inequality that drives the regularity proofs, Almgren's multi-valued machinery for the higher-codimension theory — are named here as the sketch's missing parts, per the tier's rules.

Mitigation: state the singular set's structure theorem as the deliverable, not the everywhere-smooth fantasy; and quote the dimension thresholds (regular for hypersurface minimizers up to dimension \(6\), possibly singular from \(7\)) as the honest boundary of the promise.

Price: in the dimensions where singularities live, the geometry that comes back is geometry almost everywhere — every regularity argument pays in exceptional sets, and the sets are load-bearing: the cone is the proof that they must be.

Default: when a textbook says "the minimizer is smooth", translate immediately: smooth outside its singular set — and say how big that set can be. The translation is not pedantry; it is the difference between the theorem and the poster.

Scope, honestly. What this page establishes, it establishes by exact machine certificates: the staircase decomposition \(T - T' = \partial\rho\) as integer edge-multisets, its fill \(\frac{1}{2N}\) exact for every \(N\); the Dido censuses — circumscribed perimeters and inscribed ratios for \(n = 3\) to \(48\) — with every claimed inequality re-derived in exact integers chained from \(\pi < 22/7\), itself verified by Archimedes' doubling on exact dyadic intervals (\(\pi\) bracketed in \([3.1427078, 3.1427193]\), \(22/7 = 3.1428571\)), and \(\pi > 3\) from the hexagon's exact chord; the inscribed \(12\)-gon's area \(3\) by two routes agreeing to \(10^{-12}\); the pentagon's perimeter, chord dip, and comass identity \(\sqrt{5}-1\), with the perimeter bound in exact integers (\(11236 < 12500\)); the winding tables — \(4\pi\) by turn-counting and by an independent atan2 sum agreeing to \(10^{-12}\), masses \(16\varepsilon\) summed segment by segment; and both grid censuses — all \(4096\) subsets tried, minimum \(4\) with the cross unique, minimum \(6\) with six minimizers, the star partition and tightness checks exhaustive. What this page does not establish: the Federer–Fleming compactness and existence theorems, the rectifiability theorem, and the regularity line De Giorgi \(\to\) Reifenberg \(\to\) Simons \(\to\) Bombieri–De Giorgi–Giusti \(\to\) Almgren — all named as sketches with their missing parts named; the Simons cone's minimality is quoted as consistent with the literature, not re-derived. Where the literature and this page ever disagree, the literature wins and this page's machine record gets corrected.

7 · Lineage

  • 1760 — Lagrange. Poses the least-area surface problem — the question that, two centuries later, needed a widened world to be asked without restrictions. The date is an anchor from the standard histories; nothing below leans on it.
  • 1873 — Plateau. Statique expérimentale et théorique des liquides: the soap-film experiments that gave the problem its name and every classroom its picture of a minimizing surface.
  • 1930s — Douglas and Radó. The first solutions, under topological restrictions — the competing surfaces assumed to be single discs of fixed connection. Douglas's work earned the first Fields Medal (1936); the restrictions stood for a generation.
  • 1954 — De Giorgi. Caccioppoli sets: perimeter as a measure, the reduced boundary, and smoothness theorems for minimizers of finite perimeter — the first widening that closes, in dimension \(2\).
  • 1960 — Federer and Fleming. "Normal and integral currents", Annals of Mathematics 72: the bookkeeping world built on purpose — integer-multiplicity objects with a boundary operator, a mass, and a compactness theorem — and with it the orientable Plateau problem solved without topological restrictions. The founding paper of geometric measure theory; the 1987 Steele Prize marks it.
  • 1960 — Reifenberg. A rival widening — compact sets with finite measure, no orientation asked — solving the universal Plateau problem for boundaries homeomorphic to spheres, by the epiperimetric route that reappears at the heart of the regularity theory.
  • 1968 — Simons. "Minimal varieties in riemannian manifolds": regularity extended to dimension \(6\), and the question of dimension \(7\) left open in exactly the way that made the next entry inevitable.
  • 1969 — Bombieri, De Giorgi, Giusti. "Minimal cones and the Bernstein problem", Inventiones mathematicae 7, 243–268: the minimal cone in \(\mathbb{R}^8\), area-minimizing and singular — the answer to Simons's question, and the honest end of everywhere-regularity.
  • 2000 — Almgren. Almgren's Big Regularity Paper (edited for the AMS by Taylor and Scheffer): multi-valued functions and the regularity of area-minimizing currents in arbitrary codimension — the winning-back obligation, discharged at research scale, a thousand pages long.
  • 2016 — Morgan. Geometric Measure Theory: A Beginner's Guide, fifth edition: the standard first route into everything above — and the book this page's ladder most resembles in spirit.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the certificates above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof. The 1969 Inventiones reference and the 2016 fifth edition were verified directly for this page's records; the remaining citations are consistent with the literature as this book understands it, and the verification levels travel with the page's own records.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the winding census. (a) Show that the doubly-traversed square with vertices \((\pm 1, \pm 1)\) has winding \(4\pi\): count the turns. (b) A single lap followed by its own reverse is a closed chain of mass twice the perimeter — what is its winding, and why can no winding argument bound its mass below? (c) Replace the square by the same walk scaled by \(\varepsilon\): show the winding is unchanged and the mass is \(16\varepsilon\), and read the three masses for \(\varepsilon = 1, 0.1, 0.01\).

Hint

(a) Eight right-angle turns, two laps. (b) Winding adds along a chain and the reverse lap contributes the negative of the forward one. (c) Scaling by \(\varepsilon\) multiplies lengths by \(\varepsilon\) and leaves angles alone.

Worked answer

(a) Each lap turns through four right angles — \(4 \times \tfrac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi\) — and the chain walks the lap twice: total turning \(4\pi\), the winding-two certificate. (b) The reverse lap turns the opposite way, so the total turning cancels: winding \(0\). A winding-based floor prices the class by \(\langle T, d\theta\rangle = (\text{winding}) \times 2\pi = 0\): the pairing is honest and the bound is zero — the book prices the class correctly, and the class genuinely has no positive floor from that form. This is Exhibit B's engine, run on the least convenient competitor. (c) The boundary walk is two laps of four edges each — eight edge-traversals, each of length \(2\varepsilon\): mass \(8 \times 2\varepsilon = 16\varepsilon\). So \(16, 1.6, 0.16\) — the collapse of Fig P55.2, with winding pinned at \(4\pi\) throughout.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the staircase bookkeeping. (a) At \(N = 4\), exhibit the fill \(\rho\) as four right triangles and verify \(T - T' = \partial\rho\) as oriented edge sums — the staircase's eight segments minus the diagonal's four. (b) Show \(F(T - T') \le M(\rho) = \frac{1}{2N}\), and read the flat distance at \(N = 8\). (c) The limit's mass \(\sqrt{2}\) is below every approximant's \(2\): explain why this drop is the feature that makes closures useful rather than a leak to be repaired.

Hint

(a) Use §2's edge-by-edge identity: each triangle contributes its bottom, its right side, and the reversed hypotenuse. (b) The flat norm forgives boundaries of cheap fills. (c) Ask what a limit object would be worth if its mass had to equal every approximant's.

Worked answer

(a) Four triangles with legs \(\tfrac{1}{4}\): each boundary is (bottom, right side, reversed hypotenuse), and summing over the four, the bottoms and sides assemble the staircase while the reversed hypotenuses assemble the negated diagonal — the machine checks the identity as an integer multiset at \(N = 8\), and at \(N = 4\) it is the same arithmetic with room to watch. (b) \(T - T' = \partial\rho\) with \(M(\rho) = 4 \times \tfrac{1}{2}(\tfrac{1}{4})^2 = \tfrac{1}{8}\), so \(F(T-T') \le \tfrac{1}{8}\); at \(N = 8\) the fill is \(\tfrac{1}{16} = 0.0625\), halving with every doubling — the family flat-converges to the diagonal. (c) Closure's value is that cheapening sequences land: if limits could only inherit their approximants' masses, the landing would be vacuous — no sequence could ever settle below its own start, and infima over rough competitors would stay unattained. Lower semicontinuity is the exact permission the staircase uses: the limit may be cheaper, never more expensive, and the minimum — when the world is wide enough and the bound is named — is what the permission was for.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the cross certificate. (a) Verify that the four midpoint-stars of the \(3 \times 3\) lattice — each terminal's three incident edges — are pairwise disjoint and cover all twelve edges. (b) Prove that any connected edge-set spanning the four midpoints crosses each star at least once, and conclude the \(\ge 4\) floor. (c) Verify that the cross crosses each star exactly once — the floor is tight — then run the same argument for the four corners and explain why it yields only \(4\) against the census's \(6\).

Hint

(a) Twelve edges, four terminals, three edges each — count. (b) A connected spanning subgraph cannot isolate a terminal. (c) The corner stars have two edges each, and eight edges leave four untouched — the certificate is honest, but not everything honest is tight.

Worked answer

(a) Each of the twelve edges touches at most one midpoint-terminal (the terminals are midpoints of sides, and their incident edges are distinct triples), so the stars partition the edge set — the machine verifies each edge appears in exactly one star. (b) If a star were uncrossed, its terminal would be disconnected from every other vertex — impossible in a connected subgraph spanning it. Four disjoint stars, each crossed at least once: at least four edges — the pigeonhole as a mass bound. (c) The cross is the center's four edges; each star contains exactly one of them, so the crossing count is \(1,1,1,1\) — floor met, winner certified. For the corners: each corner's star is its two incident edges; the four pairs are disjoint (eight edges), giving \(\ge 4\) — but the corner minimizers need six edges, because connecting four corners forces at least two junctions of degree three, and no star-count sees a junction. The certificate closes \(4\) of the \(6\); the census closes the rest — and the gap is the lesson of Encounter 2.

Exercise 4 ◆ Diagnose the claim (pattern-unknown). A colleague writes: "The circle is the shortest curve winding once around the origin: calibrate with \(d\theta\), whose comass is at most one, and the pairing \(2\pi\) is the floor — the circle meets it, so it wins." Diagnose. What is true, what is smuggled, which obligation of Box P55.A is violated, and what would you run to exhibit the price?

Hint

One five-decimal competitor falsifies the claim outright; then ask where the comass of \(d\theta\) actually stays at most one.

Worked answer

True: outside the unit disk the argument is exactly right — comass \(1/r \le 1\) there, every winding-one chain supported there owes \(2\pi\), and the unit circle meets the floor. Smuggled: the claim drops the domain. The comass of \(d\theta\) is \(1/r\), which exceeds \(1\) at every \(r < 1\) — so the floor is a regional bound, and Box P55.A's second obligation demands the domain be named as part of the claim. The falsifying run is Exhibit A's: the inscribed pentagon, winding exactly one (five exterior angles of \(72^\circ\)), perimeter \(10\sin(\pi/5) = 5.877853 < 2\pi = 6.283185\) — certified in integers by \(\tfrac{25}{4}(10-2\sqrt{5}) < 36 < 4\pi^2\), i.e. \(11236 < 12500\) with \(\pi > 3\). The pentagon's chords dip to \(r = \cos(\pi/5) = 0.809017\), where the comass reads \(1/r = \sqrt{5}-1 = 1.236068 > 1\) — the calibration cannot reach it, and the honest statement is: the circle wins among competitors supported on and outside the unit disk. The lesson is the same as Exhibit A's default: a floor without its address is a claim wearing a proof's clothes.

Exercise 5 ◐ The flat norm, explored (exploration). §2 priced the staircase-versus-diagonal difference two ways: \(M(T - T') = 2 + \sqrt{2} \approx 3.414214\), forever; and \(F(T - T') \le \frac{1}{2N}\), gone at the limit. (a) Compute both at \(N = 8\) and explain what each is measuring. (b) Exhibit the flat limit of the staircases and verify its mass by hand. (c) What does the flat norm forget that the mass remembers — and why must a world closed under flat convergence therefore expect lower semicontinuity, never continuity, of mass?

Hint

(a) One norm counts every edge; the other forgives what a cheap fill explains. (b) The diagonal: one segment, length \(\sqrt{2}\). (c) Ask what kind of sequence could converge flatly while its mass stays high — §2 is the example.

Worked answer

(a) \(M(T - T')\): the plain price of all sixteen staircase edges plus the four diagonal ones, multiplicity respected — \(2 + \sqrt{2}\), independent of \(N\), because no cancellation ever happens between the two chains. \(F(T - T')\): the bookkeeping price — the difference is exactly \(\partial\rho\), the rim of a fill of area \(\frac{1}{2N} = \frac{1}{16}\) at \(N = 8\), and rims of cheap fills are what the flat norm forgives. (b) The limit is the diagonal chain: the flat distance from \(T_N\) to it is at most \(\frac{1}{2N} \to 0\), and its mass is \(\sqrt{2}\) — the one-segment chain from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), priced by Pythagoras. (c) The flat norm forgets exactly the fills: two chains flat-close can have masses far apart, because the part of the difference that is a small-area rim counts for almost nothing. So a mass-functional on a flat-closed world cannot be continuous — §2's family would violate continuity at the diagonal, since \(M(T_N) = 2\) for all \(N\) but \(M(\lim) = \sqrt{2}\). Lower semicontinuity is the largest honest promise: limits may drop, never jump. And that is precisely what a minimizing sequence needs — the drop is how a floor gets attained.
