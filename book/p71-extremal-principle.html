The Patterns Atlas · P71 · the thirteenth move of tier 2

The extremal principle

Among any finite family of objects, one is largest and one is smallest — and the extreme member usually knows something its ordinary siblings do not. Pick the farthest pair, the longest path, the highest point, the shortest segment: the choice costs one sentence, and it hands you a fact for free — nothing about the extreme case is average, and every direction away from it is pinned. This page is P03's promise called in: P03 used the extremes to break claims; here the same choice builds them.

New word: ordinary line. Given a finite set of points, an ordinary line is a line that passes through exactly two of them — no more. Most lines through a pair of points pick up strays; an ordinary line is a pair standing alone. New word: diameter pair. In a finite set of points, a diameter pair is two points of the set whose distance apart is the largest the set offers. There may be ties; pick any one pair and call it the diameter pair. New word: tournament. A tournament on \(n\) players is a complete schedule in which every pair plays and every game has a winner — for each pair, exactly one of the two directions "beats" holds. It is the mathematical object behind "round-robin, no draws". New word: attained. A maximum is attained if some object actually achieves it — not merely approached. Among the reals strictly between 0 and 1 there is no largest number; the values climb towards 1 forever without touching it. The extremal principle is only licensed where the maximum is attained, and checking attainment is part of the move.

1 · Before we begin

P03 taught the adversarial case menu: boundary, degenerate, symmetric, dangerous, extreme. Its box said it outright:

Pattern P03 — Stress-test with special cases. Before believing a conjecture (yours or someone else's), or before spending effort proving it, deliberately attack it with the cases most likely to break it: the boundary cases, the degenerate configurations, the symmetric ones, the dangerous values, and the extremes. Treat every kill as information: read the wreckage, repair precisely, and re-state the claim with what you learned.

Or, in words: don't ask the claim its friendly questions. Ask it the ones it hopes you won't — the smallest, the strangest, the most extreme — and if it breaks, the broken piece tells you what the honest version needed all along.

And P03 pointed forward, in its own doors section: you want extremes to build, not break — the extremal principle is the constructive sibling: choose the largest or smallest object and it often knows something the average ones do not. Here is the tension this page resolves. P03's extremes were interrogations: pick the extreme case and watch the claim die. But half of mathematics uses the same choice as a foundation: pick the extreme object, observe that the extremality itself constrains it — the largest thing cannot have anything larger nearby — and read a proof off the constraint. The move from interrogation to construction is this page's whole content.

2 · Meet the problem: Sylvester's fifty-year question

Take these seven points (grid coordinates, easy to draw):

Every pair of points determines a line: \(\binom{7}{2} = 21\) pairs to check. Three of the lines are crowded — \(A, B, C\) on the line \(y = 0\); \(D, E, F\) on \(y = 1\); \(A, D, G\) on \(x = 0\) — and the remaining pairs stand alone: exactly 12 ordinary lines, and you can find each one by hand in a few minutes of slope-checking. So far the drawing suggests a comfortable hunch: surely every finite set of points has an ordinary line — with seven points and only three collinear triples, ordinary lines are everywhere.

Now push the hunch. Crowd the points more cleverly — the projective-plane configurations of the kind P70's ledger loves — and ordinary lines get scarce; by 1893 the question was live enough that Sylvester posed it plainly: must every finite, non-collinear set of points in the plane determine an ordinary line? The hunch says yes, and for fifty years nobody could prove it. The average line tells you nothing: a line through four points and a line through two look alike from the outside. What finally worked was an extreme choice — and the proof (Exemplar A) is a model of this page's move: among all (point, line) pairs at positive distance, pick the one where the distance is smallest, and squeeze the geometry until the extremality contradicts itself unless the line is ordinary.

Notice what the extremal choice bought. No census of configurations, no case split over "how the points lie" — one selection rule, one contradiction. The rest of this page names the move and runs it in three different neighborhoods.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P71 — The extremal principle

Pattern P71 — The extremal principle. When a claim resists direct construction and its objects come in a finite family (or a family where a maximum is provably attained): (1) choose the extreme object — the largest, smallest, longest, or closest, under a measure you state out loud; (2) extract the free constraint: extremality forbids anything of the same kind from beating the chosen object in the relevant direction, and that prohibition is a fact you can use without proving anything new; (3) derive the claim from the constraint, or derive a contradiction from supposing the claim fails — the extreme object then sits in the gap. The measure and the attainment are the two licenses: state the measure, prove the maximum is attained, and only then spend the constraint.

Or, in words: take the biggest one — it has nowhere to hide. An extreme object is surrounded by a fence the whole family built for it: nothing beyond it in the measure's direction, and that fence is a hypothesis you got for free. Proofs by the extremal principle do not work harder than other proofs; they work at the one spot where the fence does the work.

The three-key diagnosis. The claim type: an existence claim — some object with a property must exist — or a structural claim that follows from one. The problem shape: the objects form a family you can measure, the direct construction flounders because ordinary members carry no information, and yet the property you want is "visible at the top" — the extreme member either has the property or forces a contradiction. The tools at hand: P03's instinct for which measure to try (try the measure that appears in the claim), P02's small cases (to see what the extreme looks like concretely before generalizing), and P10's pigeonhole (the frequent partner — extremality sets up the boxes, pigeonhole fills one).

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar A — Kelly's proof of Sylvester's theorem (combinatorial geometry). The claim: every finite set of points in the plane, not all on one line, determines an ordinary line. Choose among all pairs (point \(P\), line \(\ell\)) with \(P\) in the set, \(\ell\) determined by the set, \(P\) not on \(\ell\), one where the distance \(h(P, \ell)\) is minimal — the family is finite, so the minimum is attained (the license, checked in one sentence). Say \(\ell\) contains the set-points \(A, B, C, \ldots\). If \(\ell\) is not ordinary, at least three of them sit on \(\ell\); drop the perpendicular from \(P\) to \(\ell\), meeting at \(Q\), and among the (at least three) set-points on \(\ell\), two — call them \(A\) and \(B\) — must lie on the same side of \(Q\) along \(\ell\) (pigeonhole, P10: three points, two sides). Name them so that \(A\) is the one nearer \(Q\): \(A\) sits between \(Q\) and \(B\). Now consider the pair (point \(A\), line \(PB\)). The triangle \(PAB\) has base \(|AB|\) lying on \(\ell\) and height \(|PQ| = h(P, \ell)\), so its area is \(\tfrac{1}{2}|AB| \cdot |PQ|\); computing the same area with \(PA\) as the base gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}|PA| \cdot d(A, PB)\), where \(d(A, PB)\) is the distance from \(A\) to the line \(PB\). Equate and cancel: \(d(A, PB) = \tfrac{|AB|}{|PB|} \cdot |PQ|\). And the fence closes: \(|AB| < |PB|\) — because \(|AB| = |BQ| - |AQ| < |BQ|\), a leg of the right triangle \(PQB\), while \(|PB|\) is its hypotenuse. So \(d(A, PB) < |PQ| = h(P, \ell)\): a (point, line) pair strictly closer than the minimizer — contradiction. The only escape is that \(\ell\) never held three points: it is ordinary. The fence did the work, and the numerics check out on the encounter's seven points, where the minimum-distance pair is \(E\) against the ordinary line \(CG\), at distance \(1/\sqrt{13} \approx 0.2774\). Moves used: P71 (the minimal pair), P10 (two of three on one side), P03 (the fence-check at the tie).

Exemplar B — Rédei's theorem: every tournament has a full directed path (graphs). A tournament on \(n\) players — every pair plays, one winner each. Claim: the players can be lined up \(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n\) so that each beats the next: \(v_1 \to v_2 \to \cdots \to v_n\). Choose among all directed paths (line-ups where each beats the next) one of maximum length — the family is finite, so a longest one exists. Suppose some player \(u\) is not on it. Then \(u\) beats \(v_1\) — otherwise \(v_1 \to u\) would extend the path at the front. And \(v_n\) beats \(u\) — otherwise the path extends at the back. So somewhere along the line-up the arrows relative to \(u\) flip: there is an \(i\) with \(v_i \to u\) and \(u \to v_{i+1}\). Splice: \(v_1 \to \cdots \to v_i \to u \to v_{i+1} \to \cdots \to v_n\) — a path of length \(n\) through all the players, contradicting maximality. No player is missing; the longest path was already full. The fence did everything: the two "otherwise the path extends" facts are the free constraint, and the flip is pigeonhole in motion. The claim holds for every tournament: all \(2^{\binom{4}{2}} = 64\) tournaments on four players and all \(2^{\binom{5}{2}} = 1024\) on five carry a full directed path (Exercise 2 works one by hand). Moves used: P71 (longest path), P03 (the flip census at the splice).

Exemplar C — the discrete maximum principle (potential theory, discrete). Let \(u\) be a function on the integer grid \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) taking integer values, bounded (all values between two fixed integers), and harmonic at every point: each value is the average of its four neighbors, \(\[u(i, j) = \tfrac{u(i{+}1, j) + u(i{-}1, j) + u(i, j{+}1) + u(i, j{-}1)}{4}.\]\) Claim: \(u\) is constant. Choose a point where \(u\) is maximal — legitimate, because a bounded set of integers is finite, so the maximum is attained (the license; note how the integer hypothesis is doing real work — the real-valued function \(u(i, j) = i\) is bounded on no line and the claim dies with it, as §5 shows). At the maximum point \(p\), all four neighbors are \(\le u(p)\), yet their average is \(u(p)\): four numbers, each at most \(M\), averaging exactly \(M\) — all four must equal \(M\). So the maximality spreads to the neighbors, then to theirs: the whole grid is one value. The fence — "nothing nearby is bigger" — plus an averaging identity is the entire proof. On a finite grid with boundary values fixed, the same argument shows the maximum of the interior values is controlled by the boundary — the fact that makes the discrete Laplacian numerically stable, and the small-grid version is the stability fact every finite-difference computation relies on. Moves used: P71 (the maximum point), P03 (attainment via discreteness), P08 (the averaging argument treats all four neighbors identically).

5 · When the trick breaks

The maximum may not be attained. On the open interval \((0, 1)\), the values of \(x\) climb towards 1 and never arrive; the "largest element" does not exist, and any argument starting "take the largest \(x\)" is decoration on a vacancy. The discrete maximum principle's fine print is the same trap: over the unbounded grid \(\mathbb{Z}^2\), the function \(u(i, j) = i\) is harmonic at every point — \(\tfrac{(i+1) + (i-1) + i + i}{4} = i\) exactly — and spectacularly non-constant. The failure is precisely the failure of attainment: the values have no maximum to stand on. The license is not a formality; it is the proof's foundation, and on infinite families it is where the work goes.

The extreme may be the wrong extreme. Extremality fences the object in one direction only. Take the diameter pair of a point set — the two farthest-apart points — and the seductive claim "every other point sees the pair at an angle of at least \(90^\circ\)". Run it: \(a = (-1, 0)\), \(b = (1, 0)\), \(c = (0, 1.1)\). The pair \((a, b)\) is a genuine diameter pair (\(|ab| = 2\), while \(|ac| = |bc| = \sqrt{2.21} \approx 1.487\)), yet the angle at \(c\) has cosine \(\tfrac{(-1)(1) + (-1.1)(-1.1)}{2.21} = \tfrac{0.21}{2.21} > 0\) — strictly less than \(90^\circ\). The diameter fence points along the segment, not around it; the claim needed a different extreme (the closest pair to a line, in Kelly's proof) and no amount of pushing repairs the wrong choice. When an extremal argument stalls, the first audit is the measure: is the fence built along the direction the claim needs?

Ties, and the honesty of "the". "The largest element" presumes uniqueness that families rarely offer. An equilateral triangle has three longest sides; a regular tournament (Exemplar B's five-player table, where every player wins exactly two games) has many longest paths. The move survives ties — "pick any one and call it the diameter pair" — but the argument must be checked to use only what every maximizer shares, not what the particular pick happens to have. The one-line audit: rerun the proof's steps against a different maximizer; if any step cites an accident of the choice, the proof is not yet done.

Greedy is not extremal. Iterating a locally extreme choice (take the shortest segment, then the next shortest, …) is a different — and much weaker — move than optimizing over the whole object at once. The non-crossing matching theorem is proved by minimizing the total length over all perfect matchings at once; the greedy shortest-first version can paint itself into a crossing. The scope of this page is the one-shot extreme over the whole family; iterated extremes belong to a different pattern (P72's monovariants are the honest bridge).

Honest scope: the extremal principle converts existence of an attained maximum into a free one-directional constraint. It gives no information about non-extreme objects, it gives nothing in the direction the measure does not point, and on infinite families the attainment license is the whole proof. When the extreme exists but the fence points the wrong way, the repair is a new measure — chosen, as P03 taught, to aim at the claim's actual load-bearing direction.

6 · What to try next

The extremal principle is the static member of a family of "one special object" moves, and its siblings are one door away. When the extreme object can be modified — moved, flipped, grown — and the modification must not decrease the measure, the argument becomes dynamic: that is P72 · Monovariants, the constructive engine behind the descent arguments, and the natural next page. When the extremal choice is over pairs and the fence is a counting statement, the partner is P10 · Pigeonhole — Kelly's proof used them braided, and the Hesse exercise below needs P70 · Double counting on top. When the family is infinite and attainment is the obstacle, the repair is usually a compactness or discreteness hypothesis imported from the claim's own statement — reread the claim's fine print before inventing machinery. The default: before any existence claim, spend one minute choosing the measure and checking attainment; the minute is the cheapest in mathematics.

7 · Lineage interlude

The move is as old as maxima and minima, but its combinatorial adulthood is recent. James Joseph Sylvester posed the ordinary-line question in 1893, in the pages of the Educational Times, and it resisted proof for half a century: Eberhard Melchior proved the projective dual in 1941, Paul Erdős restated the conjecture in 1943, Tibor Gallai proved it soon after — his proof is dated 1944 — and Leroy Kelly's minimum-distance argument, the one Exemplar A runs, became the classic proof; de Bruijn and Erdős's 1948 paper on incidence geometry brought the whole circle of ideas to a wide audience. (Kelly himself later suggested, in 1986, that Sylvester may have been prodded by the Hesse configuration — Exercise 5's nine points and twelve lines — whose inflection points over \(\mathbb{C}\) are exactly the ordinary-line theorem's failure.) László Rédei proved the tournament path theorem in 1934; the maximum-principle tradition in analysis runs from the classical elliptic theory through the discrete formulations codified by Protter and Weinberger in 1967. And the deepest modern descendant is the Erdős–Szekeres theorem of 1935 — any sequence of \((r-1)(s-1) + 1\) distinct numbers carries an increasing run of \(r\) or a decreasing run of \(s\) — whose proof is this page's fence, applied to a cleverly labeled census, and whose exercise version is Exercise 3 below.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○

In any triangle, the longest side faces the largest angle. Verify the claim on the \(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\) right triangle and on an equilateral triangle (what happens to "the" longest side, and does the claim survive the tie?). Then prove it: suppose \(a \ge b \ge c\) are the sides opposite angles \(A, B, C\); from the law of sines, \(\tfrac{a}{\sin A} = \tfrac{b}{\sin B}\), and \(\sin\) is increasing on \([0^\circ, 90^\circ]\) — say where the obtuse case needs its own sentence.

Hint

In the \(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\) triangle the angles are approximately \(36.87^\circ\), \(53.13^\circ\), \(90^\circ\) — check the order matches the side order. For the proof: an obtuse angle is automatically the largest, so you may assume all angles are acute, where \(\sin\) is strictly increasing.

Worked answer

The \(3\text{-}4\text{-}5\) triangle: sides \(3 < 4 < 5\), opposite angles \(\arcsin\tfrac{3}{5} \approx 36.87^\circ < \arcsin\tfrac{4}{5} \approx 53.13^\circ < 90^\circ\) — the order agrees. The equilateral triangle: all sides tie, all angles equal \(60^\circ\); "the longest side" is not unique, but the claim (longest side faces a largest angle) survives — every longest side faces a largest angle. Proof: if some angle \(A\) is obtuse or right, it is the unique largest angle, and its opposite side \(a\) is the longest (the side opposite the largest angle in any triangle is the longest, by the law of cosines: \(a^2 = b^2 + c^2 - 2bc\cos A \ge b^2 + c^2 - bc \ge \max(b^2, c^2)\), with equality forcing \(b = c = a\)). Otherwise all angles are acute and \(\sin\) is strictly increasing on \([0^\circ, 90^\circ]\); from \(\tfrac{a}{\sin A} = \tfrac{b}{\sin B} = \tfrac{c}{\sin C}\), larger side ⟺ larger angle, both directions. The tie audit of §5: in the acute case the correspondence is an if-and-only-if, so ties on sides match ties on angles — the proof uses only what every maximizer shares.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Five players round-robin, results: 1 beats 2 and 3; 2 beats 3 and 4; 3 beats 4 and 5; 4 beats 1 and 5; 5 beats 1 and 2. (Every player wins exactly two — a regular tournament.) (a) Find a full directed path through all five players by hand. (b) Every one of the \(2^{10} = 1024\) tournaments on five labeled players has such a path; explain in the language of Exemplar B why the extremal proof gives every tournament at once, and where the proof uses that the number of players is finite.

Hint

For (a): who beats whom among {4, 1, 2, 3, 5}, in that order? Check each consecutive pair. For (b): the longest-path fence — "otherwise the path extends" — never mentions which tournament you are in.

Worked answer

(a) \(4 \to 1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 5\): 4 beats 1 ✓, 1 beats 2 ✓, 2 beats 3 ✓, 3 beats 5 ✓ — a full directed path. (b) The proof in Exemplar B never inspects the win–loss table beyond the pair at hand: "if \(v_1\) beats \(u\), prepend" and "if \(u\) beats \(v_n\), append" are valid in any tournament, and the flip-splice is forced by scanning the fixed line-up. So one argument covers all \(2^{\binom{n}{2}}\) tournaments simultaneously — that is what the extremal fence buys: the constraint is structural, not statistical. Finiteness is used exactly once: a longest path exists because there are finitely many line-ups of \(n\) players. On an infinite tournament the claim survives but needs a different argument (a choice function or compactness), because "longest" may not be attained — the §5 license again.

Exercise 3 ●●●

Any sequence of \(17\) distinct real numbers contains an increasing subsequence of length \(5\) or a decreasing subsequence of length \(5\). (a) Show \(16\) does not suffice: verify that \(4, 3, 2, 1,\; 8, 7, 6, 5,\; 12, 11, 10, 9,\; 16, 15, 14, 13\) has no monotone run of length \(5\). (b) Prove the \(17\) claim: label each term with the pair (length of the longest increasing run ending there, longest decreasing run ending there) and show the \(16\) labels are distinct — then pigeonhole (P10) finishes. (c) A computer sweep confirms the claim on \(1000\) random permutations of \(\{1, \ldots, 17\}\); explain why that census is a detector, not a proof, and what the proof in (b) delivers that the census cannot.

Hint

For (a): an increasing run picks at most one term from each decreasing block of four — and there are four blocks. For (b): if two terms carried the same pair \((r, s)\) and the earlier one is smaller, which run grows?

Worked answer

(a) Each of the four blocks is decreasing, so a decreasing run takes at most \(4\) (one whole block); an increasing run takes at most one term per block, and there are \(4\) blocks — at most \(4\). A direct run-by-run enumeration confirms it. (b) Suppose terms \(x_i\) (earlier) and \(x_j\) (later) carry the same label \((r, s)\). If \(x_i < x_j\), an increasing run of length \(r\) ends at \(x_i\), and appending \(x_j\) makes length \(r + 1\) end at \(x_j\) — contradicting the label; if \(x_i > x_j\), the decreasing run grows the same way. Either way the labels differ. So the \(17\) terms have \(17\) distinct labels \((r, s)\) with \(1 \le r, s \le 4\) — only \(16\) pairs exist, pigeonhole contradiction. (c) The census is a detector: \(1000\) confirmations raise confidence and can kill a wrong constant, but they touch none of the structure. The proof delivers the exact boundary (\(16\) vs \(17\)), the reason (label injectivity + pigeonhole), and the general law — \((r-1)(s-1) + 1\) terms force a run of \(r\) up or \(s\) down — which no census states.

Exercise 4 ◆

A student argues: "Under \(a + b = 10\) with \(a, b > 0\), the quantity \(a^2 + b^2\) is largest at the most symmetric point \(a = b = 5\), where it equals \(50\) — by symmetry." Diagnose the argument: which pattern's warning does it violate, what is the true maximum (and where), and what does the symmetric point actually optimize? Then state the general lesson as a rule about when symmetry arguments locate maxima and when they locate something else.

Worked answer

The claim dies by computation: \(a^2 + b^2 = a^2 + (10 - a)^2 = 2a^2 - 20a + 100 = 2(a - 5)^2 + 50\). The symmetric point \(a = 5\) is the minimum (\(50\)); the maximum on \(0 < a < 10\) is approached at the boundary and, over positive integers, attained at \(a = 1, b = 9\) (or swapped): \(1 + 81 = 82\). The violated warning is Box P03.A's item 3 via P03's own register: the symmetric case is where hidden assumptions surface — it is a stress-test, not a certificate; symmetry shows a point is critical (the derivative vanishes, or the value is pinned by the symmetry), and a critical point may be a minimum. The general lesson: symmetry arguments locate candidates — points fixed by the symmetry — and the extremal direction must be checked separately (here, the sum of squares is convex, so symmetry pins the bottom, and the maximum lives at the fence: the boundary, or the integer extremes). §5's "wrong extreme" in miniature: the fence points outward, and the student read it as pointing inward.

Exercise 5 ◐

Over the complex plane, Sylvester's ordinary-line theorem fails. Consider the nine points of \(\mathbb{CP}^2\) (with \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}\) a primitive cube root of unity): \([0{:}1{:}-1], [0{:}1{:}-\omega], [0{:}1{:}-\omega^2]\); \([1{:}0{:}-1], [1{:}0{:}-\omega], [1{:}0{:}-\omega^2]\); \([1{:}-1{:}0], [1{:}-\omega{:}0], [1{:}-\omega^2{:}0]\). (i) Verify each point satisfies \(x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 0\) (the inflection points of the cubic \(x^3 + y^3 + z^3 = 0\)). (ii) Verify that every line through two of the nine contains a third — there are \(12\) such lines, each with exactly \(3\) points, so \(12 \times \binom{3}{2} = 36 = \binom{9}{2}\): zero ordinary lines (P70's ledger, audited). (iii) Explain which step of Kelly's proof breaks over \(\mathbb{C}\): with a complex distance picked, the three points on the extremal line need not have a "same side of \(Q\)" — the cube roots \(1, \omega, \omega^2\) form an equilateral triangle around any natural origin, and "side of a point on a line" is a real-line concept the complex line does not honor. This configuration (\(9_4, 12_3\)) is the Hesse configuration, and it is why the theorem's statement begins "in the plane" — the real plane.

Hint

For (ii): the \(3\) coordinate lines \(x = 0, y = 0, z = 0\) each carry three of the points; the other \(9\) lines have equations \(x + \omega^a y + \omega^b z = 0\) for \(a, b \in \{0, 1, 2\}\). For (iii): reread the pigeonhole step of Exemplar A — it needs an order on the line.

Worked answer

(i) For \([0{:}1{:}-\omega^k]\): \(0 + 1 + (-\omega^k)^3 = 1 - \omega^{3k} = 1 - 1 = 0\); the other two families are the same computation with the zero in a different slot. (ii) A direct check: the \(9\) lines \(x + \omega^a y + \omega^b z = 0\) each contain \([0{:}1{:}-\omega^{a-b}]\), \([1{:}0{:}-\omega^{-b}]\), and \([1{:}-\omega^{-a}{:}0]\) — three points each — and together with the \(3\) coordinate lines they cover all \(\binom{9}{2} = 36\) pairs exactly once each (each line accounts for \(\binom{3}{2} = 3\) pairs, \(12 \times 3 = 36\)). No pair determines a line with only two points: zero ordinary lines. (iii) Kelly's proof, after choosing the minimal-distance pair \((P, \ell)\), needs three set-points on \(\ell\) to have two on the same side of the foot \(Q\) — "side" is an order notion on a real line, delivered by P10's two-box pigeonhole. A complex line is a copy of \(\mathbb{C}\), a plane: its points have no linear order, the pigeonhole has no boxes, and the fence collapses. The extremal choice survives the passage to \(\mathbb{C}\); the free constraint does not — a precise instance of §5's scope note that the constraint is one-directional and lives where the measure lives.
