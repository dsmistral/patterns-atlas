The Patterns Atlas · P56 · the seventh move of tier 5

Rescale for asymptotic geometry

Zoom out until the space becomes its own shape. A circle hides a parabola; a crossing hides its two tangent lines; a cusp hides nothing at all — it is its own shape already, at every magnification that respects it. The move finds the one zoom at which nothing vanishes and nothing blows up, and the shape left standing — the tangent cone, the fixed point, the limit profile — is the geometry that was there all along.

New word: the dominant balance. A curve near a point, a family near its limit, a process at small scale — each is governed by a few leading terms, and the move is to name the magnification that shows them. A zoom is a pair of exponents: \(x = \varepsilon^a u\), \(y = \varepsilon^b v\), with \(\varepsilon\) the dial you will send to zero. Almost every zoom is a bad zoom: too coarse and the picture collapses to a line, too fine and it flies apart to infinity. The dominant balance is the zoom at which the leading terms survive together — nothing vanishes, nothing blows up — and the shape visible at that zoom is the object's asymptotic geometry: the tangent cone of a curve, the limit profile of a layer, the fixed point of an iterated rescaling. P15 taught the \(\varepsilon\) as a demand to be met; this page teaches it as a lens to be focused. Focusing it is itself a computation: weigh each monomial by the exponents \(a\) and \(b\), demand that the fewest survive at the lowest weight, and the balance reads off a one-line table. And when the zoom is iterated — rescale, rescale again — the question changes from "what does this look like" to "what is unchanged by looking": the shapes that answer are the self-similar ones, and hunting them is half of what the research frontier calls renormalization.

2 · Meet the problem

What does a circle look like at its own point? Take the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) near its top \((0,1)\), and measure depth downwards from the top: \(Y = 1 - y\), so the curve reads \(x^2 + (1 - Y)^2 = 1\), and expanding the square gives the exact identity

\[x^2 \;=\; 2Y - Y^2 \;=\; Y(2 - Y).\]

How deep is the curve at half-width \(x\)? The naive answer reads the balance wrong: "the left side is quadratic, so \(Y \approx x^2\)". No — the equation says \(x^2 \approx 2Y\) while \(Y\) is small, so \(Y \approx x^2/2\), and the honest value is \(Y = 1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2} = \frac{x^2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - x^2}}\), the sagitta the ancients knew. At the rational point \(x = 3/5\) the depth is exactly \(1/5\); at \(x = 7/25\), exactly \(1/25\); at \(x = 9/41\), exactly \(1/41\) — every Pythagorean point a fraction, and every one of them close to half a square.

Now zoom — and watch the zoom fail, twice. Zoom both axes by \(\varepsilon\): the depth of the rescaled curve is \(Y(\varepsilon u)/\varepsilon\), and since \(Y(\varepsilon u) \approx \varepsilon^2 u^2/2\), the quotient is \(\varepsilon u^2/2\), going to zero: the circle, magnified evenly, collapses to a horizontal line. Zoom \(x\) by \(\varepsilon\) and depth by \(\varepsilon^3\): the quotient \(Y(\varepsilon u)/\varepsilon^3 \approx u^2/(2\varepsilon)\) flies apart. Between those two failures sits the one zoom that shows something: \(x\) by \(\varepsilon\), depth by \(\varepsilon^2\), giving \(Y(\varepsilon u)/\varepsilon^2 \to u^2/2\). At that magnification the circle is a parabola, \(v = u^2/2\), and the failure of the other two zooms is not an annoyance but the discovery: the balance is a property of the equation, computable from it, and it is unique up to a common factor. Every circle hides a parabola, and the zoom that respects the equation's own weights is the only one that finds it.

Three questions, each sharpening the last.

Q1 — is the limit shape a fact about the circle, or an artifact of the zoom? The balanced zoom converges to \(u^2/2\); the others collapse or blow up. Which zooms are "legal", and does the answer depend on which one you chose?

Q2 — what is the difference between a crossing and a cusp? Two curves pass through the origin: \(y^2 = x^2(1 + x)\), which crosses itself, and \(y^2 = x^3\), which comes to a point and leaves. At unit scale they differ visibly; zoomed evenly, both collapse. Does any zoom separate them — and can the zoom's exponents serve as a fingerprint that no relabeling could forge?

Q3 — can a shape be its own limit? The circle's zoomed copies converge to a parabola that is not a circle. Is there an object that survives its own zoom — whose rescaled copy is exactly itself, at every magnification — and if so, is it found as a limit, the way a fixed point is?

The answers, in short. Q1: the balance is a ray, not a point — \((\varepsilon, \varepsilon^2)\) and \((\varepsilon^2, \varepsilon^4)\) give the same limit shape, and every zoom off the ray degenerates; the parabola is the circle's tangent cone, a fact about the circle. Q2: the exponents are a fingerprint: the node balances at weights \((1, 1)\) and its tangent cone factors as \((y - x)(y + x)\) — two lines, slopes \(\pm 1\), each confirmed by the exact chord-slope identity \(y/x = t\) on the rational parametrization; the cusp dies under \((1,1)\) and balances only at \((2, 3)\), where the rescaled equation \(v^2 = u^3\) is not merely approached but exact — the cusp's tangent cone is the cusp. Q3: yes, and three ways: the cusp is carried to itself by \((\lambda^2, \lambda^3)\) for every \(\lambda\); the middle-thirds Cantor set is the fixed point of the rescaling operator \(R(S) = S/3 \cup (S + 2)/3\), which contracts every seed to it; and the heat kernel satisfies \(G(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t) = \lambda^{-1} G(x, t)\) exactly — diffusion's shape, amplitude and all. The rest of the page earns those three answers in fractions.

3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P56 — Rescale for asymptotic geometry. Three steps, each a computation with a shape for its verdict. (1) Name the dial. Introduce the small parameter \(\varepsilon\) and say what is being sent to zero — the point approached, the time elapsed, the scale of the detail. Write the zoom \(x = \varepsilon^a u\), \(y = \varepsilon^b v\) with the exponents unknown: they are not style, they are the answer's first half. (2) Find the balance. Weigh every term: a monomial \(x^i y^j\) weighs \(i a + j b\). Demand the fewest terms survive at the lowest weight — the dominant balance — and check the demand is honest: every other choice of \((a, b)\) collapses the picture to nothing or blows it up to infinity, so the balance is a ray, computable from the equation alone. (3) Read the limit shape. Divide out the surviving power of \(\varepsilon\) and let \(\varepsilon \to 0\): the equation that remains is the tangent cone — the object's asymptotic geometry. Its factors are the tangent directions; its correction ladder — the terms the zoom suppressed, in powers of \(\varepsilon\) — is the honesty receipt. And when the limit shape is still singular, iterate: rescale again.

Or, in words: don't ask what the object looks like; ask what it looks like at the magnification that respects it, and compute that magnification from the object's own terms. The move's strength is what it refuses to promise: a tangent cone is a first-in-scale verdict, not a reconstruction — two different curves can share one — and a limit shape without its correction ladder is a rumor with a picture. What it promises is exact: the balance exponents are invariants no relabeling changes, the limit shape is unique up to the ray, and at sizes where every number is a fraction, every step is checkable by hand.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the equation must have a dominant balance. Two terms of equal lowest weight, a table to find them: that is all the move asks. Jam-mode: problems where every scale contributes equally — noise with no favored frequency, turbulence with no gap between scales — have no single zoom, and the honest alternative is P42's: analyze each scale separately and sum the stories, rather than magnify once and hope.

Key 2 — the balance must be computed, not assumed. The instinct is isotropic — zoom everything by the same factor — and the instinct is wrong exactly when it matters: evenly zoomed, the circle collapses to a line and the cusp vanishes entirely, their shapes invisible at every isotropic magnification. The weights come from the equation's own exponents, one line of integer arithmetic. Jam-mode: a zoom chosen by habit instead of by weights degenerates silently — nothing looks wrong, everything looks flat — and the silent degeneration is the failure mode the pattern exists to catch.

Key 3 — the limit must be quoted with its correction ladder. The tangent cone is a first-in-scale verdict: \(y^2 = x^2(1+x)\) and \(y^2 = x^2(1+x^2)\) share the cone \(v = \pm u\) and differ only in their suppressed terms — the first carries a correction of order \(\varepsilon u\), the second of order \(\varepsilon^2 u^2\). A shape without its ladder conflates them. Jam-mode: "the limit looks like \(v = \pm u\)" is true of both curves and therefore settles nothing between them; the honest quote is shape plus first correction, and the correction is a one-armed wall — P15's country — not a second shape.

Box P56.A — the theorem's engine, in miniature. The weight table. For a polynomial equation in \(x\) and \(y\), assign weights \((w_x, w_y)\); the monomial \(x^i y^j\) weighs \(i w_x + j w_y\); the initial form is the sum of lowest-weight monomials; the balance is the weight pair making at least two monomials share that lowest weight. The circle: \(Y(2 - Y) = x^2\) balances at \((1, 2)\) — \(2Y\) and \(x^2\) both weigh 2, \(Y^2\) weighs 4 — and the initial form \(2Y - x^2 = 0\) is \(Y = x^2/2\): the parabola, read off the table in one line. The node \(y^2 = x^2 + x^3\) balances at \((1,1)\); its initial form \(y^2 - x^2\) factors as \((y - x)(y + x)\): two lines. The cusp \(y^2 = x^3\) balances at \((2, 3)\); its initial form is the whole equation, exactly. The tacnode \(y^2 = x^4 + x^5\) balances at \((1, 2)\); its initial form factors as \((y - x^2)(y + x^2)\): two osculating parabolas. Research scale: the substitution \(x = \varepsilon u\) with the equation rescaled and \(\varepsilon\) retained is a blow-up chart — Hironaka's zoom, run on whole varieties; fractional weights like \((2, 3)\) are Newton's parallelogram and Puiseux's fractional powers, \(y = x^{3/2}\) wearing the parametrization \((t^2, t^3)\) as a disguise; and the iteration "rescale, then rescale the limit" is the skeleton of every resolution and renormalization argument this page sketches.

Box P56.B — where the move appears. Algebraic geometry: tangent cones classify singular points; blow-ups separate what cones conflate; Hironaka proved in 1964 that iterating the zoom resolves every singularity of a variety in characteristic zero — the move run to termination. Analysis: boundary layers hide their profiles at width \(\varepsilon\); matched asymptotics is the craft of reading two zooms and sewing them. Mathematical physics: Kadanoff's block spins (1966) rescale a magnet by hand; Wilson's renormalization group (1971) iterates the rescaling of a whole theory, and its fixed points and flows compute the critical exponents — work that earned the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physics. Geometric group theory: Gromov's asymptotic cones zoom a group by its own diameter until the group becomes a metric space in its own right — growth theorems read off the limit shape. Fractals: the middle-thirds Cantor set is the fixed point of \(R(S) = S/3 \cup (S + 2)/3\), an iterated rescaling whose attractor is a set no single scale describes. At the frontier: the ε-expansion in field theory, van den Dries and Wilkie's ultralimit route to asymptotic cones, and the whole industry of "scaling limits" — random walks to Brownian motion, percolation to SLE — are this one move, iterated and iterated again.

4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — the balance, computed three ways. First the closed form: multiplying \(1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) by its conjugate gives the exact identity \(Y = \frac{x^2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - x^2}}\), and at the Pythagorean points of the family \(x = a/c\) with \(c - b = 1\) — \((3,4,5)\), \((5,12,13)\), \((7,24,25)\), \((9,40,41)\) — the sag is exactly \((c-b)/c\): \(1/5, 1/13, 1/25, 1/41\). Against the parabola \(x^2/2\) the ratios are exactly \(10/9, 26/25, 50/49, 82/81\) — that is, exactly \(1 + 1/9, 1 + 1/25, 1 + 1/49, 1 + 1/81\): the circle closes on its tangent cone like \(1 + 1/a^2\), and the closing is a fraction identity, not a decimal observation. Second the ladder: expanding \(\sqrt{1 - x^2}\) gives

\[Y \;=\; \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{8} + \frac{x^6}{16} + \frac{5x^8}{128} + \frac{7x^{10}}{256} + \frac{21x^{12}}{1024} + \cdots,\]

each coefficient from the last by \(a_{k+1} = a_k \cdot \frac{2k-1}{2k+2}\) — \(1/2, 1/8, 1/16, 5/128, 7/256, 21/1024\) — and the suppressed tail is a one-armed wall: truncating after the \(x^4/8\) term at \(x = 3/5\) leaves a gap of exactly \(19/5000\), trapped below \(729/160000\), both fractions checkable by hand. Third the zoom itself: under the balanced magnification \(x = \varepsilon u\), \(Y = \varepsilon^2 v\), the rescaled depth is \(v_\varepsilon(u) = \frac{u^2}{1 + \sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2 u^2}}\), squeezed by the rational walls \(\frac{u^2}{2 - \varepsilon^2u^2/2} \le v_\varepsilon(u) \le \frac{u^2}{2 - \varepsilon^2 u^2}\) — at \(\varepsilon = 1/2, u = 1\) the walls read \(8/15 \le v \le 4/7\), a gap of exactly \(4/105\), and at \(\varepsilon = 1/4\) the gap is \(16/1953\) — a factor of \(1953/420 \approx 4.65\), a shade past the factor \(4\) that halving \(\varepsilon^2\) predicts, the correction ladder made visible. And the balance is a ray, not a point: the zoom \((\varepsilon^2, \varepsilon^4)\) shows the same parabola, while \((\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\) collapses \(v_\varepsilon(u) = \varepsilon u^2/2 + \cdots\) to zero and \((\varepsilon, \varepsilon^3)\) explodes it. P15's gap column was this move in miniature: the products \(n^2 \cdot \text{gap}\) run \(16.708, 15.777, 15.570, 15.520, 15.507, 15.504\) at \(n = 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, 192\), closing on \(\pi^3/2 \approx 15.5031\) from above — a constant that no polygon displayed and no two-sided trap ever named, because it was never a value at all: it was a shape, the asymptote of the data, visible only after the rescaling.

Encounter 2 — the fingerprints. Two singular curves through the origin, and the zoom that tells them apart. The node \(y^2 = x^2(1 + x)\): the parametrization \(x = t^2 - 1\), \(y = t(t^2 - 1)\) is an identity — \(y^2 = t^2(t^2-1)^2\) and \(x^2(1+x) = (t^2-1)^2 \cdot t^2\), the same fraction — and it visits the crossing twice, at \(t = 1\) and \(t = -1\). The chord slopes are exact: \(y/x = t\) whenever \(x \neq 0\), so the branches leave the origin along slopes \(t \to 1\) and \(t \to -1\), and the marked points \((9/16, 45/64)\), \((5/4, 15/8)\), \((3, 6)\) sit on the upper branch with slopes \(5/4, 3/2, 2\) — closing on 1 like a convergent. Zoomed isotropically, \((\varepsilon, \varepsilon)\), the node survives: \(v^2 = u^2(1 + \varepsilon u)\), the error \(\lvert v^2/u^2 - 1\rvert = \varepsilon u\) exactly — \(1/2\) at \(\varepsilon = 1/2\), \(1/4\) at \(\varepsilon = 1/4\) — and the tangent cone \(v = \pm u\) is the initial form \(y^2 - x^2 = (y - x)(y + x)\): the factorization and the zoom agree. The cusp \(y^2 = x^3\): parametrized \((t^2, t^3)\) — the point \((1,1)\) at \(t = 1\), \((9/4, 27/8)\) at \(t = 3/2\) — and here the isotropic zoom dies: \(v^2 = \varepsilon u^3\), so \(\lvert v \rvert \le \sqrt{\varepsilon}\) for \(\lvert u \rvert \le 1\): at \(\varepsilon = 1/4\) the whole rescaled cusp lives inside a band of height \(1/2\), collapsing to a segment — the cusp is invisible at every isotropic magnification. The balance is \((2, 3)\): under \((\varepsilon^2, \varepsilon^3)\) the rescaled equation is \(v^2 = u^3\) exactly, with no \(\varepsilon\) left anywhere — the cusp is not merely approximated by its tangent cone; it is its own tangent cone, at every magnification on the ray. That exactness has a name: \((t^2, t^3)\) maps to \((\lambda^2 t^2, \lambda^3 t^3) = ((\lambda t)^2, (\lambda t)^3)\), so the zoom \((\lambda^2, \lambda^3)\) carries the cusp onto itself for every \(\lambda\) — the node has no such family: under its own isotropic zoom the image equation reads \(y^2 = x^2(1 + \lambda x)\), which differs from the original for every \(\lambda \neq 1\). The balance rays are the fingerprint: node \((1,1)\), cusp \((2,3)\), tacnode \((1,2)\), and the curve \(y^3 = x^4 + x^5\) of Exercise 2 balances at \((3,4)\) — four singular points, four pairs of exponents, and no relabeling turns one ray into another.

Encounter 3 — the fixed point. A shape that is its own limit cannot be found by standing still; it must be found the way fixed points always are — by iteration. Take the operator \(R(S) = S/3 \cup (S + 2)/3\), which shrinks a set to a third of itself and keeps both a left and a right copy. From the seed \([0,1]\): \(R([0,1]) = [0, 1/3] \cup [2/3, 1]\), then \(2^N\) intervals of length \(3^{-N}\) at level \(N\), total length exactly \((2/3)^N\) — \(2/3, 4/9, 8/27, 16/81\) at the levels of Fig P56.3, \(64/729\) at level six — a set that never empties while its total length goes to zero. Each level sits inside the last, and P22's theorem says what that structure forces: both maps shrink distances by exactly \(1/3\), so \(R\) is a contraction, and every seed's iterates converge to the one fixed point \(C\) with \(R(C) = C\). The fixed point is the middle-thirds Cantor set — the space that is its own shape at scale \(1/3\) — and its membership is checkable in two independent ways that this page's lab runs to agreement: by intervals (which level-\(N\) third contains the point) and by digits (does the ternary expansion contain only \(0\)s and \(2\)s). The point \(1/4\) passes both: its ternary expansion is \(0.020202\ldots\) — exactly \(2(1/9 + 1/81 + \cdots) = 2 \cdot \frac{1/9}{1 - 1/9} = \frac{1}{4}\) — so \(1/4\) belongs to \(C\) without being an endpoint of any level: a point the dust owns from the inside.

Seeds wash out. Start the iteration elsewhere and the fixed point wins: from the single point \(\{1/2\}\), the level-\(N\) image has \(2^N\) points, each exactly \(3^{-N}/2\) from a point of \(C\) — the distance to the paired word's copy of \(0\), a fraction that at \(N = 6\) reads \(1/1458\); from the seed \([0,2]\), every endpoint of the level-\(N\) image sits exactly \(3^{-N}\) from an endpoint of the standard level — the overshoot \(f_I(2) - f_I(1) = 3^{-N}\) for every word \(I\) — so six iterations pull even a doubled seed to within \(1/729\). The limit does not remember the seed; the contraction grinds the memory out. That is the general shape of the theorem: a contraction has one fixed point, and everything flows to it — P22's promise, running here on sets instead of numbers.

The operator has a dial, and the dial has a cliff. Replace \(1/3\) by \(r\): \(R_r(S) = rS \cup (rS + 1 - r)\). At \(r = 2/5\) the first level is \([0, 2/5] \cup [3/5, 1]\) — the middle gap \((2/5, 3/5)\) is disjoint from every later level, and \(1/2\) stays outside forever: dust again, thinner. But at \(r = 1/2\) the first level is \([0, 1/2] \cup [1/2, 1]\), whose union is exactly \([0,1]\) — the copies touch, the dust becomes the whole interval, and \(R_{1/2}([0,1]) = [0,1]\) holds in one step. The transition is exact and the boundary is exactly \(r = 1/2\): for every \(r \ge 1/2\) the two copies overlap (\(1 - r \le r\)) and the attractor is the interval; for every \(r < 1/2\) the gap is born in one step and never refills. One dial position separates dust from interval — the kind of statement the research frontier calls universality, seen here at desk size.

And one fixed shape comes with its own amplitude. The heat kernel \(G(x, t) = (4\pi t)^{-1/2} e^{-x^2/(4t)}\) — the temperature profile a point source leaves after time \(t\) — satisfies, exactly, \(G(\lambda x, \lambda^2 t) = \lambda^{-1} G(x, t)\): space zoomed by \(\lambda\), time by \(\lambda^2\), height by \(1/\lambda\). The receipts are two cleared-denominator identities: the exponent, \(\frac{(\lambda x)^2}{4 \lambda^2 t} = \frac{x^2}{4t}\) because \(\lambda^2 x^2 \cdot 4t = x^2 \cdot 4 \lambda^2 t\); the prefactor, \(4\pi \lambda^2 t = \lambda^2 \cdot 4\pi t\). No limit is taken — the identity holds at every \(\lambda\), the way the cusp's did — and the balance is familiar: space weighs \(1\), time weighs \(2\), the same \((1,2)\) ray as the circle's sag. Diffusion is parabolic in exactly the sense the circle is, and its shape at any scale is its shape at every scale, half as tall each time you double the room.

SKETCH — the research theorem behind this page: Hironaka (1964) proved every algebraic variety over a field of characteristic zero can be resolved — made smooth — by a finite sequence of blow-ups, each one an explicit, chart-by-chart zoom of exactly the kind the node and cusp received above; Wilson (1971) proved that near a second-order phase transition the infinite tower of coupling constants closes into a fixed shape, because the rescaling map's fixed point — the cusp-like profile that survives its own zoom — is what "universality" names. What this page builds is the desk-size engine: compute the weight table, find the balance ray, verify the first correction. What it names but does not build: the blow-up charts \((u, uv)\) and their coordinate algebra, the Hilbert–Samuel induction that terminates Hironaka's sequence, the \(\beta\)-functions and \(\varepsilon\)-expansion that locate Wilson's fixed point. The showcase receipt is that the balance rays computed here — \((1,1)\) for the node, \((2,3)\) for the cusp, \((1,2)\) for the tacnode, the parabola, and the heat kernel — are the same numbers the research theory computes by heavier machines.

5 · Exhibits: when rescaling fails

Every pattern earns its keep by failing. Three exhibits — each a family that zooms wrong, or zooms right and then lies — with the mitigation, the price, and the default.

Exhibit A — the wrong zoom. Zoom the cusp \(y^2 = x^3\) with weights \((1,1)\) — space and space, the isotropic habit — and the equation collapses: \(v^2 = \varepsilon u^3\) degenerates as \(\varepsilon \to 0\) to \(v = 0\), a squashed line with no memory of the curve. Zoom the node \(y^2 = x^2(1+x)\) with weights \((2,3)\) — the cusp's balance, transplanted — and the equation blows up: \(y^2 - x^2 - x^3 = 0\) becomes \(\varepsilon^6 v^2 - \varepsilon^4 u^2 - \varepsilon^6 u^3 = 0\), and clearing by \(\varepsilon^6\) leaves \(v^2 = \varepsilon^{-2} u^2 + u^3\) — the \(x^2\) term has escaped to infinity, and the zoomed equation is singular the other way. The collapse and the blow-up are the two failure modes of a mis-weighted zoom, mirror images: give a variable the wrong weight and its terms either sink below the initial form (the cusp's collapse, \(v^2 = \varepsilon u^3\)) or tower above it (the node's escape, \(v^2 = \varepsilon^{-2} u^2 + u^3\)).

Mitigation: compute the weight table before zooming — the node's terms \(y^2, x^2, x^3\) under weights \((1,1)\) read \(2, 2, 3\): the minimum \(2\) is shared by two terms, the initial form \(y^2 - x^2\) wins, and \((1,1)\) is forced. Under the cusp's balance \((2,3)\) the same terms read \(6, 4, 6\): the minimum \(4\) belongs to \(x^2\) alone — one term, no initial form, the blow-up of the last paragraph. And the cusp's two terms \(y^2, x^3\) read \(2, 3\) under \((1,1)\) — minimum isolated, collapse — but \(6, 6\) under \((2,3)\): tied, balanced, exactly self-similar. The table decides; the eye does not.

Price: weight tables are honest only for polynomial-governed objects — equations with finitely many terms to weigh. The heat kernel earned its \((1,2)\) balance by cleared-denominator identities, not a table; a differential operator, a boundary condition, a physical constraint can each hide terms the table never sees. The method is exact where it applies and silent where it does not.

Default: never trust an uncomputed zoom. If the balance ray is not written down with its weight table or its identity, the "limit shape" is a guess wearing a lab coat.

Exhibit B — the limit forgets. Two curves share an initial form: \(y^2 = x^2(1 + x)\) and \(y^2 = x^2(1 + x^2)\). Both zoom to the cone \(v = \pm u\) at weights \((1,1)\); at the scale of the limit they are the same curve. But the corrections differ — the first reads \(v^2 = u^2 + \varepsilon u^3\), the second \(v^2 = u^2 + \varepsilon^2 u^4\) — and the corrections are the identity documents. The \((1,1)\) balance of the first is exact (a node, two branches crossing at slopes \(\pm 1\)); the second is tangent at the origin in a way the first is not, and the exponent on \(\varepsilon\) — \(1\) versus \(2\) — is the whole difference between a crossing and a kiss.

Mitigation: quote the shape and the first correction together, the way the sagitta squeeze did: \(u^2/2\) plus a wall, \(v = u^2/(1+\sqrt{1-r})\) with \(r = \varepsilon^2 u^2\), never the limit alone. The limit is the headline; the correction is the story.

Price: the correction puts you back in one-armed country — P15's country, where the walls improve forever without a lower partner: the two-sided squeeze closed to width \(4/105\) at one point and \(16/1953\) at the next, but a generic correction admits only one side, an upper wall on the error, and the honest statement is an inequality, not an identity.

Default: "limit plus correction" is the honest unit of asymptotic speech. A limit quoted alone is a first name without a surname — too many families share it.

Exhibit C — when no balance exists. The dyadic averages \(\frac{1}{2^n}\binom{n}{k}\) — P42's collection of coin-flip shapes — have no dominant balance at all: every \(k\) near the middle is within one scale of every other, and no reweighting of the \((n, k)\) axes separates a two-term initial form from the rest. Not every family has a zoom, and the failure is not a defect of the family — it is a fact about it, and P42's dyadic decomposition is the alternative move: when the scales will not separate, partition the problem and let the partition carry the geometry. The r-dial showed the same cliff from the other side: at \(r = 1/2\) exactly, \(R_r([0,1]) = [0,1]\) in one step — a degenerate fixed point where the zoom "works" so completely that it erases the problem instead of resolving it. A balance that is too good is a warning, not a victory.

Mitigation: before claiming self-similarity, check that something is preserved and something is lost — the cusp kept its equation and lost its scale; the heat kernel kept its profile and paid \(1/\lambda\) in height; the Cantor dust kept its construction and lost two-thirds of its length at every step. Zooms that preserve everything are usually changes of subject.

Price: the invariance check is the hard part. Wilson's Nobel citation is, in effect, the check done for a hard case: proving that the rescaling of a statistical system has a fixed point, and that the fixed point's preserved quantities are the ones physics measures. At desk size the check is a weight table; at research size it can be a career.

Default: no self-similar claim without a named preserved quantity. If you cannot say what stays fixed, you have not found the zoom — you have found a hope.

Scope, honestly. What this page establishes, it establishes by exact receipts: every balance ray quoted is an exact rational identity (node \((1,1)\), cusp \((2,3)\), tacnode \((1,2)\), heat kernel \((1,2)\), Pythagorean family computed to \(\pm 1\)); the sagitta series is computed to six terms with explicit remainder walls; the Cantor levels are enumerated to \(N = 8\) and the \(1/4\)-membership is verified by two independent routes (interval and ternary-digit) over the full \(3^6\) grid; the heat-kernel scaling is a cleared-denominator identity at every \(\lambda\); the \(\pi^3/2\) column is floats at the displayed precision, closing from above. What this page sketches, it names: Hironaka's resolution and Wilson's renormalization are research theorems cited by date, not rebuilt; matched asymptotics beyond the first correction is a named door, not an opened one. The gap between the desk engine and the research theorem is real, and every exhibit above lives in it.

6 · Doors out

  • Door — Shearer entropy counting (P59): the nearest door, and this page's footer. This page's zooms kept the shape and discarded the scale; Shearer-style entropy keeps the count and discards the arrangement. When a rescaled family stabilizes into many weakly-interacting pieces, the next question is how much freedom the pieces have left — and that is a census, one door down.
  • Door — dyadic decomposition (P42): the sibling move. When the scales refuse to separate — no dominant balance exists — the dyadic decomposition is the alternative: partition at every scale at once and let the levels carry the geometry. P42 is the move you reach for when this page's weight table comes back degenerate; Exhibit C showed the handoff.
  • Door — Nash–Moser fast Newton (P57): the hard version. Nash–Moser iteration is rescaling taken to research grade — a Newton scheme that must zoom its problem at every step because the linearization loses derivatives. The balance rays here are its baby pictures.
  • Door — the literature: Newton's De Analysi (composed 1669, published 1711) already differentiated by the leading term — the initial form, three centuries early. Puiseux (1850) built series in fractional powers, the \(x\)-axis gaining weight \(1/2\) at a branch point. Hironaka (1964, Annals of Mathematics 79, 109–203) resolved all singularities by iterated blow-ups; Kadanoff (1966, Physics 2, 263) found the block-spin scaling; Wilson (1971, Physical Review B) made the renormalization group a fixed-point theory and earned the 1982 Nobel; Gromov (1981) read groups through their asymptotic cones, with van den Dries–Wilkie (1984, Journal of Algebra 89, 349–374) showing the cones are tame enough to model.

7 · Lineage

  • 1669 — Newton. De Analysi per Aequationes Numero Terminorum Infinitas, composed 1669, circulated in manuscript, published London 1711 by William Jones: functions touched by their leading term — asymptotics as the first calculus.
  • 1850 — Puiseux. "Recherches sur les fonctions algébriques" puts the fractional-power series (first seen in Newton's 1676 letters) into algebraic geometry: near a branch point, \(y\) behaves like a power \(x^{1/2}\) — the first weight table that is not a whole number.
  • 1964 — Hironaka. "The Resolution of Singularities of an Algebraic Variety over a Field of Characteristic Zero", Annals of Mathematics 79(1), 109–203: every singularity yields to finitely many blow-ups — the zoom, iterated, always terminates. Fields Medal, 1970.
  • 1966 — Kadanoff. "Scaling laws for Ising models near \(T_c\)", Physics 2, 263: block-spin averaging — replace a patch of spins by one effective spin and rescale — the physics version of a weight table, and the seminal step toward renormalization group theory.
  • 1971 — Wilson. Physical Review B 3 and 4: the renormalization group as a fixed-point theory of the rescaling map — Kadanoff's zoom made a calculus. Nobel Prize in Physics, 1982.
  • 1981 — Gromov. Groups of polynomial growth read through asymptotic cones — the group "at infinite zoom" — with van den Dries–Wilkie (1984, Journal of Algebra 89, 349–374) proving the first-order theory of the zoomed structure stays tame: the limit shape is honest enough to reason in.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the receipts above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Parabola yes, circle no. (a) Show the parabola \(y = x^2\) is exactly self-similar: the zoom \((x, y) \mapsto (\lambda x, \lambda^2 y)\) carries the curve onto itself for every \(\lambda > 0\). Verify the identity at \(\lambda = 2, 3, 1/2\) over the points \(x = 1, 1/2, 3/2\). (b) Show the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) fails the same zoom at \(\lambda = 2\): the image of \((3/5, 4/5)\) lands at \((6/5, 16/5)\), and report the exact value of \((6/5)^2 + (16/5)^2\). (c) The circle is still honest, one order up: the sagitta \(v_\varepsilon(u) = u^2/(1 + \sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2 u^2})\) sits between the walls \(u^2/(2 - r/2) \le v \le u^2/(2 - r)\) with \(r = \varepsilon^2 u^2\). Compute both walls at \((\varepsilon, u) = (1/2, 1)\) and at \((1/4, 1)\), and verify each pair brackets \(v\) exactly (no decimals).

Hint

For (a), substitute and watch the \(\lambda\)'s cancel. For (b), square and add. For (c), the two walls come from \(\sqrt{1 - r} \le 1 - r/2\) and \(\sqrt{1 - r} \ge 1 - r\); to bracket \(v = 2/(2 + \sqrt{3})\) without decimals, square the proposed inequalities until the roots are isolated on one side.

Worked answer

(a) A point \((x, x^2)\) lands at \((\lambda x, \lambda^2 x^2)\), whose second coordinate is exactly \((\lambda x)^2\) — the identity holds for every \(\lambda\), and at \(\lambda = 2, 3, 1/2\) over \(x = 1, 1/2, 3/2\): \((2, 4)\), \((1, 1)\), \((3, 9)\); \((3, 9)\), \((3/2, 9/4)\), \((9/2, 81/4)\); \((1/2, 1/4)\), \((1/4, 1/16)\), \((3/4, 9/16)\) — every image is on the parabola. (b) The image is \((6/5, 16/5)\), and \((6/5)^2 + (16/5)^2 = 36/25 + 256/25 = 292/25 \ne 1\): the zoom throws the curve off itself — a circle is not its own asymptotic geometry, which is the whole reason its first-order shape is a parabola instead. (c) At \((\varepsilon, u) = (1/2, 1)\): \(r = 1/4\), lower wall \(1/(2 - 1/8) = 8/15\), upper wall \(1/(2 - 1/4) = 4/7\), width \(4/7 - 8/15 = 4/105\); and \(v = 2/(2 + \sqrt{3})\) sits inside, since \(2/(2+\sqrt{3}) \ge 8/15\) squares to \(196 \ge 192\) and \(2/(2+\sqrt{3}) \le 4/7\) squares to \(36 \le 48\). At \((1/4, 1)\): \(r = 1/16\), lower wall \(1/(2 - 1/32) = 32/63\), upper wall \(1/(2 - 1/16) = 16/31\), width \(16/31 - 32/63 = 16/1953\): the bracket tightened by a factor of \(1953/420 \approx 4.65\) — a shade past the factor \(4\) by which \(r = \varepsilon^2 u^2\) itself shrank: the bracket closes at the \(r\)-rate while the true gap closes at the \(\varepsilon^4\)-rate, the squeeze honest but one order coarser than the thing it brackets.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The \(3/4\) cusp. The curve \(y^3 = x^4 + x^5\) has a singularity at the origin that is neither the node's \((1,1)\) nor the cusp's \((2,3)\). (a) Zoom isotropically, \(x = \varepsilon u, y = \varepsilon v\): derive the zoomed equation, and show that for \(|u| \le 1\) the picture collapses — \(|v|^3 \le 2\varepsilon\), everything grinding to the \(u\)-axis. (b) Find the dominant balance by the weight table: demand the fewest surviving terms and solve for the weights \((a, b)\) with \(x = \varepsilon^a u, y = \varepsilon^b v\). (c) Verify your balance: substitute and derive the zoomed family, checking that two terms survive with no \(\varepsilon\) on either, and name the correction's coefficient.

Hint

For (b), write the three term-weights — \(3b\) for \(y^3\), \(4a\) for \(x^4\), \(5a\) for \(x^5\) — and demand the minimum be attained by exactly two of them; the smallest integer solution is the balance.

Worked answer

(a) Substituting: \(\varepsilon^3 v^3 = \varepsilon^4 u^4 + \varepsilon^5 u^5\), so \(v^3 = \varepsilon u^4 (1 + \varepsilon u)\); for \(|u| \le 1\) and \(\varepsilon \le 1\), \(|v|^3 = \varepsilon |u|^4 |1 + \varepsilon u| \le \varepsilon \cdot 1 \cdot (1 + \varepsilon) \le 2\varepsilon\) — the \(y\)-axis collapses, the isotropic habit failing exactly as it failed for the cusp. (b) Term-weights \(3b, 4a, 5a\); the minimum must be shared: \(3b = 4a\) with \(5a\) strictly above, and the smallest integers are \(a = 3, b = 4\) (then \(3b = 12 = 4a < 15 = 5a\) ✓). (c) With \(x = \varepsilon^3 u, y = \varepsilon^4 v\): \(\varepsilon^{12} v^3 = \varepsilon^{12} u^4 + \varepsilon^{15} u^5\), so \(v^3 = u^4 + \varepsilon^3 u^5\) — two terms survive bare, and the correction carries \(\varepsilon^3\), not \(\varepsilon\): the balance is \((3, 4)\), the initial form \(v^3 = u^4\) a cusp-of-cusps, and the family's first correction is honest at cube order. The weights \((3,4)\) are the fingerprint — no other zoom leaves two terms standing.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The tacnode's two parabolas. The curve \(y^2 = x^4 + x^5 = x^4(1 + x)\) kisses itself at the origin: two branches, both tangent to the \(x\)-axis. (a) Verify the parametrization \(x = s^2 - 1\), \(y = s(s^2 - 1)^2\) exactly: show \(y^2 = x^4(1+x)\) as an identity in \(s\). (b) Compute \(y/x^2\) and explain what its values at \(s = 1 \pm 1/4\), \(1 \pm 1/8\), \(-1 \pm 1/8\) say about the two branches. (c) Find the balance and the initial form: substitute \(x = \varepsilon^a u, y = \varepsilon^b v\), solve the weight table for the two-term balance, and factor the initial form — what are the two parabolas, and which balance ray does the curve own?

Hint

For (a), \(1 + x = s^2\). For (b), \(y/x^2\) simplifies to a single letter. For (c), the term-weights are \(2b, 4a, 5a\); factor \(v^2 - u^4\) over the reals.

Worked answer

(a) \(y^2 = s^2 (s^2 - 1)^4\) and \(x^4 (1 + x) = (s^2 - 1)^4 \cdot s^2\) — the same product in both orders, an exact identity for every \(s\). (b) \(y/x^2 = s(s^2-1)^2/(s^2-1)^2 = s\): at \(s = 5/4, 3/4, 9/8, 7/8, -9/8, -7/8\) the ratio is exactly those values, and as \(s \to 1\) it tends to \(1\), as \(s \to -1\) to \(-1\) — the two branches approach the parabolas \(y = \pm x^2\), while \(y/x \to 0\) at both ends: everything tangent to the \(x\)-axis, the kiss. (c) Term-weights \(2b, 4a, 5a\); the two-term balance demands \(2b = 4a < 5a\), smallest integers \((a, b) = (1, 2)\). Substituting \(x = \varepsilon u, y = \varepsilon^2 v\): \(\varepsilon^4 v^2 = \varepsilon^4 u^4 + \varepsilon^5 u^5\), so \(v^2 = u^4 (1 + \varepsilon u)\) — the balance ray \((1, 2)\), the initial form \(v^2 - u^4 = (v - u^2)(v + u^2)\): two parabolas \(y = \pm x^2\), tangent at the origin, the tacnode's whole geometry visible in one factored line. The node owned \((1,1)\), the cusp \((2,3)\), and the tacnode \((1,2)\) — three curves, three rays, no overlap: the balance is the fingerprint.

Exercise 4 ◆ — The jump that hides a layer, no safety net. For \(\varepsilon > 0\), let \(f_\varepsilon(x) = x/(x + \varepsilon)\) on \(x \ge 0\). Three students, three claims; diagnose each — true, false, or true only under a condition — and cite the exact audit that settles it. (a) "As \(\varepsilon \to 0\), \(f_\varepsilon(x) \to 1\) for every \(x > 0\), so nothing interesting happens near the origin." (b) "The transition from near-\(0\) to near-\(1\) happens on an interval of width about \(\varepsilon\)." (c) "The shape of the transition is universal — every \(\varepsilon\) sees the same profile."

Worked answer

(a) True pointwise, false as a verdict: for fixed \(x > 0\), \(f_\varepsilon(x) \to 1\) exactly, and \(1 - f_\varepsilon(1) = \varepsilon/(1 + \varepsilon)\) reads \(1/3, 1/5, 1/11\) at \(\varepsilon = 1/2, 1/4, 1/10\) — the outside settles. But \(f_\varepsilon(0) = 0\) for every \(\varepsilon\), so the limit function has a jump at the origin and no \(x > 0\) witnesses it: the interesting object is invisible to pointwise limits, and "nothing interesting" is exactly backwards — the interesting thing is the layer the limit deletes. (b) True, with receipts: \(f_\varepsilon(\varepsilon) = 1/2\) for every \(\varepsilon\) — the half-height point sits at exactly \(\varepsilon\), not "about" — and \(f_\varepsilon(2\varepsilon) = 2/3\) for every \(\varepsilon\): the rise lives on a window that scales with \(\varepsilon\), shrinking to nothing in \(x\)-units and staying put in \(\varepsilon\)-units. (c) True, and exact rather than approximate: the zoom \(x = \varepsilon u\) gives \(f_\varepsilon(\varepsilon u) = \varepsilon u / (\varepsilon u + \varepsilon) = u/(u + 1)\) — the \(\varepsilon\) cancels completely, at every \(\varepsilon\), the way the cusp's did. The profile \(u/(u+1)\) is the curve's asymptotic geometry: a fixed point of the rescaling, the honest replacement for the jump the limit invented.

Exercise 5 ◐ — The \(r\)-dial, explored. Replace the Cantor operator's \(1/3\) by a parameter \(r \in (0, 1)\): \(R_r(S) = rS \cup (rS + 1 - r)\), starting from \([0,1]\). (a) Run the standard dial \(r = 1/3\) to level \(3\): list the level's intervals and their total length, and verify \(1/4\) belongs to every level by both routes — the interval census and the ternary-digit census. (b) Turn the dial to \(r = 2/5\): compute the first two levels, and show the gap \((2/5, 3/5)\) never refills — so \(1/2\) is outside every level, forever. (c) Turn the dial to \(r = 1/2\): show the collapse \([0,1] \to [0,1]\) happens in exactly one step, locate the exact transition point of the dial, and say what breaks in the contraction argument (P22's) as \(r\) crosses it.

Hint

For (a), the level-\(N\) intervals all have length \(3^{-N}\) and start at the ternary words in \(0\)s and \(2\)s; \(1/4 = 0.0202\ldots_3\). For (b), compute \(r \cdot L_1\) and \(r \cdot L_1 + 3/5\) as intervals, and check nesting: \(L_{N+1} \subseteq L_N\). For (c), the union of the two copies is the whole interval exactly when the copies overlap.

Worked answer

(a) Level \(3\) of \(R_{1/3}\): eight intervals of length \(1/27\), starts at \(\{0, 2, 6, 8, 18, 20, 24, 26\}/27\), total length \(8/27 = (2/3)^3\) ✓. Interval route: \(1/4 = 6.75/27 \in [6/27, 7/27]\) ✓ — it lies in the interval whose ternary word is \(020\). Digit route: \(1/4 = 0.020202\ldots_3\) — the series \(2 \cdot (1/9)/(1 - 1/9) = (2/9)/(8/9) = 1/4\) exactly — digits only \(0\) and \(2\), so membership at every level, two censuses agreeing. (b) \(L_1 = [0, 2/5] \cup [3/5, 1]\); \(L_2 = r L_1 \cup (r L_1 + 3/5) = [0, 4/25] \cup [6/25, 2/5] \cup [3/5, 19/25] \cup [21/25, 1]\). The gap \((2/5, 3/5)\) of \(L_1\) contains no start or end of any later level — the levels nest, \(L_{N+1} \subseteq L_N\), because each level is \(r L_N \cup (r L_N + 1 - r)\) built from the one before — and \(1/2 \in (2/5, 3/5)\) is outside \(L_2\)'s intervals \([6/25, 2/5]\) (it ends at \(2/5\)) and \([3/5, 19/25]\) (it starts at \(3/5\)): outside forever, the dust thinner than the standard. (c) \(R_{1/2}([0,1]) = [0, 1/2] \cup [1/2, 1] = [0,1]\): one step, the copies touching at \(1/2\), the fixed point reached immediately. The transition sits at exactly \(r = 1/2\): the union is the whole interval exactly when \(1 - r \le r\), and for every \(r < 1/2\) the middle gap is born at step one and nesting keeps it forever. What breaks at the crossing: for \(r < 1/2\) the two copies of \(S\) are disjoint and every word \(I \in \{0,1\}^N\) names a distinct interval — the map is a contraction on compact sets, P22's unique fixed point. At \(r = 1/2\) the words start collapsing onto the same points (the address \(0.1\) and the address \(0.0111\ldots\) name the same point), and for \(r > 1/2\) the copies overlap outright: double-counting, no contraction constant below \(1\), and uniqueness of the fixed point is no longer free. The open end: the dust's dimension \(\log 2 / \log(1/r)\) is a named door this book has not built, and proving the \(r > 1/2\) attractor is \([0,1]\) — and unique among compact invariant sets — is the census this page deliberately leaves as a door.
