The Patterns Atlas · P04 · the fourth move of the spine

Complete the square

A quadratic is a perfect square with one corner missing. Pay for the corner honestly — and the leftover number answers your question.

New word: vertex form. You have completed squares in school — the algorithm is class-10 work. What school rarely names is the destination: writing a quadratic as \(a(x - h)^2 + k\), a square plus a plain number. That shape is called the vertex form, because it displays the vertex (h, k) instead of hiding it. This page is not about the mechanics you already own. It is about the move: recognizing, mid-problem, that the square is hiding there and that paying for its corner will answer the question.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far has been a course in honesty. P01 pinned claims down, P02 built conjectures from small cases, P03 attacked them with hostile configurations. All three are epistemics: moves about what to believe and when.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P02 and P03 keep ending at the same wall. The table shows the minimum of some quadratic — but tables detect, they never prove. The stress test kills a false formula — but the repair needs "a better form of the expression", and nobody said where better forms come from. Every honest method in your kit eventually asks you to change the shape of an algebraic expression on purpose — and nothing so far taught you how. This page is the first constructive move of the spine: not testing claims, but building the form a claim needs.

2 · Meet the problem

Two problems, both about the same ten. First: is \(x^2 + 10x + 41\) ever equal to zero, for any real \(x\)? Your factoring toolkit goes hunting and returns empty-handed — and that is not bad luck, it is structure: this quadratic has no roots at all, so no factorization will ever be found. Second, and older: solve \(x^2 + 10x = 39\). This is al-Khwārizmī's own problem, stated around the year 820 as "a square and ten roots are equal to thirty-nine dirhams" — and note that trial factoring does work here (\(39 = 3 \times 13\), and \(x^2 + 10x - 39 = (x + 13)(x - 3)\)), but only if you think to try. Change 39 to 38 and the hunt finds nothing forever; the equation is not obliged to factor nicely.

Now watch the move that solves both, from a geometry problem al-Khwārizmī literally drew. Take the square \(x^2\) and the ten roots as two 5-by-\(x\) rectangles glued to its east and north edges. The assembled gnomon is one corner short of a bigger square — the missing corner is \(5 \times 5 = 25\). So: pay for the corner. In the equation, add 25 to both sides (a square's worth of balance):

\[x^2 + 10x + 25 = 39 + 25, \quad\text{so}\quad (x + 5)^2 = 64,\]

and the square root hands over the answer at sight: \(x + 5 = 8\), so \(x = 3\). The modern number line adds the second sign, \(x + 5 = -8\), so \(x = -13\) — check both: \(9 + 30 = 39\) and \(169 - 130 = 39\). (Al-Khwārizmī kept only the 3; negatives were not yet numbers to him. The mathematics did not change — the address space did.)

And the first problem? Same corner, paid differently: not across an equals sign (there is none to balance), but out of your own pocket — add 25 and subtract 25 in the same breath, changing nothing but the shape:

\[x^2 + 10x + 41 = (x^2 + 10x + 25) + 41 - 25 = (x + 5)^2 + 16.\]

A square plus 16. Squares are never negative, so the whole expression is always at least 16, and it equals 16 exactly at \(x = -5\). Done — not detected, proved. Your P02 table (the values dip to 16 at \(x = -5\) and climb on both sides) was right, and now it is certified. One move, two payments, two kills: an equation solved and a proof manufactured. It has a name.

3 · Name the move

Pattern P04 — Complete the square. Given an expression that is quadratic-like — a squared thing, plus a multiple of the thing, plus the rest — rebuild it as (thing + half the middle)² plus a plain leftover number. In an equation, keep the balance: whatever you add to one side, add to the other. In a bare expression, add and subtract the same quantity: the value never changes, only the shape. Then read off what the new shape knows: the vertex, the minimum, the roots' existence, the distance.

Or, in words: a quadratic is a perfect square with its corner missing. Half the middle term tells you exactly how big the corner is. Pay for it honestly — both sides of an equation, or plus-and-minus inside an expression — and the square tells you the truth the scattered form was hiding.

Box P04.A — the recipe, both payments. For \(x^2 + bx + c\), the corner is \((b/2)^2\) and the leftover is \(c - b^2/4\):

  • Equation \(x^2 + bx = d\): add \((b/2)^2\) to both sides, then take roots — remembering both signs (P03's boundary discipline, applied to the \(\pm\)).
  • Expression \(x^2 + bx + c\): add and subtract \((b/2)^2\): you get \((x + b/2)^2 + (c - b^2/4)\). Nothing was added overall — this is why the move is safe inside proofs.
  • Leading coefficient \(a \ne 1\): factor \(a\) out of the first two terms first (Example 1 does this at full scale), or divide the equation by \(a\).
  • The doubled form, for integer hygiene: multiply the monic identity by 4 and everything fractional disappears: \(4(x^2 + bx + c) = (2x + b)^2 + 4c - b^2\).

Box P04.B — when to reach for it, and the honest boundary. Three-key diagnosis: the claim type is "quadratic-like" — any expression with a squared thing, a multiple of that thing, and leftovers; "quadratic-like" is broader than "quadratic" (Exercise 5 squares \(x^2\) itself). The problem shape is invisible structure: a vertex or minimum you must locate or prove, a curve you must identify, a positivity claim, a factorization over the reals, an integral with a quadratic denominator. The tools: half the middle term, and the willingness to pay for a corner.

The boundary, named honestly: the move's full signature is bigger than this page — "add a carefully-chosen term or parameter so that one side becomes a perfect square." Cardano depresses the cubic with a substitution; Ferrari completes the square with a parameter y chosen so the quartic collapses, and y is then found from a resolvent equation; Lagrange subtracts a linear term to force equal endpoints before invoking Rolle. That generalization — choosing the corner's size on purpose, as a variable — belongs to P05 and beyond. This page runs the fixed-corner core: half the middle term, no parameters.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, one per domain. In each, the square is paid for early and the leftover does the answering.

Example 1 (algebra) — the quadratic formula, derived, not memorized

Solve \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) once and for all, with \(a \ne 0\). Divide by \(a\) (legal since \(a \ne 0\) — P01 would make you say why): \(x^2 + (b/a)x + c/a = 0\). Now the middle coefficient is \(b/a\), so the corner is \((b/2a)^2\). Pay it across the equation:

\[x^2 + (b/a)x = -c/a \quad\to\quad (x + b/2a)^2 = (b/2a)^2 - c/a = (b^2 - 4ac)/4a^2.\]

Take roots, both signs: \(x + b/2a = \pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}/2a\), so \(x = (-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac})/2a\). Look at what the move produced: the discriminant \(b^2 - 4ac\) was not memorized into the formula — it emerged as the leftover's numerator. Its sign is now a visible question about the leftover: positive leftover, two real roots; zero, one; negative, none (the square cannot equal a negative number — the move fails to produce roots precisely when it is proving there are none). Moves used: P01 (the division by \(a\), and what "solve" demands), P04.

Example 2 (numbers) — a Diophantine equation tamed

Find all integer solutions of \(x^2 + 6x = y^2 + 4y\). Two variables, no obvious foothold — but each side is quadratic-like in its own letter. Complete both squares: \(x^2 + 6x = (x + 3)^2 - 9\) and \(y^2 + 4y = (y + 2)^2 - 4\), so the equation says

\[(x + 3)^2 - 9 = (y + 2)^2 - 4, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad (x + 3)^2 - (y + 2)^2 = 5.\]

Now the new shape knows something the old one hid: a difference of two squares factors. \((x + 3)^2 - (y + 2)^2 = (x - y + 1)(x + y + 5) = 5\), and since \(x\), \(y\) are integers, both factors are integers multiplying to 5 — so they must be a factor pair of 5: (1, 5), (5, 1), (−1, −5), (−5, −1). Each pair is a 2×2 linear system; solving gives exactly four integer solutions: (0, 0), (0, −4), (−6, −4), (−6, 0). Note the honest division of labour: the scan is a detector (P02's honesty applies to machines too); the factor-pair route is the proof that no fifth solution hides outside any box. Moves used: P04, then the difference-of-squares factorization (class-12 algebra), with P02's detector-honesty discipline.

Example 3 (geometry) — the circle that was wearing a disguise

Identify the curve \(x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0\). Two letters, so the move runs twice, once per letter: \(x^2 - 6x = (x - 3)^2 - 9\) and \(y^2 + 4y = (y + 2)^2 - 4\). Substitute:

\[(x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 - 9 - 4 - 12 = 0, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25.\]

That is the CBSE class-11 definition of a circle, center (3, −2), radius 5 — the equation never mentioned either. Beware the near-miss the disguise invites: (3, 2) uses the right numbers with the wrong signs — it satisfies nothing (the original gives −9 there), because the y-corner belongs to \((y + 2)^2\), not \((y - 2)^2\). Moves used: P04, twice; P03's near-miss discipline on the sign of the corner.

5 · When the move fails (or seems to)

The move is small and honest, and its failures are instructive enough to catalog.

  • The cross term is not a stop sign — but it is a boundary. Given \(x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2\), completing in \(x\) with \(y\) held as a parameter still works: \((x + 2y)^2 + y^2\), a sum of two squares, never negative — a genuinely useful proof). What the fixed corner cannot do is classify two-variable quadratics: telling an ellipse from a hyperbola requires rotating axes, and that is change-of-basis machinery — P05's territory, honestly deferred.
  • The negative square is a success wearing a failure costume. Complete the square on \(x^2 + 4x + 13 = 0\): \((x + 2)^2 = -9\). The move "fails" to produce roots — because it has just proved there are none over the reals. When the leftover turns negative at root-taking time, read it as a verdict, not an obstruction. (Example 2's Diophantine, by contrast, was saved by exactly this shape: two squares with a small gap between them.)
  • Cubics and quartics: out of this page's jurisdiction. \(x^3 + 3x + 1\) is not quadratic-like in any substitution of this page's kind. The parameter generalization (Box P04.B) does reach the cubic and quartic — Cardano and Ferrari built it in the 1540s — but it is a different move with a different price, and this page will not pretend otherwise.
  • Overkill has a price, and factoring has a lane. For \(x^2 + 10x - 39 = 0\), the factoring route \((x + 13)(x - 3)\) is faster if you see the pair. Mitigation: reach for P04 first when the question is about shape — vertex, minimum, positivity, identification — where factoring is silent, or when the factoring hunt fails. Price: the corner costs fractions when \(b\) is odd; the doubled form of Box P04.A removes them at the cost of larger numbers. Default: roots by factoring when the integers cooperate, everything else by the corner.
Scope note. This page owns the fixed-corner quadratic core: one square at a time, corner = half the middle term, in equations and expressions, including the doubled integer form. The parameter version (choosing the corner's size as a variable), the two-variable classification, and sums of many squares are real mathematics with their own pages ahead (P05, P06, and the bridge track). Nothing here should be stretched beyond its proof.

7 · Lineage interlude

History, lightly. The move is older than algebra. Babylonian scribes around 1800 BCE solved problems like "one number exceeds another by 7; their product is 60" (\(x - y = 7,\ xy = 60,\ \text{so } x^2 - 7x = 60\)) by the same corner — and their integer trick survives in this page's Exercise 4: double everything first, \((2x - 7)^2 = 289 = 17^2\), so \(x = 12\) (the tablet's answer, \(y = 5\)) — and, on the modern number line, also \(x = -5\), \(y = -12\). Al-Khwārizmī (c. 820) organized the move geometrically — the missing corner of this page's encounter is his drawing — and needed six separate case-forms because he had no negative numbers to unify them. The parameter generalization arrived with Cardano's Ars Magna (1545) and Ferrari's resolvent, and a modern cameo: Lagrange's trick of subtracting a linear term to force equal endpoints before invoking Rolle's theorem is completing the square on the hypothesis itself. None of this is load-bearing; it says the corner you just paid for has been worth paying for for four thousand years.

8 · Exercises

Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis is the exercise.

Exercise 1 ●○○

Write \(x^2 - 8x + 3\) in vertex form, and state its minimum value and where it is attained. Then say, in one sentence, why the minimum is now proved rather than merely tabled.

Hint

Half of \(-8\) is \(-4\), and the corner is \((-4)^2\).

Worked answer

Add and subtract 16: \(x^2 - 8x + 3 = (x - 4)^2 - 16 + 3 = (x - 4)^2 - 13\). The square is never negative, so the expression is at least −13, with equality exactly at \(x = 4\) — proved, because the add-and-subtract payment changed nothing about the value, only the shape.

Exercise 2 ●●○

Write \(2x^2 + 12x + 7\) in vertex form \(a(x - h)^2 + k\), and state its minimum. Why must the leading coefficient come out before the corner is paid?

Hint

Factor 2 out of the first two terms only: \(2(x^2 + 6x) + 7\) — now complete inside the parentheses.

Worked answer

\(2(x^2 + 6x) + 7 = 2((x + 3)^2 - 9) + 7 = 2(x + 3)^2 - 11\). Minimum −11 at \(x = -3\). The coefficient must come out first because the middle term of the whole expression is \(12x\) only when read through the factor 2: the corner inside the parentheses is half of \(6\), not half of \(12\). Paying the corner before factoring \(a\) out is the classic slip — it buys a corner of the wrong size.

Exercise 3 ●●○

Compute \(\int dx/(x^2 + 4x + 13)\). Name the class-12 template the completed form unlocks, and verify your answer by differentiating it.

Hint

\(x^2 + 4x + 13 = (x + 2)^2 + 9\) — a sum, not a difference, so the arctan template (not the log template) applies: \(\int du/(u^2 + a^2) = (1/a)\arctan(u/a) + C\).

Worked answer

With \(u = x + 2\) and \(a = 3\): \(\int dx/((x + 2)^2 + 9) = (1/3)\arctan((x + 2)/3) + C\). The completing-the-square step is the entire difficulty; the template is stock.

Exercise 4 ●●●

Find all integer solutions of \(x^2 + 14x = y^2 + 6y + 1\) — by the route of Example 2, and then say why your route proves there are no others (a machine scan cannot).

Hint

Complete both squares; the difference of squares will factor into two integers whose product is prime.

Worked answer

\((x + 7)^2 - 49 = (y + 3)^2 - 8\), so \((x + 7)^2 - (y + 3)^2 = 41\), so \((x - y + 4)(x + y + 10) = 41\). Since 41 is prime, the factor pairs are exactly (1, 41), (41, 1), (−1, −41), (−41, −1) — four 2×2 systems, four solutions: (14, 17), (14, −23), (−28, −23), (−28, 17). Why the scan cannot certify: it visits finitely many points; the factor-pair route visits all of them, because any solution whatsoever must reduce to one of the four factor pairs of 41. Finiteness of the factor pairs of a prime is doing the real work — P02's honesty rule, sharpened into a proof.

Exercise 5 ◆ Diagnose the move

Prove that \(x^4 - 4x^2 + 4\) is never negative for any real \(x\) — and name the pattern from the shelf that does the job, including the substitution that makes it legal. No hint: the diagnosis is the exercise.

Worked answer

The expression is not a quadratic in \(x\) — but Box P04.B's claim type is "quadratic-like", and that is the diagnosis: with \(u = x^2\), the expression is \(u^2 - 4u + 4\), quadratic in \(u\). Complete the corner: \((u - 2)^2\), so \(x^4 - 4x^2 + 4 = (x^2 - 2)^2\), a square, hence never negative. One honest wrinkle the grid teaches: over integers the minimum shown is 1 (at \(x = \pm 1\)), but the true minimum 0 is attained at \(x = \pm\sqrt{2}\) — outside the grid. Detectors detect only where they look; proofs (the square argument) see all of \(\mathbb{R}\) at once. The move: P04, with the substitution reading "quadratic-like" generously.

Exercise 6 ◐ The leftover and the discriminant

Complete the square on \(x^2 + bx + c\) and study the leftover \(c - b^2/4\). (a) How does the leftover's sign decide how many real roots the equation \(x^2 + bx + c = 0\) has? (b) For the monic case, find the exact relation between the leftover and the discriminant \(b^2 - 4c\) — then explain why Example 1's formula "emerges" from the corner the way it did. (c) Test your rule on a table of your own making, and say precisely what the table can and cannot confirm. No single answer is expected.
