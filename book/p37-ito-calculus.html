The Patterns Atlas · P37 · the eighth move of tier 4

Itô calculus

The chain rule assumes the step shrinks faster than its square. A coin-toss path breaks that assumption on every step — and the rule survives anyway, with its missing half restored: keep the book of squared steps, charge the second-order term honestly, and randomness itself supplies the clock.

New word: the stochastic differential. Four ideas stand at the door, each an extension of something this book already built. (1) The sentence the physics obeys. P26 taught that a rate is a sentence: \(dS = kS\,dt\) says the change is proportional to the present. But the driver on the right can be a coin — P19's census coin, not a smooth curve. (2) The census, not the path. With \(2^N\) possible paths, no single one is the answer; the honest objects are the books every path keeps, and the averages over the census of paths. (3) The two walls. P15's instrument: trap the answer between two things you can compute — here, the smooth path's dying book and the rough path's persistent one. (4) Taylor's second order, kept honest. Smooth calculus discards \((\Delta x)^2\) because it dies faster than \(\Delta x\); on a coin-toss path it does not die at all — it is the clock. This page's trade is the chain rule, re-audited for a driver that is a coin.

1 · Before we begin

Three doors lead here, and two of them said what this page would have to do. P26 closed its ledger with the commission: "The pattern's own sequel, twice over: P37 — Itô calculus — what happens to Example 3's walk when the steps shrink but the noise never does, the calculus of a world that vibrates as it moves — and P43 — Flow with surgery, where the heat equation is run not on a rod but on the shape of space itself, smoothing geometry the way it smooths temperature." And P26's third exercise, having audited a biased walk to its honest numbers — a center moving at \(0.2n\) under a spread of \(\sqrt{0.96\,n}\) — closed its worked answer: "drift and diffusion, the two terms of the first stochastic differential equation you will meet at P37." P19 named this door from its own side: "The far doors: P37, which differentiates in the presence of randomness — this page's coin given a calculus." P15 made no commission; its instrument is §2's — the two walls between which the whole discovery is trapped. This page is the errand those doors describe: the chain rule, re-audited for a driver that is a coin.

Here is the tension this page resolves. The chain rule is the oldest bookkeeping of rates: \(d(x^2) = 2x\,dx\), \(d(e^x) = e^x\,dx\) — and the license for all of it is quiet and absolute: the step \(dx\) is so small that \((dx)^2\) is nothing. A coin-toss path breaks the license on every step. Its steps are square-root sized — \(\Delta x = \pm 1/\sqrt{N}\) — so \((\Delta x)^2\) is first-order, and the discarded term is not a rounding error but a clock that ticks once per unit time, deterministically, on every path, out of pure chance. The naive chain rule on such a path is not merely wrong: it is wrong by a computable constant, the same constant every time — and the entire calculus of the research frontier, option prices to exit times, hangs on reading that constant correctly.

2 · Meet the problem

Build the driver honestly, as a census object — P19's coin, given a clock. One unit of time, cut into \(N\) steps; before each step a fair coin is tossed; the position \(x\) moves by \(\Delta x = \pm 1/\sqrt{N}\), heads up, tails down. The scaling is not decoration: after \(N\) steps the position has variance \(N \cdot \tfrac{1}{N} = 1\) — one unit of spread per unit of time, at every refinement. Call the limit path \(W\), the walk. It is not a curve. It is a census: \(2^N\) paths at refinement \(N\), and not one of them is the answer; the honest objects are the books every path keeps and the averages over the census.

Run the two censuses. The first book: squared steps. Take the smooth comparison path \(x(t) = t\), cut into \(N\) steps of size \(1/N\), and sum the squares: \(\sum (\Delta x)^2 = N \cdot \tfrac{1}{N^2} = \tfrac{1}{N}\). At \(N = 2, 4, 8, 16\): \(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{16}\) — a book that dies. Now the walk: every step squares to exactly \(\tfrac{1}{N}\), heads or tails, no exceptions, so \(\sum (\Delta x)^2 = N \cdot \tfrac{1}{N} = 1\) — at every refinement, on every one of the \(2^N\) paths. Two walls, P15's instrument fully loaded: the smooth book pinned at \(0\), the rough book pinned at \(1\), and the gap between them a number, not a mood. This is the discovery, before it has a name: chance supplies a deterministic clock.

The second book: unsigned steps. The same comparison kills the classical length. Smooth: \(\sum |\Delta x| = N \cdot \tfrac{1}{N} = 1\), finite, at every refinement. Walk: \(\sum |\Delta x| = N \cdot \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{N}} = \sqrt{N}\) — at \(N = 4, 16, 64\): \(2, 4, 8\) — a book that explodes. The walk has no length at all: it is too rough to be rectifiable, and no amount of refinement repairs it. The first-order book is the broken one; the second-order book is the honest one.

Now the failure, computed exactly. Audit the parabola \(x^2\) along the walk at \(N = 4\), by telescoping — every identity below holds path by path, no probabilities anywhere:

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{3}\bigl((x_k + \Delta x_k)^2 - x_k^2\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{3} 2x_k\,\Delta x_k \;+\; \sum_{k=0}^{3}(\Delta x_k)^2 \;=\; 2\sum_{k=0}^{3}x_k\,\Delta x_k \;+\; 1. \]

The first sum is the discrete book that the chain rule would call "\(2\int x\,dx\)" — the rectangle-sum of \(2x\) against the steps. The second sum is not noise: heads, tails, any sequence at all — it is exactly \(1\). Telescoped across the whole interval, the walk's final square obeys \(x^2_{\text{final}} - x^2_{\text{start}} = 2\text{(the discrete integral)} + 1\). The classical chain rule — which stops at the first sum — is off by exactly the squared-step book, and the book says \(1\) per unit time, every time. On the smooth path the same telescoping leaves the leftover \(\tfrac{1}{N} \to 0\), and the chain rule is vindicated. One rule, two rooms: the difference between them is a single honest term (Fig P37.1).

The tension, stated for the audit: the chain rule undercounts on rough paths by a computable, deterministic amount; the undercount is exactly the squared-step clock; and the clock is visible only if the second-order Taylor term is kept, summed, and read as a first-class book. What the next section names is the rule that keeps all of this honestly.

3 · Name the move

The move is Taylor's expansion, kept to second order, summed over the walk's steps, and read with the multiplication book of the steps. For a smooth function \(f\) and the walk \(W\):

Itô's rule — the chain rule with its second-order book kept honest. For \(f\) twice continuously differentiable, \[ f(W_t) - f(W_0) \;=\; \int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t f''(W_s)\,ds. \] The first term is the chain rule's own, evaluated left-endpoint and summed against the steps; the second is the audit's leftover — the squared steps, converted at a fixed rate of one unit of \(ds\) per unit of squared step.

In words: differentiate as usual, then pay the second-order tax. The roughness of the path promotes \(f''\) from a negligible spectator into a first-class term; the exchange rate is fixed — \(dW \cdot dW = dt\) — and it is the only number the noise itself supplies. The correction is deterministic: the census of paths averages the noise away, and the leftover book reads the same on every path.

The audit, once, by hand. Taylor to second order at each step: \(\Delta f = f'(W_k)\,\Delta W_k + \tfrac{1}{2}f''(W_k)\,(\Delta W_k)^2 + \ldots\) — and here is where the walk differs from every path classical calculus ever met: the dotted remainder dies not because \(\Delta W\) is small, but because the walk's own census kills it — \((\Delta W)^3 = \pm N^{-3/2}\) per step, so \(\sum |(\Delta W)^3| \le N \cdot N^{-3/2} = N^{-1/2} \to 0\). Sum the kept terms: the first book is the definition of the integral; the second book, by §2's census, is \(\sum \tfrac{1}{2}f''(W_k) \cdot \tfrac{1}{N}\) — a Riemann sum for \(\tfrac{1}{2}\int f''(W_s)\,ds\). The entire content of the rule is the multiplication book of the steps: \[ dW \cdot dW \;=\; dt, \qquad dW \cdot dt \;=\; 0, \qquad dt \cdot dt \;=\; 0. \] Smooth calculus is the special case where every product dies; the walk is the case where one product survives, and it survives as the clock. The construction of the integral itself — the limit of the left-endpoint sums in the mean-square census — is a named door: §4's exemplars run it only at fixed \(N\), by exact arithmetic, and §6's list says where the limit is built.

At full scale the driver carries drift as well as noise. Let \(X\) obey \(dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW\) — P26's sentence with both books on the right — and let \(f(t, x)\) be once differentiable in \(t\) and twice in \(x\). The same audit, run with the multiplication-table entry \((dX)^2 = \sigma^2\,dt\), reads: \[ df(t, X_t) \;=\; \Bigl(f_t + \mu\, f_x + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\, f_{xx}\Bigr)\,dt \;+\; \sigma\, f_x\, dW. \] Three books where classical calculus keeps two: the drift \(f_t + \mu f_x\), the noise \(\sigma f_x\,dW\), and this page's own, the correction \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f_{xx}\,dt\). The bracketed drift closes a door of its own: set it to zero and every census average \(\mathbb{E}[g(X_T)]\) becomes the solution of a deterministic heat-type equation — the generator bridge, a named door on §6's list. And the correction is not algebra's accident but curvature's fee: a fair coin kicks \(X\) symmetrically to \(X \pm \sigma\sqrt{dt}\), and on a convex \(f\) the average of the two landed values sits above \(f(X)\) by exactly the sag of the chord against the tangent — \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''(X)\,(\sigma\sqrt{dt})^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f''(X)\,dt\) (Fig P37.2).

4 · The move at work

Exemplar 1 · The founding reconciliation — \(W^2\), audited three ways

Take \(f(x) = x^2\), so \(f' = 2x\) and \(f'' = 2\), and the rule reads: \[ W_t^2 \;=\; 2\int_0^t W_s\,dW_s \;+\; t. \] The \(+t\) is §2's leftover, now with a name and a formula. Audit it three ways, honestly. (a) Path by path: at \(N = 4\), §2's telescoping is the identity — no probability entered, and the leftover book summed to exactly \(1 = t\), on each of the \(16\) paths separately. (b) Census: take expectations. The integral term is a left-endpoint sum against fair steps: each \(W_k \,\Delta W_k\) has mean zero, because the step is fair and the position is already written when the coin falls — a book that consults no future. So \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = t\), which is the walk's own variance census: both sides agree, and the \(+t\) is exactly what the naive chain rule would have undercounted. (c) At every refinement: the leftover book is \(\sum (\Delta W)^2 = t\) at every \(N\), on every path — the correction is not a limit that happens to converge; it is constant. Three censuses, one number: the missing half of the chain rule is \(t\), and it was missing in plain sight.

Exemplar 2 · The volatility drag — the correction as a market force

Money under noise: a price \(S\) with proportional drift \(\mu\) and proportional noise \(\sigma\), modeled as \(dS = \mu S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\) — P26's sentence with a coin on the right. The classical instinct takes the logarithm and reports growth at rate \(\mu\). The audit says otherwise. Take \(f = \ln\): \(f' = 1/S\), \(f'' = -1/S^2\), and the rule gives \[ d(\ln S) \;=\; \frac{1}{S}\,dS \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\,dt \;=\; \bigl(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\bigr)\,dt + \sigma\,dW. \] The correction is the drag: the log — the typical path's book — grows not at \(\mu\) but at \(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). Audit it with honest numbers: \(\mu = 8\%\) per year, \(\sigma = 20\%\) per year, so the drag is \(\tfrac{1}{2}(0.20)^2 = 2\%\). The census mean still grows at \(8\%\) — \(\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}\), because the noise term averages out against the drift — but the median grows at \(6\%\): \(S_0 e^{0.06t}\). At \(t = 25\) years the two books read \(e^{2} \approx 7.39\) against \(e^{1.5} \approx 4.48\) — a factor of \(e^{0.5} \approx 1.65\) between mean and median, produced entirely by paths wild enough to carry the average while the typical path lags (Fig P37.3). This is the rule's most consequential receipt: the difference between "the average outcome" and "the typical outcome" is exactly the second-order tax, and every long-horizon forecast that ignores it is wrong by it.

Exemplar 3 · The exit time — a count without a solution

The rule counts, and the count answers questions classical calculus cannot even pose. A particle starts at \(x\) inside the ball of radius \(R\) in \(d\) dimensions and walks until it first exits: how long, on the census average? Apply the rule to \(f(\cdot) = \|\cdot\|^2\): the gradient book is \(2W\), and the second-order term is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d} 2 = d\) — the coordinate second derivatives, summing to a constant, so the correction is \(d \cdot dt\), deterministic: \[ \|W_t\|^2 \;=\; 2\int_0^t W_s \cdot dW_s \;+\; d\,t. \] The integral term is again a fair book — an already-written position dotted against a fresh coin. So \(\|W_t\|^2 - d\,t\) is a book that stays balanced in expectation; evaluate it at the exit time \(\tau\) (the exit is certain and the steps bounded — the fine print is optional stopping's, a named door): \[ \mathbb{E}[\tau] \;=\; \frac{R^2 - \|x\|^2}{d}. \] From the center of the interval \([-3, 3]\) in one dimension: expected exit \((9 - 0)/1 = 9\) — the classical gambler's ruin answer, earned without building a single solution. From the center of a 3-ball of radius \(3\): \(9/3 = 3\). The audit did geometry: the exit time is a distance-squared, and the dimension converts it. A count from books alone — this page's trade, in one line.

Exemplar 4 · The noisy spring — equipartition by two passes

Put P26's cooling mug on a hot plate: a spring pulled toward \(0\) at rate \(\gamma\), kicked by noise \(\sigma\) — the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck sentence \(dX = -\gamma X\,dt + \sigma\,dW\). Two passes of the rule read its whole life. Pass one — the trajectory. \(f(t, x) = e^{\gamma t}x\) is linear in \(x\), so \(f_{xx} = 0\): linear books pay no second-order tax. The rule reads \(d(e^{\gamma t}X_t) = \sigma e^{\gamma t}\,dW_t\), a pure noise book, and integrates to the exact trajectory: \[ X_t \;=\; X_0\,e^{-\gamma t} \;+\; \sigma\int_0^t e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_s. \] Pass two — the energy. Apply the rule to \(x^2\): \(d(X_t^2) = (-2\gamma X_t^2 + \sigma^2)\,dt + 2\sigma X_t\,dW_t\). The noise book is fair — expectations kill it — and the variance \(v(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t^2]\) obeys an ordinary deterministic ODE, P29's trade exactly: a book pumped at \(\sigma^2\) per unit time, drained at rate \(2\gamma\): \[ \dot{v} = -2\gamma\,v + \sigma^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v(t) = X_0^2 e^{-2\gamma t} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\gamma}\bigl(1 - e^{-2\gamma t}\bigr) \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\sigma^2}{2\gamma}. \] The spring does not cool to rest: damping balances the quadratic-variation pump, and the system settles at variance \(\sigma^2/2\gamma\) — thermal equilibrium earned by books; the physicist's equipartition, priced. Audit at fixed step \(\Delta\): the discrete spring \(X_{k+1} = (1 - \gamma\Delta)X_k + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta}\,\xi_k\) has exact steady variance \(v(\Delta) = \sigma^2/(\gamma(2 - \gamma\Delta))\); at \(\gamma = \tfrac{1}{2}\), \(\sigma = 1\), \(\Delta = \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{16}, \tfrac{1}{64}\) the ladder reads \(\tfrac{16}{15}, \tfrac{64}{63}, \tfrac{256}{255}\) — dyadic fractions converging to the equipartition value \(1 = \sigma^2/2\gamma\), the census reading the pump balance at every scale.

5 · When the move fails

Every pattern page owes an honest list of failure modes. The rule fails in three distinct ways, and each has a repair, a price, and a default.

Exhibit A — the census depends on when you read. The rule's integral evaluates \(f'\) at the interval's left end — the position before the coin falls. Evaluate at the midpoint instead and the book changes: the midpoint census differs by a computable drift — the step book, converted at the same exchange rate — not by an error. Both integrals are honest; they answer different questions. The left-endpoint book cannot see the future: it is the only contract that models an agent deciding before the coin falls. The midpoint book is the limit of smooth physical systems vibrating through their steps — a pendulum read at its energy's midpoint. Jam-mode: quoting a number without naming the contract, or averaging the two books as if they agreed.

Mitigation: name the contract before computing — deciding-before-the-coin, or physics-of-the-vibration; the two are different rooms, and the number belongs to its room.

Price: converting between the contracts costs exactly the correction term — the midpoint's book reads as the left-endpoint's plus the step book's conversion — and any drift moves by \(\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma'\) in the conversion.

Default: left-endpoint for prediction and decisions; midpoint when the noise stands in for fast smooth motion. The two never mix inside one computation.

Exhibit B — the kink: \(f''\) refuses to be a function. Take \(f(x) = |x|\): smooth except at \(0\), and there the second derivative is not a value but a mass. Away from \(0\) the rule reads \(d|W| = \operatorname{sgn}(W)\,dW\), a fair book with mean \(0\) — but the census says \(\mathbb{E}|W_t| = \sqrt{2t/\pi} > 0\), so the naive reading undercounts, and the missing mass is concentrated exactly at the kink the rule was never licensed to cross. The honest formula carries a third book — the local time, the clock of the visits to the origin (Tanaka's formula, a named door): \(|W_t| = \int_0^t \operatorname{sgn}(W_s)\,dW_s + L_t^0\), and the census balances: \(\mathbb{E}[L_t^0] = \sqrt{2t/\pi}\). Jam-mode: differentiating through a kink and reporting the fair-book answer as the census.

Mitigation: audit \(f''\) before trusting the rule — if it is not an honest function, the correction term is not \(\tfrac{1}{2}f''\,dt\), and the rule needs the kink's own book.

Price: the correction book becomes a measure — richer than a function, but no longer readable off \(f\) by two derivatives.

Default: smooth \(f\) for the dictionary; kinks route to the local-time door, named in §6.

Exhibit C — jumps break the two-term cutoff. The rule's audit discarded \((\Delta W)^3\) and beyond because the walk's steps shrink like \(N^{-1/2}\). A jump process does not shrink: at a jump the move is order \(1\), every power of it is first-order, and no two-term Taylor book can collect them. The honest calculus for jumps carries a third book with its own census — the jump book and its compensation, a named door — while the two-term rule silently miscounts any \(f\) with curvature, at every jump, forever. Jam-mode: running the smooth rule on data with jumps and blaming the residual on noise.

Mitigation: separate the books before differentiating — continuous part and jump part never share a Taylor expansion.

Price: the integral gains a compensator term; the books no longer close at two terms, and the multiplication table grows entries.

Default: continuous noise for this page's dictionary; jumps are a named door, and §6's list says where their calculus is built.

Scope, honestly. The audited counts on this page run at fixed refinements with exact fractions: the two books of §2 (\(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{16}\) against \(1, 1, 1, 1\)), the unsigned books (\(1\) against \(\sqrt{N} = 2, 4, 8\)), the founding reconciliation \(W_t^2 = 2\int W\,dW + t\) audited at \(N = 4\) path by path, the drag audit at \(\mu = 8\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\) with the \(25\)-year mean \(e^{2}\) and median \(e^{1.5}\), the exit-time count \((R^2 - \|x\|^2)/d\) checked at \(9\) and \(3\), the noisy spring's dyadic variance ladder \(\tfrac{16}{15}, \tfrac{64}{63}, \tfrac{256}{255}\), and the cubic book's telescoping at \(N = 4\). The research-scale construction — the integral as a mean-square limit, the optional-stopping theorems, Tanaka's formula, Girsanov's change of measure, and the jump calculus — is stated as named doors with every gap named; nothing on this page proves the general case. What this page settles is the dictionary at integer scale: the two books read, the correction computed path by path, the drag and the exit time certified by census, and the failure modes named with their prices.

6 · What to try next

The move is second-order bookkeeping, and several neighboring patterns are the same bookkeeping in different dress.

  • Door — the scales of the walk (P42): the squared-step clock is a multiscale census — each scale of steps contributes its own book, and P42's decomposition is the audit that reads the scales separately before summing.
  • Door — one-sided books (P29): \(\|W\|^2 - d\,t\) staying balanced is exactly P29's trade — find the quantity that moves only one way in expectation, and let it settle questions without solving anything; the exit time was P29's method wearing this page's steps.
  • Door — the mother sentence (P26): every book on this page began as P26's sentence with a coin on the right-hand side; when the model misbehaves, rewrite the sentence first and audit after.
  • Door — the walk's self-similarity (P56): rescale time by \(c^2\) and space by \(c\) and the walk is again a walk — the rescaling that makes \(\sqrt{t}\) the honest clock of spread; P56 owns the general discipline of zooming until a process becomes its own shape.
  • Door — the literature: Wiener (1923) built the path as an honest object; Itô (1944, and the integral at full scale, 1951) wrote the rule this page runs at fixed scale; Doob (1953) assembled the martingale books; and the receipt that made the calculus the frontier's accounting standard — a hedge that cancels the noise term exactly, a price that is the drag book discounted — is Black, Scholes, and Merton (1973). The reference construction (the mean-square limit, the generator bridge, Girsanov's change of measure, Tanaka's formula, the jump calculus) is Karatzas and Shreve's Brownian Motion and Stochastic Calculus — doors, listed, not built here.

7 · Lineage

The move's content is the second-order Taylor term, kept when the path got rough; its assembly is twentieth-century probability; its working life is the frontier's pricing and physics.

  • 1827 — Brown. The pollen dances: observed, measured, unexplained — the rough path enters the record two generations before any calculus could read it.
  • 1900 — Bachelier. The Bourse's first census: prices as a walk, options priced by the spread — the calculus used before it existed, and forgotten for want of its books.
  • 1905 — Einstein. The physical clock: the diffusion constant ties the spread to the molecular world, and the walk becomes measurable physics — this page's \(d\,t\), weighed in a laboratory.
  • 1923 — Wiener. The path exists: an honest construction with honest limits — the room this page's censuses live in, built.
  • 1940 — Lévy. The law itself: along every refining census the squared steps of the walk sum to \(t\) — the clock of §2, discovered and later named quadratic variation. The rule's premise, standing on its own.
  • 1944/1951 — Itô. The rule at small scale, then the integral at full: the chain rule re-audited, the second-order book promoted, the multiplication table written. The move itself, named and constructed.
  • 1953 — Doob. The fair books systematized: the martingale — the book that stays balanced — becomes the engine of every expectation this page ran.
  • 1973 — Black, Scholes, Merton. The receipt: a hedge that cancels the noise term, a price that is exactly the drag book discounted — the correction term of Exemplar 2, priced, and the frontier adopts the calculus as its accounting standard.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the counts above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — The two books, by hand. Take \(N = 4\) steps over one unit of time. (a) Smooth path \(x(t) = t\): compute \(\sum (\Delta x)^2\) and \(\sum |\Delta x|\) exactly. (b) Walk with steps \(\pm\tfrac{1}{2}\): over all \(16\) paths, how many distinct values does each book take? (c) Refine to \(N = 16\): which book changed, which did not, and where do the two sit against P15's walls?

Hint

(b) The squares never consult the coin; the absolute values never consult it either — at fixed \(N\). (c) \(\sqrt{16} = 4\).

Worked answer

(a) \(\sum (\Delta x)^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{16} = \tfrac{1}{4}\); \(\sum |\Delta x| = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = 1\). (b) One value each: \(\sum (\Delta x)^2 = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4} = 1\) and \(\sum |\Delta x| = 4 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 2\), identically on all \(16\) paths — the unsigned book's crime is growth in \(N\), not variance across paths. (c) At \(N = 16\): smooth squares \(\tfrac{1}{16}\) (dying), walk squares \(1\) (the clock, unchanged); smooth unsigned \(1\), walk unsigned \(4\) (exploding). The squared books are the two walls — pinned at \(0\) and \(1\) — and every refinement leaves the gap exactly \(1\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — Earn the fourth moment. Compute \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^4]\) by the rule, not by memory. (a) Apply the rule to \(f(x) = x^4\) and write the identity for \(W_t^4\). (b) Take expectations, using that the integral term is a fair book and \(\mathbb{E}[W_s^2] = s\). (c) Evaluate at \(t = 1\), and report the size of the naive chain-rule undercount. (d) The cubic book: apply the rule to \(f(x) = x^3\) and read the identity \(\int_0^T W_t^2\,dW_t = \tfrac{1}{3}W_T^3 - \int_0^T W_t\,dt\); name the two fixed-\(N\) books the telescoping carries, and their fates.

Hint

\(f'(x) = 4x^3\), \(f''(x) = 12x^2\), so the correction integrand is \(6W_s^2\). For (d): \(f'(x) = 3x^2\), \(f''(x) = 6x\).

Worked answer

(a) \(W_t^4 = 4\int_0^t W_s^3\,dW_s + 6\int_0^t W_s^2\,ds\). (b) The integral is fair: \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^4] = 6\int_0^t \mathbb{E}[W_s^2]\,ds = 6\int_0^t s\,ds = 3t^2\). (c) At \(t = 1\): \(3\). The chain rule alone would report a fair book with mean \(0\) — the undercount is the entire \(3t^2\), carried by the second-order book. The walk's moments are the correction term's receipts. (d) \(d(W_t^3) = 3W_t^2\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt\), so the cubic identity reads \(\int_0^T W_t^2\,dW_t = \tfrac{1}{3}W_T^3 - \int_0^T W_t\,dt\). At fixed \(N\) the telescoping of \(x^3\) carries two books beyond the integral: \(3\sum W_k (\Delta W_k)^2 = \tfrac{3}{N}\sum W_k\), a Riemann sum for exactly the \(\int 3W\,dt\) the identity keeps, and \(\sum (\Delta W_k)^3\), which dies at the audit's rate \(N^{-1/2}\). The cubic book is the founding reconciliation's sibling: the same audit, one power up.

Exercise 3 ●●● — The drag, compounded. A market reads \(\mu = 8\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\) yearly. (a) Compute the typical path's rate and the drag. (b) At \(t = 5\) and \(t = 25\), compute the census mean \(e^{\mu t}\) and the median \(e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)t}\), and the ratio between them. (c) Explain, in census words, why the mean exceeds the median without contradiction.

Hint

\(e^{0.4} \approx 1.4918\), \(e^{0.3} \approx 1.3499\), \(e^{2} \approx 7.389\), \(e^{1.5} \approx 4.482\).

Worked answer

(a) Median rate \(\mu - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 = 0.08 - 0.02 = 6\%\); drag \(2\%\). (b) \(t = 5\): mean \(e^{0.4} \approx 1.4918\), median \(e^{0.3} \approx 1.3499\), ratio \(e^{0.1} \approx 1.105\). \(t = 25\): mean \(e^{2} \approx 7.389\), median \(e^{1.5} \approx 4.482\), ratio \(e^{0.5} \approx 1.649\). The ratio is \(e^{\text{drag} \cdot t}\) — the drag compounds exactly like interest. (c) The mean is carried by rare enormous paths: the right tail grows with the spread while every typical path pays the drag yearly — the mean is an average over paths, the median is one path's book, and the second-order term is precisely the toll for the difference.

Exercise 4 ◆ — The exit time, audited. A particle walks from the center of the ball of radius \(R = 3\). (a) One dimension: verify \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 9\) by the rule. (b) Three dimensions: \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 3\); explain why adding dimensions reduces the expected exit time. (c) Now start at distance \(2\) from the center, in one dimension: predict, then reconcile with the fair book \(\|W\|^2 - \tau\).

Hint

(a) \((9 - 0)/1\). (c) \((9 - 4)/1 = 5\).

Worked answer

(a) The rule gives the balanced book \(\|W\|^2 - t\); at the exit \(\|W_\tau\|^2 = 9\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = (9 - 0)/1 = 9\) — the classical gambler's ruin answer, earned without solving anything. (b) \((9 - 0)/3 = 3\): more directions to exit in raise the clock's rate \(d\) while the boundary's square stays \(9\) — the boundary is statistically nearer in time. (c) \((9 - 4)/1 = 5\): starting nearer the wall cuts the expectation by exactly the starting square, and the balance \(\|W\|^2 - \tau\) is honest wherever the walk starts.

Exercise 5 ◐ — Diagnose the three claims, no safety net. Cite the exact audit of this page that settles each. (a) "For \(f\) smooth, \(\int_0^t f'(W_s)\,dW_s\) is a fair book, so \(\mathbb{E}[f(W_t)] = f(0)\) always." (b) "Since \(\ln\) is the inverse of \(\exp\), a price with median growth \(6\%\) and \(\sigma = 20\%\) has mean growth \(6\%\)." (c) "The midpoint and left-endpoint books differ by noise that averages out, so for pricing they are interchangeable."

Worked answer

(a) False as stated: the rule gives \(\mathbb{E}[f(W_t)] = f(0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \mathbb{E}[f''(W_s)]\,ds\), so only \(f'' = 0\) (truly linear \(f\)) keeps the mean at \(f(0)\) — the founding reconciliation is the counterexample, with \(f = x^2\). (b) False: the drag runs the other way — mean rate \(= 6\% + \tfrac{1}{2}(0.20)^2 = 8\%\) (Exemplar 2's audit; the log of the census mean is not the census of the log). (c) False: the difference is a drift, not noise — deterministic, growing like interest, and it does not average out (Exhibit A); the books answer different questions and the contract must be named.

Exercise 6 ◐ — The exponential book, and the seed of the receipt. (a) Apply the rule to \(f(x) = e^x\) and take expectations. (b) Show the book \(M_t = e^{W_t - t/2}\) stays balanced in expectation — the exponential's own correction cancels the drift exactly. (c) State the balanced book for general \(\lambda\), and why this one cancellation is the seed of §6's option-price receipt.

Hint

(a) \(f' = f'' = f\). (c) The correction's coefficient is \(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^2\).

Worked answer

(a) \(e^{W_t} = 1 + \int_0^t e^{W_s}\,dW_s + \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t e^{W_s}\,ds\); expectations: \(\mathbb{E}[e^{W_t}] = 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}\int_0^t \mathbb{E}[e^{W_s}]\,ds\), so \(m(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{W_t}]\) obeys \(m' = \tfrac{1}{2}m\), \(m(0) = 1\): \(m(t) = e^{t/2}\). (b) The rule on \(f(x, t) = e^{x - t/2}\) — the \(t\)-book contributes \(-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{x - t/2}\,dt\), canceling the correction \(+\tfrac{1}{2}\) exactly — gives \(\mathbb{E}[M_t] = 1\) for all \(t\): a book the noise cannot unbalance. (c) \(e^{\lambda W_t - \lambda^2 t/2}\) is balanced for every \(\lambda\); the option price is built by choosing the hedge that cancels a portfolio's noise book — exactly this cancellation with the interest rate in the \(dt\) seat. §6's receipt, seeded.
