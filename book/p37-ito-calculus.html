The Patterns Atlas · P37 · the third move of tier 4

Itô calculus (differentiating randomness)

When a random walk takes steps of size \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), the square of a tiny step is not negligible — it is the clock \(\Delta t\) itself, so every chain rule must keep its second-order Taylor term.

Brownian motion \(W_t\) and quadratic variation \([W]_t\). A standard Brownian motion (or Wiener process) \(W_t\) starting at \(W_0 = 0\) is the continuous-time limit of a symmetric coin-toss walk where, over each tiny time slice \(\Delta t = T/N\), the position steps by \(\Delta W_k = \pm\sqrt{\Delta t}\) with equal probability \(\frac{1}{2}\), so that increments over disjoint time intervals are independent with mean \(\mathbb{E}[\Delta W_k] = 0\) and variance \(\mathbb{E}[(\Delta W_k)^2] = \Delta t\). The quadratic variation \([X]_T = \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{k=1}^N (X_{t_k} - X_{t_{k-1}})^2\) measures the accumulated sum of squared increments along a refined partition of \([0, T]\).

1 · Before we begin

P26 taught us how to turn a physical balance law into a differential equation — as on the cooling coffee mug \(dT/dt = -k(T - T_{\text{env}})\) — by writing a small change over time \(\Delta t\), dividing by \(\Delta t\), and discarding higher powers \((\Delta t)^2\) in the limit (P15). P19 introduced probability spaces where independent coin flips accumulate variance linearly with the number of trials.

Here is the collision between those two moves. Suppose a particle or asset price is buffeted at every instant by independent random kicks whose total variance over one second equals \(1\). If we chop one second into \(N\) steps of duration \(\Delta t = 1/N\), each kick must have variance \(\Delta t\) — which forces the step size itself to have magnitude \(\sqrt{\Delta t} = 1/\sqrt{N}\). When \(\Delta t = 10^{-6}\), the step is \(\sqrt{\Delta t} = 10^{-3}\), a thousand times larger than \(\Delta t\)! As \(N \to \infty\), the slope \(\Delta W / \Delta t = \pm 1/\sqrt{\Delta t} \to \pm\infty\) blows up everywhere, and the squared increment \((\Delta W)^2 = \Delta t\) refuses to vanish. How do you run calculus on a path that is continuous everywhere, differentiable nowhere, and accumulates non-zero squared steps at every scale?

2 · Meet the problem: the squared step that refuses to die

Chop the unit time interval \([0, 1]\) into \(N\) equal slices of width \(\Delta t = 1/N\) at times \(t_k = k/N\). Compare two trajectories that both travel from \(0\) to \(O(1)\) across \([0,1]\):

  • A smooth parabola: \(x(t) = t^2\), whose \(k\)-th increment is \(\Delta x_k = t_k^2 - t_{k-1}^2 = \frac{2k-1}{N^2}\).
  • A scaled coin-toss walk: \(W_{t_k} = \sum_{j=1}^k \Delta W_j\), where each step is \(\Delta W_k = \xi_k \sqrt{\Delta t} = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\) for a fair coin \(\xi_k \in \{-1, +1\}\).
Now compute the sum of squared steps \(Q_N = \sum_{k=1}^N (\text{step}_k)^2\) for both paths as the grid gets finer:

Slices \(N\) Time Step \(\Delta t = 1/N\) Smooth Step Size \(\Delta x_k\) Smooth Sum \(\sum_{k=1}^N (\Delta x_k)^2 = \frac{4N^2-1}{3N^3}\) Random Walk Step \(|\Delta W_k| = \sqrt{\Delta t}\) Walk Sum \(\sum_{k=1}^N (\Delta W_k)^2\)
\(4\) \(1/4 = 0.25\) \(\le 7/16 \approx 0.4375\) \(\frac{21}{64} \approx 0.328125\) \(1/2 = 0.50\) \(4 \times \frac{1}{4} = \mathbf{1.000000}\)
\(16\) \(1/16 = 0.0625\) \(\le 31/256 \approx 0.1211\) \(\frac{341}{4096} \approx 0.083252\) \(1/4 = 0.25\) \(16 \times \frac{1}{16} = \mathbf{1.000000}\)
\(64\) \(1/64 \approx 0.0156\) \(\le 127/4096 \approx 0.0310\) \(\frac{5461}{262144} \approx 0.020832\) \(1/8 = 0.125\) \(64 \times \frac{1}{64} = \mathbf{1.000000}\)
\(256\) \(1/256 \approx 0.0039\) \(\le 511/65536 \approx 0.0078\) \(\frac{87381}{16777216} \approx 0.005208\) \(1/16 = 0.0625\) \(256 \times \frac{1}{256} = \mathbf{1.000000}\)
\(N \to \infty\) \(dt \to 0\) \(2t\,dt \to 0\) \(\frac{4}{3N} \to \mathbf{0}\) (P15) \(\sqrt{dt} \to 0\) \(N \times \frac{1}{N} \to \mathbf{1}\) (on all \(2^N\) paths!)

Look at the last column. Even though the random walk \(W\) is random — at \(N = 256\) there are \(2^{256} \approx 1.16 \times 10^{77}\) distinct coin-toss trajectories — on every single trajectory each step squares to \((\pm 1/\sqrt{N})^2 = +1/N\), so the sum of squared steps over \([0, t]\) equals \(t\) deterministically! Now watch what that non-zero squared step does when we telescope (P69, B11) the square of the position \(W_N^2\):

\[W_{k+1}^2 - W_k^2 = \bigl(W_k + \Delta W_k\bigr)^2 - W_k^2 = 2\,W_k\,\Delta W_k + (\Delta W_k)^2.\]

Sum both sides from \(k = 0\) to \(N - 1\) (with \(W_0 = 0\) and \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} (\Delta W_k)^2 = T\)) and divide by \(2\):

\[\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} W_{t_k}\bigl(W_{t_{k+1}} - W_{t_k}\bigr) = \frac{1}{2}W_T^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}(\Delta W_k)^2 = \frac{1}{2}W_T^2 - \frac{1}{2}T.\]

In ordinary calculus, \(\int_0^T x_t\,dx_t = \frac{1}{2}x_T^2\). For a random walk evaluated at the left endpoint \(W_{t_k}\) (before the \((k+1)\)-st coin is tossed), the integral \(\int_0^T W_t\,dW_t\) is not \(\frac{1}{2}W_T^2\) — it is \(\frac{1}{2}W_T^2 - \frac{1}{2}T\)! Why must there be a \(-\frac{1}{2}T\) subtraction? Take the expected value of both sides: on the left, \(W_{t_k}\) is already known before the fair coin \(\Delta W_k\) is flipped, so \(\mathbb{E}[W_{t_k}\,\Delta W_k] = \mathbb{E}[W_{t_k}]\mathbb{E}[\Delta W_k] = 0\). On the right, \(\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{2}W_T^2] = \frac{1}{2}T > 0\). Only after subtracting \(\frac{1}{2}\sum (\Delta W_k)^2 = \frac{1}{2}T\) do the two expectations match at \(0\).

3 · Name the move

Kiyosi Itô observed in 1944 that because \((\Delta W_t)^2\) has size \(\Delta t\) while \((\Delta t)^2\) and \(\Delta t\,\Delta W_t\) have sizes \((\Delta t)^2\) and \((\Delta t)^{3/2}\) (both negligible as \(\Delta t \to 0\) by P15), any Taylor expansion along a stochastic differential equation

\[dX_t = \mu(t, X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, X_t)\,dW_t\]

must retain its second-order derivative in \(x\) and substitute the quadratic variation rule \((dW_t)^2 = dt\).

Non-anticipating (adapted) processes and the Itô vs. Stratonovich convention. A process \(H_t\) is non-anticipating (or adapted) if its value at time \(t\) depends only on the history of the random walk up to time \(t\), never peeking at future coin flips. The Itô integral \(\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t = \lim \sum H_{t_k}(W_{t_{k+1}} - W_{t_k})\) evaluates the integrand at the left endpoint \(t_k\), guaranteeing that \(\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T H_t\,dW_t] = 0\) (a fair game, or martingale). Evaluating at the midpoint \(\frac{1}{2}(t_k + t_{k+1})\) defines the Stratonovich integral \(\int_0^T H_t \circ dW_t\), which obeys the classical chain rule but loses the zero-mean martingale property.

Pattern P37 — Itô calculus (differentiating randomness). Let \(X_t\) satisfy the stochastic differential equation \(dX_t = \mu(t, X_t)\,dt + \sigma(t, X_t)\,dW_t\). For any smooth function \(f(t, x) \in C^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \times \mathbb{R})\), expand \(f(t + dt, X_t + dX_t)\) to second order in \(dX_t\) and apply the multiplication table \((dt)^2 = 0\), \(dt\,dW_t = 0\), \((dW_t)^2 = dt\) so that \((dX_t)^2 = \sigma(t, X_t)^2\,dt\). This yields Itô's Lemma:

\[df(t, X_t) = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu(t, X_t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma(t, X_t)^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\right) dt + \sigma(t, X_t)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t.\]

Or, in words: When differentiating a function of a noisy trajectory, add half the variance rate times the second derivative (\(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f_{xx}\,dt\)) to the ordinary drift; convexity (\(f_{xx} > 0\)) converts random jiggling into upward drift, while concavity (\(f_{xx} < 0\)) converts random jiggling into downward drag.

Box P37.A — The Itô multiplication table and the three obligations.

  1. The formal differential table:

    \[dt \cdot dt = 0, \qquad dt \cdot dW_t = 0, \qquad dW_t \cdot dW_t = dt, \qquad dW_t^{(i)} \cdot dW_t^{(j)} = \rho_{ij}\,dt.\]

  2. Left-endpoint (non-anticipating) audit: Verify that the integrand \(\sigma(t, X_t) \partial_x f(t, X_t)\) is evaluated strictly at the start of each step \(t_k\). Because \(\mathbb{E}[H_{t_k}\Delta W_k] = 0\), the stochastic integral \(\int_0^T \sigma\,\partial_x f\,dW_t\) has expected value zero (provided \(\mathbb{E}\int_0^T (\sigma\,\partial_x f)^2\,dt < \infty\)).
  3. The Generator–PDE bridge (P26): Setting the bracketed \(dt\) drift of \(df(t, X_t)\) to zero pairs the random process \(X_t\) with the backward parabolic PDE \(\partial_t f + \mu\,\partial_x f + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial_{xx} f = 0\), turning expected values \(\mathbb{E}[g(X_T) \mid X_t = x]\) into exact solutions of a deterministic heat-type equation (Feynman–Kac).
Key Signature
Claim type An exact closed-form trajectory, expected value, conservation law, or PDE representation for a nonlinear function \(f(t, X_t)\) of a noisy continuous-time process.
Problem shape Continuous dynamics driven by white-noise / Brownian fluctuations where step sizes scale as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), invalidating the first-order chain rule of P26.
Tools at hand Second-order Taylor expansion, quadratic variation \([W]_t = t\), and the zero-mean property of left-endpoint martingale integrals.

Three-key diagnosis. Reach for P37 whenever you apply a nonlinear coordinate change (such as \(\ln S_t\), \(X_t^2\), or \(e^{\lambda X_t}\)) to a differential equation driven by Brownian noise.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 · Volatility drag and geometric Brownian motion (\(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\))

Imagine an investment \(S_t\) that starts at \(S_0 = 100\) and undergoes proportional shocks: in discrete steps, tossing Heads raises \(S\) by \(+50\%\) (\(\times 1.5\)) and tossing Tails cuts \(S\) by \(-50\%\) (\(\times 0.5\)). The expected return on any single toss is \(\frac{1}{2}(+50\%) + \frac{1}{2}(-50\%) = 0\%\). Yet after one Head and one Tail, your capital is \(100 \times 1.5 \times 0.5 = 75\) — a \(25\%\) loss! Where did that downward drag come from?

Model the continuous limit (P26) by the geometric Brownian motion equation \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\). To turn multiplication into addition (P05), apply Itô's lemma (P37) to the concave logarithm \(f(S) = \ln S\), whose derivatives are \(f'(S) = \frac{1}{S}\) and \(f''(S) = -\frac{1}{S^2}\). Because \((dS_t)^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2 (dW_t)^2 = \sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt\), Box P37 gives:

\[d(\ln S_t) = \frac{1}{S_t}\,dS_t + \frac{1}{2}\!\left(-\frac{1}{S_t^2}\right)(dS_t)^2 = \left(\mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\right) - \frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{S_t^2}\bigl(\sigma^2 S_t^2\,dt\bigr) = \left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)dt + \sigma\,dW_t.\]

The \(S_t\) factors cancel completely! Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\) and exponentiating yields the exact trajectory formula

\[S_t = S_0 \exp\!\left(\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t\right).\]

Notice the sharp dichotomy between the mean \(\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0 e^{\mu t}\) and the median (where \(W_t = 0\)) \(S_t^{\text{med}} = S_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t}\). Because \(\ln S\) is concave (\(f''(S) = -1/S^2 < 0\)), the chord in Fig P37.2 sags below the tangent line, carving out a deterministic volatility drag of \(-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\) per unit time. If \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma > 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[S_t] = S_0\) stays constant only because an exponentially tiny fraction of lucky trajectories shoot high enough to mask the fact that almost every individual trajectory decays to \(0\) at rate \(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t}\).

Exemplar 2 · Expected exit time from a sphere without solving a PDE (\(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\tau] = \frac{R^2 - \|\mathbf{x}\|^2}{d}\))

Suppose a Brownian particle \(\mathbf{W}_t = (W_t^{(1)}, \dots, W_t^{(d)})\) starts at position \(\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^d\) inside a ball of radius \(R\) (\(\|\mathbf{x}_0\| < R\)), with independent coordinates satisfying \(dW_t^{(i)}\cdot dW_t^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}\,dt\). Let \(\tau = \inf\{t \ge 0 : \|\mathbf{W}_t\| = R\}\) be the first moment the particle hits the boundary sphere. What is the expected exit time \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0}[\tau]\)?

Instead of setting up a Green's function integral, apply P37 to the squared radius \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^d x_i^2\). Since \(\partial_{x_i} f = 2x_i\) and \(\partial_{x_i x_i} f = 2\) for each of the \(d\) independent coordinates, summing Itô's lemma across \(i = 1, \dots, d\) gives

\[d\bigl(\|\mathbf{W}_t\|^2\bigr) = \sum_{i=1}^d 2W_t^{(i)}\,dW_t^{(i)} + \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^d (2)\,dt = 2\,\mathbf{W}_t \cdot d\mathbf{W}_t + d\,dt.\]

Subtract \(d\,dt\) from both sides: the combination \(M_t = \|\mathbf{W}_t\|^2 - d\,t\) has zero \(dt\)-drift (\(dM_t = 2\,\mathbf{W}_t \cdot d\mathbf{W}_t\)), so \(M_t\) is a martingale (P09 in expectation)! By Doob's optional stopping theorem (valid here because the ball is bounded, \(\|\mathbf{W}_{t \wedge \tau}\| \le R\)), the expected value of \(M_\tau\) at the exit moment \(\tau\) equals its starting value \(M_0 = \|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2\):

\[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0}\bigl[\|\mathbf{W}_\tau\|^2 - d\,\tau\bigr] = \|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2 - d\cdot 0 = \|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2.\]

At the exact moment \(\tau\) of hitting the boundary sphere, \(\|\mathbf{W}_\tau\|^2 = R^2\) deterministically! Solving for \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0}[\tau]\) yields

\[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_0}[\tau] = \frac{R^2 - \|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2}{d}.\]

How you could have found this: Each of the \(d\) coordinates contributes \(+1\cdot dt\) to the radial quadratic variation, so the squared distance from the origin grows on average at speed \(d\). To climb from \(\|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2\) to \(R^2\) at speed \(d\) takes average time \((R^2 - \|\mathbf{x}_0\|^2)/d\).

Exemplar 3 · Thermal equilibrium of a noisy spring (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck equipartition)

Combine the deterministic cooling-mug restoring force of P26 with continuous thermal kicks of intensity \(\sigma\): a particle in an optical trap with stiffness \(\gamma > 0\) obeys the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck stochastic differential equation

\[dX_t = -\gamma X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t.\]

We can extract both the exact trajectory and the steady-state variance in two passes of P37:

  • Pass 1 (Linear integrating factor, P06): Apply P37 to \(f(t, x) = e^{\gamma t} x\). Because \(f\) is linear in \(x\), \(\partial_{xx} f = 0\) and the Itô correction vanishes! Thus \(d(e^{\gamma t} X_t) = \gamma e^{\gamma t} X_t\,dt + e^{\gamma t}(-\gamma X_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t) = \sigma e^{\gamma t}\,dW_t\), which integrates to

    \[X_t = X_0 e^{-\gamma t} + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\gamma(t-s)}\,dW_s.\]

  • Pass 2 (Quadratic energy balance, P29): Apply P37 to \(g(x) = x^2\), where \(g'(x) = 2x\) and \(g''(x) = 2\):

    \[d(X_t^2) = 2X_t\,dX_t + \frac{1}{2}(2)(dX_t)^2 = \bigl(-2\gamma X_t^2 + \sigma^2\bigr)dt + 2\sigma X_t\,dW_t.\]

    Taking expectations kills the \(dW_t\) martingale term and gives an ordinary linear ODE for the variance \(v(t) = \mathbb{E}[X_t^2]\): \(\dot{v}(t) = -2\gamma v(t) + \sigma^2\). Solving \(\dot{v} = -2\gamma(v - \frac{\sigma^2}{2\gamma})\) gives

    \[\mathbb{E}[X_t^2] = X_0^2 e^{-2\gamma t} + \frac{\sigma^2}{2\gamma}\bigl(1 - e^{-2\gamma t}\bigr) \;\xrightarrow{t \to \infty}\; \frac{\sigma^2}{2\gamma}.\]

    Without the Itô term \(+\sigma^2\,dt\), the energy would decay to zero; with it, the spring settles into exact thermal equilibrium where linear damping \(-2\gamma\mathbb{E}[X^2]\) balances the quadratic variation pump \(+\sigma^2\).

5 · When the trick breaks

Itô's lemma is exact whenever its three structural assumptions hold, and fails sharply when any one of them is violated:

Exhibit A — Peeking into the future (Midpoint / Stratonovich vs. Left-Endpoint / Itô) changes the answer by \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). In our 16-path table of Section 2 (\(N=4, \Delta t = \frac{1}{4}, \Delta W_k = \pm\frac{1}{2}\)), evaluating the Riemann sum at the left endpoint \(W_{t_k}\) gave \(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} W_{t_k}\Delta W_k = \frac{1}{2}W_T^2 - \frac{1}{2}T\) with mean \(0\). If instead you evaluate at the midpoint \(\overline{W}_k = \frac{1}{2}(W_{t_k} + W_{t_{k+1}})\) (the Stratonovich convention, which occurs naturally when physical white noise is smoothed at correlation time \(\tau_c \to 0\)), then \(\overline{W}_k \Delta W_k = \frac{1}{2}(W_{t_{k+1}}^2 - W_{t_k}^2)\) telescopes directly to \(\frac{1}{2}W_T^2\) with mean \(+\frac{1}{2}T\)! Why? Because the midpoint \(\frac{1}{2}(W_{t_k} + W_{t_{k+1}}) = W_{t_k} + \frac{1}{2}\Delta W_k\) already contains half of the current step \(\Delta W_k\), and \(\frac{1}{2}(\Delta W_k)^2 = \frac{1}{2}\Delta t\).

Mitigation: Explicitly declare whether an SDE is written in Itô form (\(dW_t\)) or Stratonovich form (\(\circ\,dW_t\)), and convert between them using the exact bridge \(dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma \circ dW_t \Longleftrightarrow dX_t = \bigl(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma\partial_x\sigma\bigr)dt + \sigma\,dW_t\).

Price: One extra derivative \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma\sigma_x\,dt\) when translating physical smoothed-noise models into Itô form.

Default: Use Itô in finance,discrete-time filtering, and martingale proofs (where non-anticipation is mandatory); convert physical Wong–Zakai limits from Stratonovich to Itô before computing expectations.

Exhibit B — Kinks and corners where \(f''(x)\) does not exist (Tanaka's local time). Suppose you apply Itô's lemma to the absolute value \(f(x) = |x|\) (the payoff of a straddle at strike \(0\)). Everywhere away from \(x = 0\), \(f''(x) = 0\), so a naive application of Box P37 would say \(d|W_t| = \operatorname{sgn}(W_t)\,dW_t + 0\,dt\), implying \(\mathbb{E}[|W_t|] = 0\) — an obvious falsehood since \(|W_t| \ge 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[|W_t|] = \sqrt{2t/\pi} > 0\)! The second derivative at the kink \(x = 0\) is twice a Dirac delta \(2\delta_0(x)\).

Mitigation: Replace \(\frac{1}{2}f''(X_t)\,dt\) at a corner \(x = a\) with Tanaka's formula \(d|W_t - a| = \operatorname{sgn}(W_t - a)\,dW_t + dL_t^a\), where \(L_t^a = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_0^t \mathbf{1}_{|W_s - a| < \varepsilon}\,ds\) is the local time spent at the kink.

Price: Tracking a singular increasing boundary process \(dL_t^a\) supported on \(\{t : W_t = a\}\).

Default: Always check \(f \in C^{1,2}\) before dropping the second-order term on piecewise-linear functions.

Exhibit C — Jump processes (Poisson / Lévy jumps) violate the two-term Taylor cutoff. Itô's lemma stops at the second derivative \(f_{xx}\) only because continuous Brownian paths have \((\Delta W_t)^3 \sim (\Delta t)^{3/2} \to 0\) (sum of cubes \(\sim N(\Delta t)^{3/2} = N^{-1/2} \to 0\)). If \(X_t\) has discrete jumps \(\Delta X_t = J\) of macroscopic size \(O(1)\) arriving at Poisson rate \(\lambda\), then \((\Delta X_t)^3, (\Delta X_t)^4, \dots\) are all of order \(O(1)\) at the jump instant!

Mitigation: Separate the continuous Brownian part from the discrete jump sum: \(df(X_t) = f'(X_{t^-})\,dX_t^{\text{cont}} + \frac{1}{2}f''(X_{t^-})\,d[X^{\text{cont}}]_t + \bigl(f(X_{t^-} + \Delta X_t) - f(X_{t^-})\bigr)\).

Price: The generator becomes an integro-differential operator rather than a local second-order PDE.

Default: Verify path continuity before truncating the Taylor expansion at order \(2\).

Honest scope of this chapter. Our discrete telescoping proof on the \(2^N\) coin-toss lattice captures the exact algebraic mechanism of quadratic variation. On continuous Brownian paths, \([W]_T = T\) holds in \(L^2(\Omega)\) over arbitrary sequences of partitions with mesh \(\max_k \Delta t_k \to 0\) (since \(\operatorname{Var}(\sum (\Delta W_k)^2) = 2\sum (\Delta t_k)^2 \le 2T\max_k \Delta t_k \to 0\)), and holds almost surely along any Refining sequence of dyadic partitions \(N = 2^m\). Also remember that a driftless Itô integral \(M_t = \int_0^t H_s\,dW_s\) is initially a local martingale; promoting it to a true martingale with \(\mathbb{E}[M_t] = 0\) requires a square-integrability check such as \(\mathbb{E}\int_0^T H_s^2\,ds < \infty\).

7 · Lineage interlude

From Brown's pollen grains and Bachelier's options to Wiener's measure and Itô's chain rule. In 1827 the botanist Robert Brown observed jittering microscopic particles suspended in water. Seventy-three years later, Louis Bachelier (Théorie de la Spéculation, 1900) modeled Paris Bourse option prices via Gaussian random walks and derived the heat equation five years before Albert Einstein (Annalen der Physik, 1905) and Marian Smoluchowski (1906) connected molecular collisions to the diffusion constant \(D = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\). Norbert Wiener (Journal of Mathematics and Physics, 1923) constructed a rigorous probability measure on the space of continuous paths, proving that almost every path is nowhere differentiable, and Paul Lévy (1940) discovered the quadratic variation law \([W]_t = t\). During World War II, Kiyosi Itô (Proceedings of the Imperial Academy of Tokyo 20, 1944, 519–524; Memoirs of the American Mathematical Society 4, 1951) constructed the left-endpoint stochastic integral and published his second-order chain rule, transforming stochastic differential equations into an exact calculus later universalized across martingale theory by Joseph Doob (1953) and across mathematical finance by Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton (1973).

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Fourth moment \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^4] = 3t^2\) and the cubic integral \(\int_0^T W_t^2\,dW_t\)

Let \(W_t\) be a standard Brownian motion with \(W_0 = 0\).
(a) Apply Itô's lemma (P37) to \(f(x) = x^3\) to express \(\int_0^T W_t^2\,dW_t\) in terms of \(W_T^3\) and an ordinary time integral \(\int_0^T W_t\,dt\).
(b) Apply Itô's lemma to \(g(x) = x^4\), take expectations on both sides using \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^2] = t\), and solve the resulting ordinary differential equation to prove that \(\mathbb{E}[W_t^4] = 3t^2\).

Hint

For \(f(x) = x^3\), \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) and \(f''(x) = 6x\). For \(g(x) = x^4\), \(g'(x) = 4x^3\) and \(g''(x) = 12x^2\).

Worked answer

(a) Cubic Itô identity: For \(f(x) = x^3\), we have \(f'(x) = 3x^2\) and \(f''(x) = 6x\). Applying Box P37 with \(\mu = 0, \sigma = 1\) gives

\[d(W_t^3) = 3W_t^2\,dW_t + \frac{1}{2}(6W_t)(dW_t)^2 = 3W_t^2\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt.\]

Integrating from \(0\) to \(T\) and dividing by \(3\) yields the exact pathwise identity

\[\int_0^T W_t^2\,dW_t = \frac{1}{3}W_T^3 - \int_0^T W_t\,dt.\]

(b) Fourth moment via ODE: For \(g(x) = x^4\), we have \(g'(x) = 4x^3\) and \(g''(x) = 12x^2\), so

\[d(W_t^4) = 4W_t^3\,dW_t + \frac{1}{2}(12W_t^2)\,dt = 4W_t^3\,dW_t + 6W_t^2\,dt.\]

Integrating from \(0\) to \(t\) and taking expectations kills the zero-mean martingale integral \(\mathbb{E}[\int_0^t 4W_s^3\,dW_s] = 0\), leaving

\[\mathbb{E}[W_t^4] = 6\int_0^t \mathbb{E}[W_s^2]\,ds = 6\int_0^t s\,ds = 6\left(\frac{t^2}{2}\right) = 3t^2.\]

Exercise 2 ●●○ — The exponential martingale and Hermite polynomials

For any constant parameter \(\lambda \in \mathbb{R}\), define the process

\[Z_t = \exp\!\left(\lambda W_t - \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t\right).\]

(a) Apply Itô's lemma to \(f(t, x) = \exp(\lambda x - \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t)\) to compute \(dZ_t\) and prove that \(Z_t\) has zero \(dt\)-drift (\(dZ_t = \lambda Z_t\,dW_t\)), so \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t] = Z_0 = 1\) for all \(t \ge 0\).
(b) Expand \(Z_t = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda^n}{n!} H_n(t, W_t)\) in powers of \(\lambda\) for \(n = 0, 1, 2, 3\) to read off three non-trivial polynomial martingales \(H_1(t, W_t)\), \(H_2(t, W_t)\), and \(H_3(t, W_t)\) immediately.

Hint

Compute \(\partial_t f = -\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 f\), \(\partial_x f = \lambda f\), and \(\partial_{xx} f = \lambda^2 f\). For part (b), multiply the Taylor series of \(e^{\lambda W_t}\) and \(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t}\).

Worked answer

(a) Zero drift of the exponential martingale: With \(f(t,x) = \exp(\lambda x - \frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t)\), the partial derivatives are \(\partial_t f = -\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 f\), \(\partial_x f = \lambda f\), and \(\partial_{xx} f = \lambda^2 f\). By Box P37,

\[dZ_t = \left(-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 Z_t + \frac{1}{2}(1)^2(\lambda^2 Z_t)\right)dt + \lambda Z_t\,dW_t = 0\,dt + \lambda Z_t\,dW_t.\]

Because the \(dt\) drift cancels identically, \(Z_t = 1 + \lambda\int_0^t Z_s\,dW_s\) is a martingale with \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t] = 1\) (which also confirms the Gaussian moment generating function \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda W_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t}\)).

(b) Hermite polynomial martingales by power-series matching (P73): Multiplying the two series

\[e^{\lambda W_t}\cdot e^{-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^2 t} = \left(1 + \lambda W_t + \frac{\lambda^2}{2}W_t^2 + \frac{\lambda^3}{6}W_t^3 + \cdots\right)\left(1 - \frac{\lambda^2}{2}t + \cdots\right)\]

and collecting coefficients of \(\lambda^n / n!\) gives

\[H_1(t, W_t) = W_t, \qquad H_2(t, W_t) = W_t^2 - t, \qquad H_3(t, W_t) = W_t^3 - 3t\,W_t.\]

Because \(Z_t\) is a martingale for every \(\lambda\), each coefficient \(H_n(t, W_t)\) has zero \(dt\)-drift: indeed, from Exercise 1(a), \(d(W_t^3 - 3tW_t) = (3W_t^2\,dW_t + 3W_t\,dt) - (3W_t\,dt + 3t\,dW_t) = 3(W_t^2 - t)\,dW_t\).

Exercise 3 ●●● — Asymmetric gambler's ruin and hitting probabilities in a drift field

A particle starts at \(x \in (-L, L)\) and satisfies the drifted Brownian motion \(dX_t = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\) with constant drift \(\mu \ne 0\) and volatility \(\sigma > 0\). Let \(\tau = \inf\{t \ge 0 : X_t \notin (-L, L)\}\) be the first exit time from the interval \([-L, L]\).
(a) Find the non-zero exponent \(\alpha = -\frac{2\mu}{\sigma^2}\) such that \(f(x) = e^{\alpha x}\) satisfies the stationary generator equation \(\mu f'(x) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f''(x) = 0\), making \(M_t = e^{\alpha X_t}\) a martingale.
(b) Use \(\mathbb{E}_x[M_\tau] = M_0 = e^{\alpha x}\) to compute the exact probability \(p(x) = \mathbb{P}_x(X_\tau = +L)\) that the particle exits through the top wall \(+L\) before hitting \(-L\), and verify that \(\lim_{\mu \to 0} p(x) = \frac{x + L}{2L}\).

Hint

Substitute \(f(x) = e^{\alpha x}\) into \(df(X_t) = (\mu \alpha + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \alpha^2)e^{\alpha X_t}\,dt + \sigma\alpha e^{\alpha X_t}\,dW_t\). At exit time \(\tau\), \(X_\tau = +L\) with probability \(p(x)\) and \(X_\tau = -L\) with probability \(1 - p(x)\).

Worked answer

(a) Choosing \(\alpha\) to kill the drift (scale function): Applying Box P37 to \(f(x) = e^{\alpha x}\) gives

\[d\bigl(e^{\alpha X_t}\bigr) = \alpha\left(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \alpha\right)e^{\alpha X_t}\,dt + \alpha\sigma e^{\alpha X_t}\,dW_t.\]

Setting the bracketed \(dt\) coefficient to zero for \(\alpha \ne 0\) pins down \(\alpha = -\frac{2\mu}{\sigma^2}\). Thus \(M_t = \exp\!\bigl(-\frac{2\mu}{\sigma^2}X_t\bigr)\) is a bounded martingale up to exit time \(\tau\).

(b) Exact exit probability \(p(x)\): By optional stopping, \(\mathbb{E}_x[M_\tau] = M_0 = e^{\alpha x}\). Because \(X_\tau\) equals \(+L\) with probability \(p(x)\) and \(-L\) with probability \(1 - p(x)\),

\[p(x)\,e^{\alpha L} + \bigl(1 - p(x)\bigr)e^{-\alpha L} = e^{\alpha x} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p(x) = \frac{e^{\alpha x} - e^{-\alpha L}}{e^{\alpha L} - e^{-\alpha L}} = \frac{e^{-2\mu x/\sigma^2} - e^{2\mu L/\sigma^2}}{e^{-2\mu L/\sigma^2} - e^{2\mu L/\sigma^2}}.\]

As \(\mu \to 0\) (\(\alpha \to 0\)), expanding \(e^{\alpha z} = 1 + \alpha z + O(\alpha^2)\) (P15) gives \(\lim_{\alpha \to 0} \frac{(1 + \alpha x) - (1 - \alpha L)}{(1 + \alpha L) - (1 - \alpha L)} = \frac{\alpha(x + L)}{2\alpha L} = \frac{x + L}{2L}\).

Exercise 4 ◆ — Pattern-unknown diagnosis: does Itô's correction appear or not?

For each differential equation or integral below, diagnose whether an Itô second-derivative correction \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 f_{xx}\,dt\) is present, or whether classical first-order calculus (P26) applies directly:

  1. A particle's position \(X_t\) obeys \(\frac{dX_t}{dt} = V_t\), where the velocity \(V_t\) is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process \(dV_t = -\gamma V_t\,dt + \sigma\,dW_t\). You want to differentiate \(f(X_t) = X_t^2\).
  2. A random phase angle is \(\theta_t = W_t\) (standard Brownian motion), and a point on the unit circle is \(Z_t = (\cos W_t, \sin W_t)\). You want the drift of \(Z_t\).
  3. A noisy circle rotation is written explicitly in Stratonovich form: \(dX_t = -Y_t \circ dW_t\), \(dY_t = +X_t \circ dW_t\). You want to know whether \(X_t^2 + Y_t^2\) is conserved.
Worked answer
  1. No Itô correction on \(X_t\) (classical chain rule P26 applies to \(X_t\)!): Because \(dX_t = V_t\,dt + 0\,dW_t\) has zero \(dW_t\) noise coefficient directly in \(dX_t\), its quadratic variation is \((dX_t)^2 = V_t^2 (dt)^2 = 0\)! Thus \(d(X_t^2) = 2X_t V_t\,dt\) with no Itô term. (The Itô correction appears only when differentiating functions of the velocity \(V_t\), where \((dV_t)^2 = \sigma^2\,dt \ne 0\).)
  2. Genuine P37 (Itô damping toward the origin): Since \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cos x = -\cos x\) and \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\sin x = -\sin x\), applying Box P37 gives \(d(\cos W_t) = -\sin W_t\,dW_t - \frac{1}{2}\cos W_t\,dt\) and \(d(\sin W_t) = \cos W_t\,dW_t - \frac{1}{2}\sin W_t\,dt\). Even though \(Z_t\) stays on the unit circle pathwise, its expected position decays inward at rate \(\mathbb{E}[Z_t] = e^{-t/2} Z_0\)!
  3. Stratonovich form obeys the classical chain rule (Exhibit A): In Stratonovich calculus (\(\circ\,dW_t\)), midpoint evaluation cancels the second-order correction, so \(d(X_t^2 + Y_t^2) = 2X_t \circ dX_t + 2Y_t \circ dY_t = (-2X_t Y_t + 2Y_t X_t)\circ dW_t = 0\), keeping \(X_t^2 + Y_t^2 = R^2\) conserved. (If the same equations were written in Itô form \(dX_t = -Y_t\,dW_t, dY_t = X_t\,dW_t\), then \(d(X_t^2 + Y_t^2)\) would gain \(+((dX_t)^2 + (dY_t)^2) = +(X_t^2 + Y_t^2)\,dt\), causing the radius to spiral outward exponentially as \(e^{t/2}\)!)

Exercise 5 ◐ — Exploration: The Black–Scholes PDE by killing the \(dW_t\) term

An asset follows \(dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t\), and a risk-free bank account grows at rate \(r\): \(dB_t = r B_t\,dt\). Consider a derivative contract whose price at time \(t\) is \(V(t, S_t)\), and form a self-financing portfolio \(\Pi_t = V(t, S_t) - \Delta_t S_t\) holding \(1\) option and shorting \(\Delta_t\) shares of stock.
(a) Apply Itô's lemma (P37) to expand \(d\Pi_t = dV(t, S_t) - \Delta_t\,dS_t\), and choose the hedge ratio \(\Delta_t\) that makes the coefficient of \(dW_t\) vanish identically.
(b) Since the hedged portfolio \(\Pi_t\) is completely deterministic over the next instant \(dt\), no-arbitrage requires \(d\Pi_t = r\Pi_t\,dt\). Substitute your expression for \(d\Pi_t\) to derive the Black–Scholes partial differential equation, and explain why the stock's subjective drift rate \(\mu\) vanishes completely from the equation.

Hint

By Box P37, \(dV = (\partial_t V + \mu S_t \partial_S V + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_t^2 \partial_{SS} V)\,dt + \sigma S_t \partial_S V\,dW_t\). Subtract \(\Delta_t(\mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t)\).

Worked answer

(a) Eliminating the noise \(dW_t\): Expanding \(dV(t, S_t)\) by Box P37 and subtracting \(\Delta_t\,dS_t\) gives

\[d\Pi_t = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \bigl(\mu - \mu\Delta_t\bigr)S_t + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_t^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt + \sigma S_t\!\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta_t\right)dW_t.\]

Choosing the delta-hedge \(\Delta_t = \frac{\partial V}{\partial S}(t, S_t)\) zeroes out the \(dW_t\) coefficient — and simultaneously cancels the drift terms \(\mu S_t \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \mu S_t \Delta_t = 0\)!

(b) The Black–Scholes PDE: With \(\Delta_t = \partial_S V\), the remaining change in the portfolio is purely deterministic: \(d\Pi_t = \bigl(\partial_t V + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S_t^2 \partial_{SS} V\bigr)dt\). Equating this to the risk-free return \(r\Pi_t\,dt = r\bigl(V - S_t \partial_S V\bigr)dt\) yields

\[\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + r S \frac{\partial V}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0.\]

The physical drift \(\mu\) disappears because both the option \(V(t, S_t)\) and the underlying stock \(S_t\) are driven by the exact same Brownian coin \(dW_t\) in fixed ratio \(\partial_S V\); cancelling \(dW_t\) automatically cancels \(\mu\,dt\), leaving only the quadratic-variation convexity cost \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \partial_{SS} V\).
