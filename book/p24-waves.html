The Patterns Atlas · P24 · the seventh move of tier 2

Split into waves

An organ builder needs a square wave — the raw, reedy register — and the factory manual gives a recipe that cannot possibly work: add pure tones, one dial per odd frequency, each dial set to the reciprocal of its frequency — \(\sin x + \tfrac{1}{3}\sin 3x + \tfrac{1}{5}\sin 5x + \tfrac{1}{7}\sin 7x + \cdots\). Smooth plus smooth is smooth, the builder objects, and a wall is not smooth: an endless sum of gentle wiggles should wiggle, not jump. The partial sums, computed honestly, answer the objection in public: at every point strictly inside the room the sums settle — to a constant, \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) — and the wall assembles itself out of pure smoothness, the one shape the builder was sure could not be built. This page makes the move deliberate, in both directions: given the complicated object, compute its waves — each dial setting is one definite integral, legal because distinct waves are orthogonal, their products cancelling to nothing over the room — and given the waves, recombine, then answer the hard questions wave by wave, where everything is simple. Three runs in public: the square wave split end to end (its dial table reads \(1, 0, \tfrac{1}{3}, 0, \tfrac{1}{5}\) — the wave content, the object's fingerprint), the parabola \(x^2\) split into cosines (the carried reading at \(x = \pi\) harvesting the Basel sum \(\sum \tfrac{1}{n^2} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\), the most famous hard sum in the school world falling out of a dial computation), and the wave move where no integral ever appears — the roots-of-unity filter, summing binomial coefficients by residue class with waves on the cyclic room \(\mathbb{Z}/3\), \(\tfrac{1}{3}\!\left(2^{12} + 2\cos\tfrac{12\pi}{3}\right) = 1366\) against a hand count of the same 1366. The honest prices, paid in full: at jumps the split reads the midpoint and noisily overshoots on approach — the Gibbs overshoot, about 9% of the jump, refusing to die — the room's waves carry the room's boundaries, and the split is blind to single points, seeing averages only. Prerequisites: P05 (the basis a theory appoints), P15 (walls that trap an answer), P16 (the same problem in different clothes). Next: the census — P25, where every table this page prints becomes one sweep of a machine.

New word: frequency. A frequency is how many full cycles a wave completes across the room: \(\sin x\) is frequency 1 on \([- \pi, \pi]\), \(\sin 3x\) is frequency 3. Musicians call frequency 1 the fundamental and every whole multiple of it a harmonic — the organ builder's dial for frequency \(m\) is the wave \(\sin mx\). New word: orthogonal. Two waves are orthogonal when their product integrates to zero over the room: \(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx = 0\) unless \(m = n\). Orthogonal waves do not mix: each wave's dot product with an object measures that wave's share alone, exactly as perpendicular measuring rods read different directions — B06's dictionary, worn for functions. New word: spectrum. The spectrum of an object is the table of its wave shares, one number per frequency — the dial settings of the factory manual's recipe, read as a table. The spectrum is the same object wearing different clothes: P16's whole promise, at the scale of functions.

1 · Before we begin

The reader arrives owning three tools and one unpaid debt. From P05: the canonical form — a theory appoints the basis in which its objects are simplest, and the proof is done once, in that basis, for all; surds wear square-free radicands, residues wear \(0\) to \(n-1\). From P16: the dictionary — the same object reads differently in two schemes, the readings translate perfectly, and a problem hopeless in one scheme may be already solved in the other. From P15: the walls — two computable things that trap an unknown answer between them, with the gap shrinking to nothing. The debt: at the end of P23 we executed a phantom. The sine that passed every integer row and read anything at the half-integers was declared a spoiler of uniqueness, killed by decree of the shape demand. P23 promised it a promotion: the wave this page is built on is that same sine, promoted from forger to instrument.

Here is the promotion. P23 needed a function that could hide inside tables; we need objects that can be built out of waves, and the waves are the sine's whole family: \(\sin x\), \(\sin 2x\), \(\sin 3x\), \(\dots\), the cosines, and one constant — a fixed, countable rack of simple shapes, every one of them smooth, every one of them periodic. The claim this page makes and pays for: a very large class of complicated objects — jagged signals, awkward sums, functions with corners and even jumps — are exactly sums over this rack, with computable dial settings; that the dial settings are computable is a gift of cancellation, the orthogonality that keeps every wave's measurement clean of every other wave's interference; and that the split pays is the point: questions that strangle in the object's own clothes — what does it weigh? which piece carries the energy? what does the operator do to it? — fall apart into questions about single waves, each answered in one line.

The runner is the organ builder's square wave, and the object of this § is to meet it twice: once as a sum, in §2, where the surprise is that smoothness can build a wall; and once as an object, in §4, where the surprise is that the wall confesses its dial settings to two definite integrals. Between the two meetings, §3 names the move and the machine that runs it.

2 · A recipe that cannot possibly work

The electric-organ builder needs a square wave — the signal that jumps between two levels and holds each one flat, the raw buzz behind every reedy register an organ owns. Building a jump electronically is awkward; holding two hundred frequencies steady is easy. So the factory manual gives a recipe instead of a circuit: mount one dial per odd frequency, set each dial to the reciprocal of its frequency, and add the pure tones: \[ \sin x \;+\; \tfrac{1}{3}\sin 3x \;+\; \tfrac{1}{5}\sin 5x \;+\; \tfrac{1}{7}\sin 7x \;+\; \cdots \] The builder reads it twice and objects. Every tone on that rack is smooth — \(\sin 3x\) is as gentle as \(\sin x\), just faster. A finite sum of smooth things is smooth. The target is a wall, and a wall is the least smooth shape there is. The recipe cannot possibly work, and the manual's word for the sum's target is an ellipsis — no help at all.

Compute honestly, the way P02 taught: partial sums first, at points chosen for information. At \(x = \tfrac{\pi}{2}\) the tones alternate exactly — \(\sin\tfrac{(2k+1)\pi}{2} = (-1)^k\) — so the partial sums there are the alternating series of the odd reciprocals:

Three cold questions, asked before any technique is offered, because they are the ones the move must answer:

First — how can smoothness build a wall? The builder's objection was correct as far as it went: any finite sum of smooth waves is smooth, and a wall is not. The table's answer is that the recipe is not a finite sum: it is a limit, and limits of smooth things are not smooth in general — they are only what the squeezing forces them to be. Here the walls of P15 force every point strictly inside a half-period to \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), and force nothing at the jump itself, where the sums read \(0\) at every stage. The limit is flat on each side of \(x = 0\) and drops at \(x = 0\): a wall, built out of gentle waves by taking infinitely many of them. The ellipsis was doing structural work all along.

Second — was the recipe unique? The wall is the sum of the recipe; is it also the sum of some other recipe? The question is a legality demand in P05's exact format, and the answer has a price: the split of an object is unique up to what the measurement cannot see, and what it cannot see is smaller than a point — but not nothing. §5's third exhibit shows an object and a second object, differing at a single point, whose dial settings are identical to the last digit. Uniqueness here is honest but priced: the waves determine the object's averages, and averages do not read points.

Third — where did the dial settings come from? The manual's recipe — reciprocals of the odd frequencies, zeros on the even ones — arrived by fiat. Nobody tuned those dials; the manual's author did not say how the settings were found, and that omission is the whole of what this page adds. Run the encounter backwards: given the wall as the object — the square wave, say \(f(x) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) on \((0, \pi)\) and \(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) on \((-\pi, 0)\) — compute the dial settings from scratch. If the settings come out \(1, 0, \tfrac{1}{3}, 0, \tfrac{1}{5}, \dots\), then the move is a machine and not a magic recipe: any object, any rack of waves, the settings computed the same way, once, for all.

The move this page teaches is exactly that machine, and the reader who owns P16 already knows its shape: the wall and its dial table are the same object in two sets of clothes, and the translation between the clothes is honest in both directions — one direction is §2 (waves recombine to the object), the other is §4 (the object splits into waves). §3 builds the machine; §5 prices it.

3 · Name the move

P24 · Split into waves — rewrite the complicated object as a sum of simple waves; work wave by wave; recombine

When an object is complicated as a whole — a jagged signal, an awkward sum, a function with corners or jumps — but the problem's arithmetic treats the object piecewise — sums, scaling, averaging, integration pass through a sum term by term — do not attack the whole. Instead: choose the room the object lives in and the rack of waves that fits it — sines and cosines of whole frequencies on a period, or the roots of unity on a cyclic room; compute the dial settings — each wave's share of the object is one dot product, legal because distinct waves are orthogonal, their products cancelling to nothing over the room; then work wave by wave — every question the problem asks of the object becomes, under the split, the same question asked of each wave alone, where the answer is one line — and recombine. What the move buys: a dictionary between two sets of clothes for the same object — the object's own coordinates and its wave coordinates — with every hard question translated to the side where it is easy.

In words, the move has three working parts, and the page keeps saying which part is doing the work. First, the room and its rack: waves only make sense in a room — a period, a boundary, a cyclic set — and the rack must fit the room: \(\sin x\), \(\sin 2x\), \(\dots\) and the cosines on a full period, only the sines on a room with two pinned walls, the \(n\)-th roots of unity on the cyclic room \(\mathbb{Z}/n\). P05's sentence again, verbatim: the theory appoints the basis. Second, the extraction machine: the dial settings are dot products, and the dot products are clean because of cancellation — this is the part that is genuinely new, and Box P24.A builds it. Third, the wave-by-wave work and the recombination: the question must be one that passes through sums — P16's dictionary only pays when the two schemes carry the same arithmetic, and here the arithmetic that carries is linearity.

Box P24.A · The extraction machine — orthogonality and the dial recipes

Give functions a dot product, borrowed from the integral the reader already owns: \[ \langle f, g\rangle \;=\; \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\,g(x)\,dx. \] The waves of the room are mutually perpendicular under it. The whole proof is the product-to-sum identity of class 11 plus one integral: \(2\sin(mx)\sin(nx) = \cos\bigl((m{-}n)x\bigr) - \cos\bigl((m{+}n)x\bigr)\), and \(\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(kx)\,dx = 0\) for every whole \(k \ge 1\) because a cosine finishes whole cycles in that room. So: \[ \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(mx)\sin(nx)\,dx \;=\; 0 \text{ unless } m = n, \qquad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx \;=\; 0 \text{ unless } m = n, \qquad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx \;=\; 0 \text{ always}, \] and when \(m = n\) the two like-waved integrals read \(\pi\) — the wave's own weight. (When \(m = n\) the first product-to-sum term is \(\cos 0 = 1\), and half of \(\int 1\,dx = 2\pi\) is \(\pi\): one number, no mystery.) Sines never speak to cosines; distinct frequencies never speak at all.

Cancellation, seen once: the product \(\sin x\sin 2x\) over the room —

Now the machine. Suppose the object is secretly a sum — say \(f = b_1\sin x + b_2\sin 2x + b_3\sin 3x\) — and the \(b\)'s are unknown. Dot both sides with \(\sin 2x\). On the left, \(\langle f, \sin 2x\rangle\), a computable integral. On the right, three integrals; the first and third die by orthogonality, and the middle one is \(b_2 \cdot \pi\). Every wave except the interrogated one cancels, so \[ b_2 \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(2x)\,dx, \] and the same interrogation of \(\sin x\), \(\sin 3x\), \(\dots\) returns \(b_1, b_3, \dots\) — one integral per dial, no interference. The general recipes, worth boxing in the memory: if \(f\) is to be worn as \[ f(x) \;=\; \frac{a_0}{2} \;+\; \sum_{n \ge 1}\Bigl( a_n\cos(nx) \;+\; b_n\sin(nx)\Bigr), \quad\text{then}\quad a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx, \;\; b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx, \] with the constant's share \(a_0/2\) extracted by dotting with the constant wave \(1\), whose weight is \(2\pi\). The reader who did B06 has seen this shape smaller: columns read in the other scheme's numbers, one dot product per column. The extraction machine is a change of basis for functions — and the dial settings it returns are the object's coordinates in the wave scheme.

Box P24.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Audio, visibly: a graphic equalizer's sliders are the dial settings of the room's waves, moved one band at a time — when the engineer cuts the 50 hertz slider, no re-recording occurs; the hum leaves the sum because it never belonged to the other waves. Compression, industrially: an image or a track is split, the small dials are dropped, and the big ones are kept — the whole JPEG and MP3 economy is a spectrum with a budget. Physics, structurally: heat in a ring splits into waves and each frequency decays at its own rate — the wave-by-wave work in its purest form, and the very problem the split was invented for. Number theory: Example 3's filter sums arithmetic progressions out of binomial sums, and the same move at research scale counts lattice points in circles. Crystallography: a diffraction pattern is a spectrum — the photograph develops the dial settings, and the crystal's structure is read back from them. And the equal-tempered organ of §2, where the whole idea was named for the harmonics of a stretched string.

The three-key signature — the checklist that says the move applies, and the checklist that says it was honestly run. (1) The object is periodic or bounded to a room — a repeating signal, a function on a closed interval, a sum over a cyclic index: the room must exist before the rack does. (2) The question passes through sums — energy, mass, expectation, decay, extraction of one residue class: each wave answers alone, and the recombination is addition. A question that cannot be asked of each wave separately — "is the object positive on Thursday?" — does not become easier in wave clothes. (3) The dot products are computable — the object's integrals, or its finite sums, must be honest work: a spectrum that cannot be computed is a conjecture wearing the move's clothes. And one diagnostic read off the answer: the dial settings' decay measures the object's smoothness — fast-dying dials mean a smooth object, slow-dying dials mean corners or jumps, and §5's first exhibit shows the slowest decay there is being paid for in public.

4 · The move at work

Three runs in public, one per species of object: a signal with a jump (the square wave, split by the extraction machine), a smooth function (the parabola, whose split pays the Basel sum), and a finite sum (the binomial coefficients, split on a cyclic room where the waves are roots of unity and no integral ever appears). Each run states the room, computes the dial settings, works wave by wave, and prices the answer.

Example 1 · The square wave's confession — the dial settings computed from the object

Moves used: P05 (the room appoints the rack: whole frequencies on a period), P08 (divide by symmetry: the object's oddness kills the cosine interrogations before they happen).

Room: \([-\pi, \pi]\), period \(2\pi\). Object: the square wave of §2 — \(f(x) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}\) on \((0, \pi)\), \(f(x) = -\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) on \((-\pi, 0)\), with the jump at \(x = 0\). Rack: the full rack, sines and cosines, before any symmetry is used.

Symmetry first, the P08 habit. The object is odd — \(f(-x) = -f(x)\) — and every cosine is even, so every cosine dot product integrates an odd thing over a symmetric room and dies without computation: \(a_n = 0\) for all \(n\), and the constant dies with them. Half the rack is gone before a single integral is run; this is what P08 means by doing one representative case and multiplying by the orbit — here, dividing it out.

The dial settings. For the sines, run the machine: \[ b_m \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f(x)\sin(mx)\,dx \;=\; \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{\pi}{4}\sin(mx)\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{2m}\bigl(1 - \cos m\pi\bigr) \;=\; \frac{1 - (-1)^m}{2m}, \] where the first equality is the oddness of \(f\sin(mx)\) (odd times odd is even, so the two halves agree) and the last is \(\cos m\pi = (-1)^m\). Read the formula: \(b_m = \tfrac{1}{m}\) for odd \(m\), and \(b_m = 0\) for even \(m\). The dial table, computed:

Reciprocals of the odd frequencies, zeros on the even ones — the factory manual's recipe, arrived at honestly: the object confessed it to ten definite integrals (two, after symmetry), no fiat anywhere. This table is the object's spectrum, and the two zeros are worth a second look: the even frequencies are absent because of symmetry, not accident — the square wave looks the same upside down after a shift of \(\pi\), and only odd-frequency waves respect that loyalty. A spectrum read carefully is a symmetry detector.

Recombine, and price it. The split says \[ f(x) \;=\; \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{\sin\bigl((2k+1)x\bigr)}{2k+1}, \] and §2's tables were this identity's convergence, audited: pointwise inside the halves, at the jump itself every term vanishes and the sum reads \(0\) — the exact midpoint of \(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\) and \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), which §5 prices as the honest rule and not a convention. What the split bought, stated once: the hard question "what is this jagged thing, exactly?" became a table of ten numbers with a pattern; the pattern (\(1/m\), odd only) is now a theorem about the object, checkable, reusable, and the input to every equalizer, filter, and compression scheme in Box P24.B's catalogue.

Example 2 · The parabola's dials — and the Basel sum harvested

Moves used: P05 (the appointed rack again), P23 (the carried reading: a formula honest at every point of a room is read at one well-chosen point, and the reading is carried back as a theorem about numbers).

Room: \([-\pi, \pi]\). Object: \(f(x) = x^2\), smooth — no jumps, no corners. Symmetry: the parabola is even, so the sine dot products all die (\(x^2\sin(nx)\) is odd), and only the cosines and the constant remain. The constant: \(a_0 = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2\,dx = \tfrac{1}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{2\pi^3}{3} = \tfrac{2\pi^2}{3}\), so the constant's dial — recall the recipe's \(a_0/2\) — reads \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}\). The cosines: integration by parts, twice, the reader's own class-12 tool, run honestly: \[ a_n \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x^2\cos(nx)\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\frac{x^2\sin(nx)}{n}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} + \frac{2}{n\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin(nx)\,dx \;=\; \frac{2}{n\pi}\left[\frac{-x\cos(nx)}{n}\right]_{-\pi}^{\pi} \;=\; \frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}, \] where the sine boundary terms vanish because \(\sin(\pm n\pi) = 0\), the cosine integral dies by orthogonality, and the two surviving boundary readings agree because \(\cos(\pm n\pi) = (-1)^n\). First, the convergence: the object is smooth and the dials die like \(1/n^2\) — fast, and this is no accident but the smoothness diagnostic of Box P24.B, running in the good direction. Second, the identity itself — that the sum equals the parabola at every point, not merely approximates it — is a limit statement of exactly the kind §2 audited; this page names its honest condition (Dirichlet's, §7) and does not pretend to prove it.

The carried reading. Now wear the P23 move on top: a formula honest across a room can be read at any point of it, and the reading carries. At \(x = \pi\), every \(\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n\), so the alternating signs in the dials cancel them: \[ \pi^2 \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{3} + \sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}(-1)^n \;=\; \frac{\pi^2}{3} + 4\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{1}{n^2}. \] Solve: \(\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{1}{n^2} = \tfrac{\pi^2}{6}\). The Basel problem — the sum of reciprocal squares, famous for a century before Euler, the flagship "hard sum" of the school world — falls out of a dial computation as a one-line carried reading. And the second reading, at \(x = 0\), costs nothing extra: the signs now survive, \(0 = \tfrac{\pi^2}{3} + 4\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\), so \(1 - \tfrac14 + \tfrac19 - \tfrac{1}{16} + \cdots = \tfrac{\pi^2}{12}\). Example 3 · Waves with no integrals — the roots-of-unity filter

Moves used: P16 (the awkward sum, re-worn in wave coordinates), P05 (the cyclic room appoints its rack: the roots of unity), with B02's congruence classes as the room itself.

The move is not chained to the integral. Its cleanest home is a finite room, and the example is a sum every binomial theorem user has met and no one wants to compute by hand: among the \(2^{12} = 4096\) subsets of a 12-element set, how many have a size divisible by 3? The direct answer — add \(\binom{12}{0} + \binom{12}{3} + \binom{12}{6} + \binom{12}{9} + \binom{12}{12}\) — is five lookups and a sum, and the reader should do it once for the receipts (it gives 1366). The question this example answers is the one the direct route cannot: what happens for general \(n\), where the lookups become a page of arithmetic.

The room and its waves. The room is the cyclic room of size 3 — B02's three teams \(k \equiv 0, 1, 2 \pmod 3\). Its waves are the three cube roots of unity: the complex numbers \[ 1, \qquad \omega = \cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3} + i\sin\tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \qquad \omega^2 = \cos\tfrac{4\pi}{3} + i\sin\tfrac{4\pi}{3}, \] class-11 polar form, nothing more. Multiplication adds angles (that is the whole of the polar form's arithmetic), so the three are exactly the numbers whose cubes are 1: powers of \(\omega\) walk a 3-cycle, \(\omega^3 = 1\). A wave of the room is a sequence that repeats with period 3, and the three pure waves are \(\omega^{jk}\) as \(k\) runs — the analogue of \(\sin(mx)\), exactly as periodic, exactly as orthogonal:

The orthogonality, discrete. Sum a wave around the room: for \(k = 0, 1, 2\), \[ 1 + \omega^k + \omega^{2k} \;=\; \begin{cases} 3 & \text{if } 3 \text{ divides } k,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise},\end{cases} \] because if \(3 \mid k\) every term is \(1\), and if not, the three terms are the three distinct roots of \(z^3 = 1\) — whose sum is zero, as \(z^3 - 1 = (z-1)(z^2+z+1)\) shows in one line. This is \(\int\sin(mx)\cos(nx) = 0\) wearing finite clothes: the wave sums to its full weight on its own team and to nothing on the others.

The filter. The binomial theorem says \((1+z)^{12} = \sum_k \binom{12}{k}z^k\) — the awkward sum wearing polynomial clothes. Feed it the three waves in turn and add, and the orthogonality does the rest: \[ \sum_{k \equiv 0 \, (3)} \binom{12}{k} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{3}\Bigl[(1+1)^{12} + (1+\omega)^{12} + (1+\omega^2)^{12}\Bigr], \] because the coefficient of \(\binom{12}{k}\) in the bracketed sum is exactly \(1 + \omega^k + \omega^{2k}\): it is 3 on the team \(k \equiv 0\), 0 on the others. Now the arithmetic, one fact at a time: Fig P24.3's parallelogram reads \(1 + \omega = \cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\tfrac{\pi}{3}\), a unit-circle number at angle \(60^\circ\), so \((1+\omega)^{12}\) is the unit number at angle \(12 \times 60^\circ = 720^\circ\) — a full double turn — which is \(1\); its conjugate \(1 + \omega^2\) at \(-60^\circ\) gives the same \(1\). So \[ \sum_{k \equiv 0 \, (3)} \binom{12}{k} \;=\; \tfrac{1}{3}\bigl(4096 + 1 + 1\bigr) \;=\; \tfrac{4098}{3} \;=\; 1366. \] The same answer the five lookups gave — and the filter route is the one that scales: replace 12 by any \(n\), 3 by any modulus \(m\), and the same two lines run. The reader who wants the general closed form finds it in Exercise 3, where the same filter, aimed at the other two teams, splits \(2^{12}\) into \(1366 + 1365 + 1365\) — three teams, three spectra readings, one census.

Price it. The filter answered a question about one team out of three by consulting all of \(2^{12}\) terms — wave questions are global questions, as §5's third exhibit says twice. And the complex numbers, though they carry the computation, cancel out of the answer: the sum is an integer, the waves were scaffolding. The move often works exactly this way — the wave clothes are worn for the work and taken off for the answer, and only the answer's honesty survives the undressing.

5 · When it fails — the jump, the wrong room, and the blind spot

The split is honest only where the waves are honest, and this section shows where the waves fall short of the law: three exhibits — a jump the split reads halfway forever, a room whose walls the split rewrites without asking, and a blind spot for everything that lives at a single point. None is a footnote; the first carries a name from the history books, the second is the price of the room's very structure, and the third is the boundary between this page and the local questions P42 will ask. The move's honesty lives here, not in §4.

Exhibit A · The jump — the split reads the midpoint forever, and overshoots on the way. Return to Example 1's square wave, honest everywhere on the open stretch: partial sums close to \(\pi/4\) pointwise, dials read one line of the spectrum. At the wall \(x = 0\) the law itself leaps — from \(-\pi/4\) on the left to \(+\pi/4\) on the right, a jump of \(\pi/2\) — and the split cannot follow. Every wave in the sine rack reads \(0\) at the wall (\(\sin(m \cdot 0) = 0\)), so every partial sum, at every \(N\), reads \(0\): exactly the average of the two sides. This is the midpoint rule, and it is the honest reading — no finite recombination of waves can read a jump any other way — but it means the split's answer at a jump is a compromise the law never made.

Worse happens just to the right of the wall, and the numbers should be watched, not believed. The partial sums of Example 1 overshoot the target \(\pi/4 \approx 0.7854\): \(S_5\) climbs to \(0.9286\), \(S_{50}\) to \(0.9260\), \(S_{500}\) to \(0.9260\) — and the peak does not die with growing \(N\); it slides toward the wall, cresting at exactly \(\pi/(2N)\) (\(0.314\), then \(0.031\), then \(0.003\)) while holding its height, which tends to \(\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Si}(\pi) \approx 0.9260\). The overshoot above the target holds at \(\approx 0.1406\) — \(8.95\%\), about 9%, of the full jump (\(9.12\%\) already at \(N = 5\)) — and no depth of the split ever shrinks it. The convergence of §2 is pointwise, and at a jump the points close in from both sides while the peak slips between them.

The overshoot has a history worth one paragraph, because it is the history of watching, not computing. Henry Wilbraham analyzed it in 1848 — and the reading was forgotten for sixty-six years, until Burkhardt's 1904–1914 encyclopedic review of the subject's first century gathered the stray pages under one roof. In 1898 Josiah Willard Gibbs, answering A. E. H. Love in a short exchange of letters printed in Nature, guessed the limit wrong first and corrected himself a week later in a second note: the overshoot does not vanish, it sharpens. The phenomenon carries Gibbs's name because the second note was clearer than Wilbraham's paper, which is Stigler's law doing its usual work, and Carslaw's 1925 monograph — the book that finally taught the subject to engineers — closes the file. The moral for the page: the overshoot is invisible to the coefficient dial readings of §3 and only visible to someone who evaluates the recombined sum near the jump and looks. The audit step of the default habit exists for exactly this class of surprise.

The repair, and its price. Lipót Fejér, 1904: average the partial sums themselves — take \(\sigma_N\), the mean of \(S_0, S_1, \dots, S_N\), a weighted recombination that damps the newest waves. The overshoot dies: \(\sigma_{50}\) and \(\sigma_{200}\) stay strictly below the target \(\pi/4\) on the whole approach run (their maxima over \(0 < x \le 0.3\) read \(0.7616\) and \(0.7792\)), the wall still reads its midpoint \(\sigma_{200}(0) = 0\) exactly, and the convergence near the jump is honest from below. But the averaging that kills the overshoot also blurs the target: at \(x = 0.02\), \(\sigma_{200}\) reads \(0.7139\) where the law says \(\pi/4 \approx 0.7854\) and the raw sum \(S_{200}\) reads \(0.7871\). Fejér trades Gibbs's overshoot for a slower approach — undershoot, paid in flatness — and neither reading is free. The choice between \(S_N\) and \(\sigma_N\) is not a technicality; it is the choice between honest wiggles and honest blur, and the page that uses the split for a claim near a jump must say which it is carrying.

Exhibit B · The wrong room — the constant that the sine room hears as a square wave. Split the simplest law there is: the constant \(1\) on \((0, \pi)\). Nothing could be less wavy — no oscillation, no jump inside the room — and yet the sine rack answers with dials \(b_m = \frac{4}{\pi m}\) for odd \(m\): \(1.2732\), then \(0.4244\), then \(0.2546\), a spectrum decaying like \(1/m\), the signature of a law with a jump. Every dial is Example 1's dial scaled by \(\frac{4}{\pi}\). The room is telling the truth about a law the splitter never wrote: the sine waves are odd and \(2\pi\)-periodic, so the split they recombine to is the law's odd periodic extension — constant \(1\) on \((0, \pi)\), constant \(-1\) on \((-\pi, 0)\), leaping between them at the walls, forever in both directions. The walls read \(0\) — the midpoint again — and inside the room the recombination converges to \(1\), slowly and with Exhibit A's overshoot at the edges: at the room's center \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) the partial sums read \(1.0631\) at \(N = 5\), \(0.9936\) at \(N = 50\), \(0.9994\) at \(N = 500\) — bracketing \(1\) from both sides, Leibniz-slow.

The lesson is structural, not cosmetic: the room's waves carry the room's boundaries. A split is never a statement about the law on the interval alone; it is a statement about the periodic object the interval becomes when the waves are put on. Choose the wrong rack — sines for a law the odd extension would tear — and the tear enters the spectrum as slow \(1/m\) decay and Exhibit A's overshoot at every wall. Choose the matching rack (the cosine rack, for this law: one dial \(a_0\), everything else \(0\)) and the same law splits in one line. The spectrum's decay rate is therefore read with care: fast decay certifies smoothness of the extended law, and slow decay may be the room's doing, not the law's.

Exhibit C · The blind spot — two laws with one spectrum. Change a law at a single point — raise it to a spike of height \(100\) at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\), leave every other input alone — and ask the split to tell the difference. It cannot. Every dial reading is an integral, and a spike of height \(100\) sitting on a cell of width \(w\) contributes at most \(100 \times w\) to any of them: \(10\) when \(w = 0.1\), \(1\) when \(w = 0.01\), \(0.1\) when \(w = 0.001\) — and as the spike narrows to the point it actually is, the contribution narrows to \(0\). Both laws read identical dials, hence identical recombination, at every depth of the split: the spectra match exactly, and the \(100\) is nowhere in the answer. The left-sum check makes the blindness vivid: two left sums for the two laws differ by exactly \(100w\), a difference that survives every refinement the split can afford and dies only where the integral lives, in the limit the split's equality already took.

The midpoint rule of Exhibit A is this same blindness seen from the other side: the two walls of a jump differ only where the spectrum cannot look, so the split reads their average and calls it a day. And the blindness generalizes: two laws equal except on any finite set of points — or on any set short enough that no integral can feel it — have the same spectrum, the same recombination, the same split. The move is honest about laws up to what integration can see, and integration cannot see points. A question that lives at a point, or in a window the waves cannot resolve, is a question for a different instrument: the dyadic decomposition of P42, which zooms into the window instead of averaging over the room. Until that page arrives, the rule stands: the split is global, and global tools must not be quoted at local questions.

Scope and price of P24

What the move gives. Given a law on a room with a natural wave rack — periodic laws on intervals, cyclic rooms of any finite size — the dial readings are computable one at a time by the extraction machine (a dot product each), the recombination converges to the law pointwise wherever the law is smooth (Dirichlet, 1829), and the spectrum is a certificate: identities fall out by reading the split at well-chosen points, teams are filtered by orthogonality, and decay rates diagnose the extended law's smoothness.

What the move costs, and cannot not cost. First: convergence theorems are stated, not proved — Dirichlet's conditions are named, and the page's authority for "the recombination reads the law" is a theorem the reader meets in a rigorous analysis course, not a proof included here. Second: jumps are read halfway (the midpoint rule), overshoot persists near them (Gibbs, ~9% of the full jump, at every depth), and repairs trade overshoot for undershoot (Fejér). Third: the room's waves carry the room's boundaries — the split speaks for the periodic (or cyclic) extension, and a wrong rack writes tears into the spectrum that the law never had (Exhibit B). Fourth: the split is blind to anything integration cannot feel — point values, spikes, every local detail — and two laws differing at a point are one law to the spectrum (Exhibit C). Fifth: the split is a limit — infinitely many waves in principle, finitely many in practice — and every finite truncation carries its wiggles.

When not to use it. When the object is already simple — a constant, a polynomial, a single sine: the split re-describes what one line states, and the cosine rack's one-dial answer to Exhibit B's constant is the whole argument. When the question is local — at a point, in a small window, near a spike: the spectrum cannot hear it (Exhibit C), and the honest instrument is the dyadic door of P42. And when the room has no natural waves — no periodicity, no cycle, no symmetry appointing a rack: the extraction machine still runs, but the dials answer a question no one asked, and the recombination's edge effects will be the room's, not the law's.

6 · What next

The doors this page opens

The nearest door: P25 · Check every case by machine. This page's audit step — recombine, evaluate, watch — was run by hand on numbers small enough to print: partial sums at three depths, filter counts at \(n = 12\), bracketing checked to \(N = 200\). Every one of those checks is finite, and finitely many finite checks are exactly what a machine sweep reads whole. P25 turns the audit into a census: the bracketing verified at every \(N\) up to a million, the filter formula against direct counts for every \(n\) up to the machine's patience. But the sweep reads cases, never limits — the convergence claims of this page (pointwise, at jumps, in the Fejér mean) are statements about infinity that a sweep can only sample, and P25 is the honest instrument for that boundary too.

The working door: P36 · Count with characters. Example 3's filter is a group-theory theorem wearing arithmetic clothes: the three cube roots are the characters of the cyclic group of order 3, the orthogonality \(1 + \omega^k + \omega^{2k} = 0\) is character orthogonality, and the team-splitting of \(2^{12}\) into \(1366 + 1365 + 1365\) is a census of orbits. P36 grows the room: any group of symmetries, not just a bare cycle, appoints its own rack of characters, and the same extraction machine — dot the object against each wave, read the dial, filter the team — counts the objects that respect the symmetry. The reader who felt Example 3 was the page's cleanest run has already understood the move's largest setting.

The far door: P42 · Dyadic decomposition. This page's split is global — every dial reads the whole room, which is why jumps are averaged and points are invisible (Exhibit C). The research frontier inherits both the power and the blindness, and its repair is a different split: not into waves of different frequencies, but into pieces of different scales — analyze what happens at each size separately, then sum the stories. P42 builds that instrument, and it is this page's direct descendant: the wave rack stays, the room gets windows, and the local questions this page refused become answerable one scale at a time.

The default habit

From this page on, the reader handed a complicated signal or an awkward sum should run, without being told: name the room and its natural rack — periodic law on an interval, cyclic structure on a set — and check the law's symmetry first, because P08's divide-by-symmetry kills half the dials before any integral is computed; extract each dial as a dot product, one at a time, and let orthogonality do the canceling; recombine and audit — evaluate the partial sums at well-chosen points and near every jump, watching for the ~9% that refuses to die; read the spectrum's decay rate as a diagnosis of the law, remembering Exhibit B's warning that the room's tears decay slowly too; and print the price with the answer — midpoint at jumps, the room's boundaries carried, points unseen. The habit takes a minute at the signal's edge and is the whole difference between a spectrum that certifies a law and one that merely describes a room.

7 · Where it came from

The idea is older than its license, and lived in hardware before it lived on paper. Greek planetary theory — Apollonius and Hipparchus, refined by Ptolemy — rode every planet's uneven loop on a sum of uniform circular motions: epicycles, waves wearing compasses. The organ builder of §2's runner is the same idea cast in brass: every stop a simple wave, every registration a sum. Additive synthesis — complicated signals are sums of simple ones — was a working craft assumption for two millennia before anyone asked what it would take to make it true.

The license was demanded by a string. Daniel Bernoulli, working on vibrating chains and rods in the 1730s and 1740s, heard the overtone series in a plucked string and claimed that its general motion was a sum of the simple modes — superposition, asserted. d'Alembert, in 1747, solved the wave equation for the vibrating string and read the general solution his own way; Euler's 1748 memoir, Sur la vibration des cordes, defended arbitrary initial shapes. The three-way argument that followed — can a curve drawn with a free hand really equal a sum of sines? — is §2's question in its birthplace, and Bernoulli's superposition claim is Example 1 run before the extraction machine existed. Nobody won, because nobody could compute the dials: the orthogonality integrals of Box P24.A were sitting in the open, unproved and unused.

Joseph Fourier supplied the claim and the machine. In his 1807 memoir on heat, and again in the prize memoir of 1811 and the 1822 book that collected them, he asserted that any function — any — splits into waves, and wrote down the dial recipes \(a_n\), \(b_n\) that this page's Box P24.A uses verbatim. The 1811 committee was skeptical — Lagrange, on it, had spent sixty years on the other side of the string argument and did not concede — and the prize was granted with the book's publication effectively discouraged. The claim, as stated, was too strong, and the honesty took another lifetime: Dirichlet, 1829, named the conditions under which the recombination actually reads the law — the page's named-but-not-proved license — and in the same era the identity now called Parseval's (presented to the Paris Academy on 5 April 1799, before Fourier's memoir, and usually credited later) showed what the dials know: the room's whole energy is the sum of the squared dial readings. Bessel, in an 1819 work growing out of planetary problems, found the same inequality independently: the first \(N\) dials capture as much of the law as \(N\) waves can, and no more.

The fine print's history was told in §5 where it belongs — Wilbraham 1848, Gibbs 1898, Burkhardt's review gathering the strays in 1914, Carslaw closing the file in 1925 — and Fejér's repair appeared in 1904, in Mathematische Annalen (volume 58, pages 51–69): average the partial sums and the overshoot dies. What the twentieth century did with the licensed move is the rest of the ladder — Parseval's identity grown into P36's characters, the split by frequency grown into P42's split by scale — and the FFT, in 1965, made the machine of this page run at planetary scale; that story, and the P25 door that audits it, is the next page's.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 · A sawtooth of your own ●○○
Split the identity law \(f(x) = x\) on the room \((-\pi, \pi)\), sine rack. (a) Before computing, say which dials must read zero, and why. (b) Compute the nonzero dials and recombine. (c) Read the split at \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and harvest a series the page has already met; read it at the wall and name the rule you get.

Hint

Integration by parts — P15's ladder to Example 2 — and then evaluate at a well-chosen point; the wall is Exhibit A's territory.

Worked

(a) The cosine dials all read zero, by oddness — the law is odd, the cosines are even, and P08 kills the whole rack before any integral is run; \(a_0\) dies the same way. (b) \(b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\) by parts: the dials read \(2, -1, \frac{2}{3}, -\frac{1}{2}, \dots\), decaying like \(1/n\) — the signature of the jump the odd periodic extension carries at the walls. So \(x = 2\sum_{m \ge 1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{m}\sin(mx)\) on the open room. (c) At \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\): \(x = \frac{\pi}{2}\) and \(\sin(m\frac{\pi}{2})\) vanishes on even \(m\), alternates on odd \(m\), so \(\frac{\pi}{2} = 2\left(1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \dots\right)\) — the Leibniz series, \(\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots\), the alternating series §2 bracketed, harvested as a dial reading. At the wall \(x = 0\): the law reads \(0\), every partial sum reads \(0\) — the two sides' average, \(\frac{\pi + (-\pi)}{2}\) — the midpoint rule again, though here the reading happens to agree with the law's honest value at the point. (The agreement is luck of the location, not a theorem about the law's value there: Exhibit C already said the spectrum cannot see points.)

Exercise 2 · The absolute value, and two Basel harvests ●●○
Split \(f(x) = |x|\) on \((-\pi, \pi)\), cosine rack. (a) Compute the dials and recombine. (b) Read the split at \(x = 0\) and harvest the odd part of Basel. (c) Combine with Example 2's harvest to get the even part, and check both numerically to six decimals.

Hint

Even law, odd rack: half the dials die by symmetry again. The dial integral for \(a_n\) is P15's — integrate by parts twice, or split the room at \(0\).

Worked

(a) \(|x|\) is even, so every \(b_n = 0\) and only the cosine rack reads. \(a_0 = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} x\,dx = \pi\), and \(a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} x\cos(nx)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\left[\frac{x\sin(nx)}{n} + \frac{\cos(nx)}{n^2}\right]_0^{\pi} = \frac{2}{\pi n^2}\left((-1)^n - 1\right)\): zero on even \(n\), \(-\frac{4}{\pi n^2}\) on odd \(n\). So \(|x| = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\cos x + \frac{\cos 3x}{9} + \frac{\cos 5x}{25} + \dots\right)\), dials decaying like \(1/n^2\) — the smoothness signature, no jumps anywhere. (b) At \(x = 0\): \(\cos(0) = 1\), so \(0 = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{\text{odd } n}\frac{1}{n^2}\), giving \(\sum_{\text{odd } n}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8} = 1.233701\). (c) Example 2 harvested the whole of Basel at \(x = \pi\): \(\sum_n \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}\). Subtract: \(\sum_{\text{even } n}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} - \frac{\pi^2}{8} = \frac{\pi^2}{24} = 0.411234\). Numerically: the odd terms \(1 + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{25} + \dots\) sum to \(1.233701\) and the even terms \(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{36} + \dots\) to \(0.411234\); the two sum to Basel's \(1.644934\), and the even sum is exactly a quarter of it — as it must be, since \(\sum_{\text{even}}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{1}{4}\sum\frac{1}{n^2}\). Two harvests, one split each, both landing on \(\pi^2\): the room pays in identities.

Exercise 3 · The other two teams ●●●
Example 3 counted the 12-subsets whose size is \(0 \pmod 3\). Do the same for the team \(k \equiv 1 \pmod 3\). (a) Count it directly from the binomial table. (b) Count it with the roots-of-unity filter, evaluated in the room's own arithmetic (no decimals). (c) Derive the closed form for any class \(r\) at any \(n\): \(\sum_{k \equiv r \, (3)}\binom{n}{k} = \frac{1}{3}\left(2^n + 2\cos\frac{(n-2r)\pi}{3}\right)\), and verify it against direct counts for \(n = 1\) through \(20\).

Hint

Example 3's filter, aimed one team over: multiply the \(\binom{n}{k}\)-term by \(1 + \omega^{k-1} + \omega^{2(k-1)}\) instead. For (b), Fig P24.3's parallelogram arithmetic gives \((1+\omega)^{12}\); for (c), write \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}\) and use class-11 polar form throughout — the three filter values always land on unit-circle numbers, and their sum is real.

Worked

(a) \(k = 1, 4, 7, 10\): \(12 + 495 + 792 + 66 = 1365\). (b) The filter: \(S = \frac{1}{3}\bigl[(1+1)^{12} + \omega^{-1}(1+\omega)^{12} + \omega^{-2}(1+\omega^2)^{12}\bigr]\), since the coefficient of \(\binom{12}{k}\) is now \(1 + \omega^{k-1} + \omega^{2(k-1)}\): it reads 3 exactly on the team \(k \equiv 1\) and 0 elsewhere. Example 3's arithmetic gives \((1+\omega)^{12} = (1+\omega^2)^{12} = 1\), so \(S = \frac{1}{3}(4096 + \omega^2 + \omega)\); the unit circle's own identity \(\omega + \omega^2 = -1\) (the two non-real cube roots sum with 1 to zero) leaves \(S = \frac{4095}{3} = 1365\) — an integer, as it must be, with the complex arithmetic cancelled to nothing. The third team reads the same, \(k \equiv 2\): \(12 + 66 + 792 + 495\) rearranged, \(\frac{4095}{3}\) again — and the census closes: \(1366 + 1365 + 1365 = 4096\), every subset on exactly one team. (c) In polar form the filter reads \(S_r = \frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=0}^{2}\omega^{-rj}(1+\omega^j)^n\). The \(j = 0\) term is \(2^n\); for \(j = 1, 2\), Fig P24.3's identity \(1 + \omega = e^{i\pi/3}\) (and its conjugate \(1 + \omega^2 = e^{-i\pi/3}\)) gives \((1+\omega^j)^n = e^{\pm in\pi/3}\), so the two terms are \(e^{i(n-2r)\pi/3} + e^{-i(n-2r)\pi/3} = 2\cos\frac{(n-2r)\pi}{3}\) — and the cosine can only read \(1, -\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\) at the three residues, which is why every team's count is within one of a third of \(2^n\). The formula, checked against direct table counts for \(n = 1\) to \(20\), never misses; and it scales where the table cannot — the same two lines give the answer at \(n = 10^6\) as at \(n = 12\).

Exercise 4 · Twenty coins, and a friend with a simulator ◆
A fair coin is flipped \(n = 20\) times. Of the \(2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576\) possible sequences, how many have a number of heads divisible by 4? A friend proposes to settle it by machine: flip 20 coins a million times, count, and report the fraction — and argues the simulation is the answer. Give the exact count, with proof, in two lines of arithmetic; then say precisely what the simulation could and could not have told the friend.

Hint

Example 3's move with a four-room cycle instead of three: the waves are the fourth roots of unity, and each is a unit-circle number in polar form — one of them, worryingly, gives \((1 - 1)^{20}\). Count that term with care.

Worked

The filter, mod 4 this time: with \(i^2 = -1\), the four waves are \(1, i, -1, -i\), and \(\sum_{j=0}^{3} i^{jk} = 4\) or \(0\) according as \(4 \mid k\) or not. So \(\sum_{4 \mid k}\binom{20}{k} = \frac{1}{4}\bigl[(1+1)^{20} + (1+i)^{20} + (1-1)^{20} + (1-i)^{20}\bigr]\). Polar form: \(1 + i = \sqrt{2}\,e^{i\pi/4}\), so \((1+i)^{20} = 2^{10}e^{i \cdot 5\pi} = -1024\); its conjugate \(1 - i = \sqrt{2}\,e^{-i\pi/4}\) gives \((1-i)^{20} = -1024\) as well; and the third term is \(0^{20} = 0\) — the care the hint flagged, since \(0^{20}\) is \(0\), not \(1\), and the term contributes nothing rather than something. So the count is \(\frac{1}{4}\bigl(2^{20} - 1024 + 0 - 1024\bigr) = \frac{1{,}048{,}576 - 2{,}048}{4} = 261{,}632\). Direct check, from the binomial table: \(1 + 4845 + 125970 + 125970 + 4845 + 1 = 261{,}632\) — the filter and the table agree. The probability is \(261{,}632 / 1{,}048{,}576 = 0.249512\), within \(0.000488\) of a quarter — and Example 3's closed-form trick explains the gap: at \(n = 20\) the residue correction is \(\frac{1}{4} \cdot 2^{11}\cos(5\pi) = -512\) counts below \(2^{18}\). What the simulation could have told the friend: a number near \(0.25\), with sampling noise around it. What it could not: the exact count, the exact probability, the deviation's closed form, the proof, and the answer's structure — the same two lines give \(n = 2000\) as easily as \(n = 20\), where a lifetime of simulated flips would not converge to the fourth decimal. P25 will make the boundary a page of its own: the machine reads cases, the wave filter reads the law.

Exercise 5 · Corner the overshoot yourself ◐
Exhibit A claimed a lot: an overshoot of about 9% of the jump that refuses to die, a peak that slides toward the wall and crests at exactly \(\pi/(2N)\), and a repair that kills the overshoot at a price. Run all three claims on Example 1's square wave, with a grid fine enough to trust, and report the numbers to four decimals. (a) Find the maximum of \(S_N\) on the stretch \((0, \pi]\) for \(N = 5, 50, 500\), and the overshoot as a fraction of the jump. (b) Locate each maximum and compare with \(\pi/(2N)\). (c) Average the partial sums (Fejér's \(\sigma_N\)) and report what happens to the overshoot, to the wall's midpoint reading, and to the reading at \(x = 0.02\).

Hint

Evaluate \(S_N(x) = \sum_{j < N}\frac{\sin((2j+1)x)}{2j+1}\) (the first \(N\) tones) on a fine grid near the wall — the crest's address is exactly \(\pi/(2N)\), so grid spacings well below that are needed before \(N = 500\) can be trusted; \(\sigma_N\) is the average of \(S_0, \dots, S_N\), and it can be built from the same evaluations.

Worked

(a) Maxima: \(S_5\) reaches \(0.9286\), \(S_{50}\) reaches \(0.9260\), \(S_{500}\) reaches \(0.9260\) — converging visibly to \(\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Si}(\pi) = 0.9260\) (where \(\operatorname{Si}(\pi) = 1.8519\), the sine integral up to \(\pi\)). Overshoot over the target \(\pi/4 \approx 0.7854\), as a fraction of the full jump \(\pi/2\): \(0.0912\), then \(0.0895\), then \(0.0895\) — about 9% of the jump, and it never dies. (b) Addresses: the peaks sit at \(0.3142\), \(0.0314\), \(0.0031\) for \(N = 5, 50, 500\) — exactly \(\pi/10\), \(\pi/100\), \(\pi/1000\): the law \(\pi/(2N)\) holds to the digit. The law is provable, not lucky: the partial sum's derivative is \(\sum_{j < N}\cos((2j+1)x) = \frac{\sin(2Nx)}{2\sin x}\), which vanishes first at \(x = \pi/(2N)\) — the crest of the first hump, exactly, and it is the global maximum at all three depths. (c) Fejér: the averaged sums never exceed the target — \(\sigma_{50}\) and \(\sigma_{200}\) top out at \(0.7616\) and \(0.7792\) on \((0, 0.3]\), both below \(\pi/4 = 0.7854\) — so the overshoot is dead; the wall still reads \(\sigma_{200}(0) = 0\) exactly, the midpoint preserved; and the price is the undershoot: \(\sigma_{200}(0.02) = 0.7139\) and \(\sigma_{200}(0.05) = 0.7575\), where the raw sum reads \(S_{200}(0.02) = 0.7871\) and the law says \(0.7854\). Exhibit A's three claims survive contact with the machine, each to four decimals — and the reader who ran the grid honestly also saw why \(N = 500\) needed a grid of a few thousand points before it would tell the truth.
