The Patterns Atlas · P39 · the ninth move of tier 4

The optimized sieve

Crossing out multiples is free; counting what survives the crossing-out is not — the honest count has \(2^k\) terms, each carrying a rounding error. When the exact bookkeeping drowns, stop counting exactly: build a square that sits above the truth, and let a minimization pick the weights.

New word: sieve. A sieve is a crossing-out procedure plus its bookkeeping: fix a list of primes \(p < z\), cross out every multiple of each, and ask what is left. The word is Eratosthenes' ancient one, and the whole subject is what the bookkeeping can honestly certify about the leftovers. New word: survivor. A survivor is an integer the sieve leaves uncrossed — a number divisible by none of the listed primes. Counting survivors is the sieve's one job. New word: majorant. A majorant is a quantity that sits pointwise above the thing you actually want: \(w(n) \ge \mathbf{1}_{\text{\(n\) is a survivor}}\) for every \(n\), no exceptions. A good majorant is easier to sum than the truth is to count — and its sum is a certified ceiling, not an estimate.

1 · Before we begin

The ladder so far: P10 taught that more objects than boxes forces a collision, and — the part this page needs — that the boxes can be congruence classes: a prime \(p\) owns \(p\) boxes, and crossing out multiples is herding integers into the boxes numbered \(0\). P30 sharpened the lens to one prime at a time: the valuation \(v_p\) measures exactly how much of \(p\) a number carries, and the \(p\)-local room is where divisibility questions become easy. This page runs the whole battery at once — every prime below a threshold, simultaneously, over a whole interval — and asks the question the single lens never faces: what can you certify when the primes are too many for exact bookkeeping?

Both parents commissioned this page by name. P30's door: "the optimized sieve (P39, planned) puts the lens to work at scale." And P10, handing over its boxes: "(P39) carries this move into number-theoretic territory where the boxes are residue classes and the objects are primes — it needs the localize-at-a-prime move (P30) first." The commission is honest about the scale of the difficulty: this is a tier-4 page, and its central trick — replacing a rigid sign-sum by an optimized square — is the kind of move that looks strange until you watch it certify a bound that the exact count cannot.

2 · The encounter

Start with the smallest honest census. Take the primes \(2, 3, 5\), cross out their multiples, and count survivors up to \(30\). By hand: \(1, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 29\) — eight of them. The exact bookkeeping also gives eight, without any listing: every survivor avoids each prime, and the counting runs by inclusion–exclusion over the eight divisors of \(30\),

\[30 \;-\; 15 \;-\; 10 \;-\; 6 \;+\; 5 \;+\; 3 \;+\; 2 \;-\; 1 \;=\; 8,\]

adding back the double-crossed, subtracting the triple-crossed. Each term is exact because each divisor of \(30\) divides \(30\) — no rounding anywhere. Push the interval to \(100\) and the rounding arrives: the same eight-term sum becomes \(100 - 50 - 33 - 20 + 16 + 10 + 6 - 3 = 26\), and only the floors — each term secretly carries an error up to \(1\) — keep it honest. Eight error terms against an interval of \(100\): a budget you can afford.

Now scale the prime list. Cross out by every prime below \(50\) — there are fifteen — and the exact sum has \(2^{15} = 32{,}768\) signed terms, each carrying a rounding error up to \(1\), against an interval of length \(2{,}500\). The error budget alone is thirteen times the thing being counted. The exact bookkeeping has not become wrong; it has become vacuous — the certificate it produces is wider than the quantity.

So truncate: keep only the small divisors, the ones a budget can afford. Five divisors of \(30\) fit inside a budget of \(6\): \(1, 2, 3, 5, 6\). Run the signs by hand at \(n = 10\) — the divisors within budget that divide \(10\) are \(1, 2, 5\), so the truncated sum gives \(1 - 1 - 1 = -1\). But the true indicator — "is \(10\) a survivor?" — is \(0\). The truncated sum is below the truth. At \(n = 30\) all five divide, giving \(1 - 1 - 1 - 1 + 1 = -1\), again below the truth. A sum that can dip below the truth cannot certify a ceiling: truncate the signs and the certificate dies, in either direction.

The repair is the page's whole idea, and it is almost embarrassingly small: square the truncated sum. Give the five divisors real weights \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3, \lambda_5, \lambda_6\) with the single anchor \(\lambda_1 = 1\), and at each \(n\) evaluate

\[w(n) \;=\; \Bigl( \sum_{\substack{d \in \{1,2,3,5,6\} \\ d \mid n}} \lambda_d \Bigr)^{2}.\]

Check the two cases by hand. A survivor shares no factor with \(30\), so only \(d = 1\) divides it: \(w(n) = \lambda_1^2 = 1\) — the square sits exactly on the truth. A crossed-out number gets the square of some real sum — and a square is never negative: \(w(n) \ge 0\), always, for every choice of the remaining weights. So \(w\) is a majorant of the survivor indicator, pointwise, no matter how the weights are chosen; the entire question of quality becomes a question of which weights make the sum of \(w\) smallest — an optimization with a real answer. Fig P39.1 shows the census that makes the contrast sharp: across all eight gcd-profiles of \(30\), the truncated sign-sum dips below the indicator exactly where the crossing-out overlaps — while the square, whatever weights it wears, never dips below zero. The rest of the page is that optimization, run honestly.

3 · Name the move

Here is the general ledger. Fix a sieve interval \(1 \le n \le X\) and a prime list \(p < z\); write \(P(z)\) for their product and \(S(X, z)\) for the count of \(n\) divisible by none of them. The exact count is an alternating sum over all squarefree \(d \mid P(z)\), with one rounding error per divisor and \(2^{\pi(z)}\) divisors. Selberg's 1947 move — this is the pattern's name — replaces the rigid signs by the anchored square:

Pattern P39 — The optimized sieve. Majorize the survivor indicator by the square of a short divisor sum — \(w(n) = \bigl( \sum_{d \mid n, \, d \le R} \lambda_d \bigr)^2\) with \(\lambda_1 = 1\) — so the inequality \(\mathbf{1}_{\text{survivor}}(n) \le w(n)\) holds pointwise for every choice of the other weights. Summing over the interval turns the total into a quadratic form in the weights,

\[\sum_{n \le X} w(n) \;=\; X \underbrace{\sum_{d_1, d_2 \le R} \frac{\lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}}{\operatorname{lcm}(d_1, d_2)}}_{Q(\lambda)} \;+\; O(R^2),\]

and then P04's move, not luck, picks the weights: the change of basis \(u_m = \sum_{m \mid d \le R} \lambda_d / d\) diagonalizes \(Q\) into \(\sum_{m \le R} \varphi(m) u_m^2\) under the single constraint \(\sum_{m \le R} \mu(m) u_m = 1\), and P15's squeeze (Cauchy–Schwarz, once) pins the minimum exactly:

\[S(X, z) \;\le\; \frac{X}{V(R)} + O(R^2), \qquad V(R) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{m \le R \\ m \mid P(z)}} \frac{\mu(m)^2}{\varphi(m)}.\]

Or, in words: never sum \(2^{\pi(z)}\) signed rounding errors. Majorize the indicator by the square of a short divisor sum, so the inequality survives truncation; diagonalize the resulting quadratic form by a totient change of basis; and let one Cauchy–Schwarz choose the weights that minimize the main term. The ceiling you get is certified — and the only price is the \(R^2\) remainder, which the budget \(R\) must be chosen to afford.

The shape of the answer deserves a pause. The main term is \(X / V(R)\), and \(V(R)\) — a sum of reciprocal totients over squarefree \(m \le R\) — grows like \(\log R\): every prime in the list contributes, through \(1/\varphi\), its fair share. And the depth has a one-line certificate against the bare logarithm — every truncated budget on this page stays below its prime horizon, so the sum ranges over every squarefree \(m \le R\): group the harmonic sum by radicals — the integers with \(\operatorname{rad}(n) = m\) contribute at most \(\frac{1}{m}\prod_{p \mid m}\bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{p} + \cdots\bigr) = \frac{1}{\varphi(m)}\) — so \(V(R) \ge \sum_{n \le R} \frac{1}{n} > \log R\). So a deeper budget buys a proportionally tighter ceiling, and the only brake is the \(R^2\) remainder swelling behind it. The whole architecture of the bound — main term in front, budget squaring behind — is visible already in the five-divisor census of §2, and the worked exemplars make the numbers honest.

4 · Worked exemplars

Exemplar 1 · Five divisors instead of eight: the census certified

Run the pattern on the §2 census, exactly. Budget \(R = 6\), divisor set \(D = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\), primes \(\{2, 3, 5\}\). The capacity is a five-term sum of reciprocal totients:

\[V \;=\; \frac{1}{\varphi(1)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(2)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(3)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(5)} + \frac{1}{\varphi(6)} \;=\; 1 + 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \;=\; \frac{13}{4}.\]

Cauchy–Schwarz then hands back the optimal weights in closed form, \(\lambda_d = \mu(d)\,\frac{d}{\varphi(d)} \cdot V_d / V\), where \(V_d\) sums \(1/\varphi(m)\) over the \(m \le R/d\) coprime to \(d\). Evaluating term by term: \(\lambda_1 = 1\), \(\lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = -\tfrac{12}{13}\), \(\lambda_5 = -\tfrac{5}{13}\), \(\lambda_6 = +\tfrac{12}{13}\). The weights also carry a built-in guarantee the remainder needs: factoring every \(m \le R\) as \(m = rk\) with \(r \mid d\) and \(\gcd(k, d) = 1\) shows \(V \ge \frac{d}{\varphi(d)} \cdot V_d\), so every optimal weight satisfies \(|\lambda_d| = \frac{d}{\varphi(d)} \cdot \frac{V_d}{V} \le 1\) — each of the \(R^2\) divisor pairs contributes at most one unit to the remainder, the hypothesis Exercise 6 takes as given. The certified ceiling on the survivor density is \(Q_{\min} = 1/V = \frac{4}{13} \approx 0.3077\), against the true density \(\frac{8}{30} = \frac{4}{15} \approx 0.2667\) — a ceiling fifteen percent above the truth, certified using five divisors instead of eight, with a bound that never dips below it. Notice the shape of the weights: not the rigid signs \(\pm 1\), but tapered amplitudes — the optimizer pulls \(\lambda_5\) toward zero (the boundary divisor is trusted less) and lets \(\lambda_6\) turn positive to cancel the double-counting on multiples of \(6\).

Exemplar 2 · The budget ladder: deeper is tighter, and full is exact

Watch the ceiling close in as the budget deepens. Sieve by \(\{2, 3\}\) with \(R = 3\): \(D = \{1, 2, 3\}\), \(V = 1 + 1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac52\), ceiling \(\tfrac25 = 0.4\), truth \(\tfrac13 \approx 0.333\). By \(\{2, 3, 5\}\) with \(R = 6\): ceiling \(\tfrac{4}{13} \approx 0.308\), truth \(\tfrac{4}{15} \approx 0.267\). By \(\{2, 3, 5, 7\}\) with \(R = 10\): the divisor set grows to \(\{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 10\}\), \(V = \tfrac{13}{4} + \tfrac{1}{\varphi(7)} + \tfrac{1}{\varphi(10)} = \tfrac{13}{4} + \tfrac16 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac{11}{3}\), ceiling \(\tfrac{3}{11} \approx 0.273\), truth \(\tfrac{8}{35} \approx 0.229\). And at full budget — all sixteen divisors of \(210\) — the capacity factors completely, \(V = \prod_{p < 7} \bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{\varphi(p)}\bigr) = \tfrac{35}{8}\), and the ceiling \(\tfrac{8}{35}\) equals the truth exactly: with no truncation there is no gap. Fig P39.2 shows the ladder — every rung a certified ceiling, none below the truth, converging to it as the budget deepens. That is the pattern's honesty in one picture: the bound never lies downward, and every extra unit of budget buys a provably tighter ceiling.

Exemplar 3 · Twin primes at census scale: two boxes per prime

Count \(n \le X\) such that both \(n\) and \(n+2\) survive — the twin-prime census. The sieve is the same machine with one changed input: how many boxes does each prime own? For \(p = 2\): \(n(n+2)\) is even only when \(n \equiv 0\), so one box, \(\omega(2) = 1\). For every odd prime: \(n \equiv 0\) or \(n \equiv -2\), two distinct boxes, \(\omega(p) = 2\). The capacity swaps each totient for the two-box analogue \(h(m)\): with the budget \(D = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\),

\[V_{\text{twin}} \;=\; 1 + 1 + 2 + \tfrac{2}{3} + 2 \;=\; \frac{20}{3}, \qquad \text{ceiling } \frac{3}{20} = 0.15 \;\text{ vs. truth } \tfrac{1}{10},\]

where the \(2\) at \(m = 3\) is the two-box signature — an odd prime's removal power is doubled, and its reciprocal capacity is doubled with it. Scale the budget up and \(V_{\text{twin}}(R)\) grows like a squared logarithm, \(\log^2 R\): two-box sieving is doubly logarithmic, which is why the certified bound on twin pairs up to \(X\) lands at order \(X/\log^2 X\) — the true conjectured order, from above. And the ceiling, run through the dyadic shells of P42, assembles into Viggo Brun's 1919 theorem: the \(j\)-th shell \(2^j \le p < 2^{j+1}\) holds at most a constant times \(2^j/j^2\) twin primes, each contributing at most \(2^{-j}\) to the reciprocal sum, so \(\sum_{p \text{ twin}} \frac{1}{p} \le C \sum_{j} \frac{1}{j^2} = \frac{C\pi^2}{6} < \infty\) — the ledger of Exercise 5. The twin reciprocals converge (to Brun's constant, \(B_2 \approx 1.902\)): a census that never once located a pair of twins certified that their reciprocals cannot diverge. What the census cannot do — a floor under the count, a proof that the pairs never run out — is the subject of §5's first exhibit, and the honest boundary of this page.

5 · When the move breaks

Exhibit A — The parity wall: a census cannot count prime factors. The optimized sieve, however deep its budget, reads only which boxes each \(n\) falls into — the divisibility census, nothing else. Now invent an adversary: the set of integers with an even number of prime factors. It fills exactly the same boxes as the primes do, to any depth the sieve can afford — and it contains no primes at all beyond the composites it shares them with. A machine that cannot distinguish one prime factor from two must certify its ceilings over both populations at once, and the standard cost is a factor of \(2\) in the prime-counting bound; worse, no lower bound for primes can survive the adversary at all. This is the parity barrier, discovered in this same tradition in 1949, and it is a wall, not a fence: pure census sieves do not climb it.

Mitigation: change the input, not the optimizer — sieve \(k\)-tuples and compare two weighted sums (the bounded-gaps story of §7), or feed in bilinear cancellation that sees beyond residue boxes.

Price: each route demands distribution information — equidistribution of the sifted set in arithmetic progressions — that the census alone cannot supply.

Default: never ask a residue-box machine to separate primes from products of two primes; check what your inputs actually see before trusting the certificate's sharpness.

Exhibit B — The remainder's budget: \(R^2\) eats the main term. The ceiling is \(X/V(R) + O(R^2)\), and the two terms race. Deepen the budget and \(V(R)\) grows only logarithmically while the remainder grows quadratically: at interval length \(Y = 10^6\), a budget of \(R = 10^3\) already spends its entire length on the remainder — \(R^2 = Y\), and the ceiling is vacuous. The classic escape is calibration, not power: on an interval of length \(Y\), choose \(R \approx \sqrt{Y}/\log Y\), where the remainder stays a vanishing fraction and the main term delivers a ceiling of the honest shape \(2Y/\log Y\) — the classical interval-bound for primes, a named door ahead. Where the interval is short or the set is thin, the budget that makes \(V(R)\) big is the same budget that drowns it, and no choice of weights repairs a mis-calibrated \(R\).

Mitigation: sieve well-factorable moduli, or prove equidistribution beyond \(R \approx \sqrt{X}\) (distribution level — the deep results of §7's last bullet live exactly here).

Price: restricting the shape of the moduli costs flexibility; proving distribution costs the hardest analytic work in the subject.

Default: set the budget from the remainder first — \(R^2 \ll X/V(R)\) — and only then ask how tight the ceiling is.

Exhibit C — The tuple that sieves itself: \(\omega(p) = p\). Apply the two-box logic of Exemplar 3 to the triple \((n, n+2, n+4)\). Modulo \(3\), the three shifts cover every residue — \(0, 2, 1\) — so every \(n\) lands in a forbidden box and \(\omega(3) = 3\): the prime owns as many boxes as there are residues, the "removal fraction" \(\omega(p)/p\) hits \(1\), and the capacity's term \(1/h(3)\) divides by zero. The sieve is not merely weak here; it is undefined, because the tuple is P10's collision turned against you: three objects, three boxes, a collision forced in advance for every single \(n\). P10 taught you to aim at collisions; this exhibit is the same anatomy with the aim reversed — the adversary forced it first.

Mitigation: check admissibility before sieving — no prime may own all its boxes — and widen the tuple's diameter until every prime leaves at least one box free: \((n, n+2, n+6)\) leaves the \(n \equiv 2\) box free and sieves honestly.

Price: a wider diameter weakens the density gains the tuple was chosen for.

Default: admissibility is step zero; a tuple that fails it has no ceiling to optimize.

One hinge deserves to be shown even though its door stays shut. This page's square certifies ceilings — how could it ever force primes to exist close together? The Maynard–Tao move (2013) keeps the square and changes the question: sieve an admissible \(k\)-tuple \((n + h_1, \dots, n + h_k)\) with the same non-negative \(w(n) = \bigl(\sum \lambda_{d_1 \cdots d_k}\bigr)^2\), and compare two of its sums over \(n \le X\) — the total weight \(S_1\), and the prime-weighted \(S_2\), which counts how many of the \(k\) shifts land on primes. A distribution-level input — primes spreading evenly over residue classes, Bombieri–Vinogradov's theorem for the large tuples, Zhang's deeper level for the small ones — pins the ratio \(S_2/S_1\) at order \(\log k\), which exceeds \(1\) once \(k\) is large enough. Then P10's pigeonhole, running backwards, does the forcing: \(S_2 > S_1\) with \(w \ge 0\) means some single \(n\) carries two prime shifts — two primes inside the tuple's span, which admissibility keeps bounded; fifty shifts within a span of \(246\) are known to suffice. The ratio computation itself is the door left open; the hinge is P10's, and the square's.

Honest scope of this page. Everything built here is an upper-bound machine: squares sit above the truth, and minima certify ceilings. Lower-bound sieving — proving survivors exist — is a different architecture (weighted \(k\)-tuple ratios, distribution levels, bilinear cancellation), and this page only names its doors: the bounded-gaps breakthroughs of §7, the almost-prime theorems that split the parity wall, and the analytic large sieve that duality-builds the same ceilings from exponential sums. None of them is built here; each is a door with its name on it. The sketch above shows the bounded-gaps hinge — the square, the ratio, the pigeonhole — while stating its distribution-level input, not proving it; the other doors keep their names only.

6 · What to try next

  • When a question is really about one prime — which prime divides what, lifting congruences, valuations — go back to the lens: P30 · Localize at a prime, and mind the two moves' division of labor: P30 answers at a prime, this page bounds across all of them at once.
  • When the boxes themselves are the story — forced collisions, residue-class pigeonholes, admissibility — the grammar is P10 · Force a collision, running forwards for existence and backwards (Exhibit C) for inadmissibility.
  • When a count over all sizes needs summing shell by shell — dyadic decomposition turns one divergent-looking sum into a convergent ledger; see P42 · Decompose by scale, the engine behind §8's twin-prime sum.
  • When the problem is additive — primes as summands, where sieves supply the minor-arc ceilings — the analytic end of the story is major and minor arcs (P38, planned): not yet on the shelf; this page's ceilings are among its standard inputs.
  • Named doors ahead, in order of appearance on this page: Brun's constant (the convergent sum of reciprocal twin primes), the interval bound \(2Y/\log Y\) for primes, Chen's almost-prime theorem, the parity problem, and the large sieve. Each door opens onto the research shelf; each is named so you can find it when you need it. The interval-bound door is this page's own theorem: at \(R = \sqrt{Y}/\log Y\) the certified ceiling \(Y/V(R) + R^2\) is at most \(\frac{Y}{\log R} + \frac{Y}{\log^2 Y}\), and §3's radical argument makes \(\log R = \tfrac{1}{2}\log Y - \log\log Y\), so the ceiling reads \(\frac{2Y}{\log Y}\) to first order — Brun–Titchmarsh, earned from the box.

7 · Lineage interlude

From a parchment stencil to a quadratic form (c. 240 BCE – today).

  • Eratosthenes (c. 240 BCE): the stencil — cross out multiples, keep what remains. A procedure, no bookkeeping: fine for finding primes, useless for certifying counts.
  • Legendre (1808): the stencil becomes an exact formula — the alternating divisor sum with its \(2^{\pi(z)}\) terms — and the counting problem is born, together with its rounding-error plague.
  • Viggo Brun (1915–1919): the first honest truncation — keep the inclusion–exclusion running to a bounded number of prime factors per divisor, and the signs stay under control long enough to prove the twin-prime reciprocal sum converges: the first real theorem about twins in two millennia, and the birth of the truncation budget.
  • Atle Selberg (1947): the square. Majorize the indicator by \((\sum \lambda_d)^2\), diagonalize the quadratic form, minimize — truncation without sign-indefiniteness, weights chosen by optimization. Two years later, with Erdős, sieve weights powered the first elementary proof of the prime number theorem; and the same tradition's 1949 parity observation — this page's Exhibit A — drew the wall that pure censuses still do not climb.
  • Chen (1966–1973) and Iwaniec (1978–1980): the wall is outflanked, not climbed — Chen splits it with a switched sieve to prove every large even number is prime-plus-almost-prime; Iwaniec's bilinear sieve feeds the machine cancellation that no census contains.
  • Zhang, Maynard, Tao (2013): the modern dividend — \(k\)-tuple weights plus a new distribution level prove gaps between consecutive primes are bounded: the sieve, optimized and fed, certifies existence at last. The bound has fallen from millions to a few hundred and continues to fall.

8 · Exercises

●○○ ●●○ ●●● ◆ ◐ ◐

Exercise 1 ●○○ — Run the pattern at the smallest honest scale. Sieve by \(\{2, 3\}\) with budget \(R = 3\), so \(D = \{1, 2, 3\}\). (a) Compute the capacity \(V = \sum_{m \in D} 1/\varphi(m)\) and the ceiling \(Q_{\min} = 1/V\); compare with the true density \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac13\). (b) Recover the optimal weights from the diagonal coordinates \(u_m = \mu(m)/(\varphi(m) V)\) via \(\lambda_d = d \sum_{k\,:\, dk \in D} \mu(k)\, u_{dk}\), and confirm \(\lambda_1 = 1\). (c) Verify \(Q(\lambda) = \sum_{d_1, d_2 \in D} \lambda_{d_1}\lambda_{d_2}/\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2)\) equals your \(Q_{\min}\) exactly.

Hint

\(\varphi(1) = \varphi(2) = 1\), \(\varphi(3) = 2\); since \(6 \notin D\), for \(d = 2\) and \(d = 3\) the inversion sum has only the \(k = 1\) term.

Worked answer

(a) \(V = 1 + 1 + \frac12 = \frac52\), so \(Q_{\min} = \frac25 = 0.4\), against truth \(\frac13 \approx 0.333\) — a ceiling twenty percent above, from three divisors instead of four. (b) \(u_1 = \frac{2}{5}\), \(u_2 = -\frac{2}{5}\), \(u_3 = -\frac{1}{5}\). Then \(\lambda_1 = u_1 - u_2 - u_3 = \frac{2}{5} + \frac{2}{5} + \frac{1}{5} = 1\) ✓; \(\lambda_2 = 2u_2 = -\frac45\); \(\lambda_3 = 3u_3 = -\frac35\). (c) \[Q = 1 + \frac{(4/5)^2}{2} + \frac{(3/5)^2}{3} - \frac45 + 2\cdot\frac{(-3/5)}{3} + \frac{(4/5)(3/5)}{6}\cdot 2\] — collecting over \(25\): \(25 + 8 + 3 - 20 - 10 + 4 = 10\), so \(Q = \frac{10}{25} = \frac25 = Q_{\min}\) ✓. The minimizer certified by Cauchy–Schwarz is the true minimizer.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — Check the majorant, profile by profile. With \(D = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\) and the weights of Exemplar 1, the value \(w(n) = (\sum_{d \in D, d \mid n} \lambda_d)^2\) depends only on \(\delta = \gcd(n, 30)\). (a) Compute \(w\) for all eight profiles \(\delta \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30\}\) and verify \(w(\delta) \ge \mathbf{1}_{\delta = 1}\) in every one. (b) Each profile occurs \(\varphi(30/\delta)\) times per period of \(30\); verify the period average \(\frac{1}{30}\sum_{\delta \mid 30} \varphi(30/\delta)\, w(\delta) = \frac{4}{13}\), the certified density itself.

Hint

For each profile, sum the weights of the divisors in \(D\) that divide \(\delta\), then square: e.g. at \(\delta = 10\), \(1 - \frac{12}{13} - \frac{5}{13} = -\frac{4}{13}\), and the square is \(\frac{16}{169}\). For (b), put everything over \(169\) and multiply by the profile counts \(8, 8, 4, 2, 4, 2, 1, 1\).

Worked answer

(a) \(w(1) = 1\); \(w(2) = w(3) = w(6) = \frac{1}{169}\); \(w(5) = \frac{64}{169}\); \(w(10) = w(15) = w(30) = \frac{16}{169}\). All are \(\ge 0\), and \(w(1) = 1\) exactly — the majorant sits on the truth at survivors and above it (trivially, by non-negativity) everywhere else. (b) \[\frac{8(169) + 8(1) + 4(1) + 2(64) + 4(1) + 2(16) + 1(16) + 1(16)}{30 \cdot 169} = \frac{1352 + 8 + 4 + 128 + 4 + 32 + 16 + 16}{5070} = \frac{1560}{5070} = \frac{4}{13}.\] The average of the majorant over one full period is exactly the certified ceiling — the quadratic form's value at the optimizer, computed by pure census.

Exercise 3 ●●● — Sieve for twins at the census scale. To count \(n\) with \(n, n+2\) both surviving, the box counts are \(\omega(2) = 1\) and \(\omega(p) = 2\) for odd \(p\), and the capacity's per-prime factor becomes \(1/h(p) = \frac{\omega(p)}{p - \omega(p)}\). (a) Compute \(1/h(m)\) for each \(m \in D = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 6\}\). (b) Compute \(V_{\text{twin}}\) and the ceiling \(Q_{\text{twin}} = 1/V_{\text{twin}}\), and compare with the exact local density \(\frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} \cdot \frac{3}{5} = \frac{1}{10}\). (c) Verify that the full budget — all eight divisors — recovers \(Q_{\text{twin}} = \frac{1}{10}\) identically, via the factorization \(V = \prod_{p < 7} (1 + 1/h(p))\).

Hint

\(1/h\) is multiplicative: \(1/h(6) = (1/h(2))(1/h(3))\). For (c), \((1 + 1/h(2))(1 + 1/h(3))(1 + 1/h(5))\).

Worked answer

(a) \(1/h(1) = 1\); \(1/h(2) = \frac{1}{2-1} = 1\); \(1/h(3) = \frac{2}{3-2} = 2\); \(1/h(5) = \frac{2}{5-2} = \frac23\); \(1/h(6) = 1 \cdot 2 = 2\). (b) \(V_{\text{twin}} = 1 + 1 + 2 + \frac23 + 2 = \frac{20}{3}\), so \(Q_{\text{twin}} = \frac{3}{20} = 0.15\) — a ceiling fifty percent above the truth \(\frac{1}{10}\), from five of eight divisors. (c) \(V_{\text{full}} = (1+1)(1+2)(1+\frac23) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac53 = 10\), so \(Q = \frac{1}{10}\) — the exact density, no truncation, no gap: the two-box sieve's Euler product, met exactly like the budget ladder's last rung.

Exercise 4 ◆ — Diagnose, then bound: "how many twin pairs are there up to \(X\), and does this page's move prove there are infinitely many?" (a) Say what the sieve inputs are (the set, the boxes, \(\omega\)), and what ceiling the pattern certifies for the count \(\pi_2(X)\), using Exercise 3's capacity shape \(V_{\text{twin}}(R) \asymp \log^2 R\) with the calibration of Exhibit B. (b) Explain — via Exhibit A — why the same machine cannot certify a positive lower bound for primes or twin pairs, and name what extra input the modern lineage (§7's last bullet) fed it to prove bounded gaps anyway.

Worked answer

(a) The set is \(\{n(n+2) : n \le X\}\), the boxes are the residue classes \(0\) and \(-2\) (one box at \(p = 2\)), and the certified ceiling is \(\pi_2(X) \le (X + R^2)/V_{\text{twin}}(R)\); with \(V_{\text{twin}}(R)\) growing like \(\log^2 R\) and \(R\) calibrated so \(R^2 \ll X/\log^2 X\), this is \(\pi_2(X) \ll X/\log^2 X\) — the conjectured true order, certified from above. (b) The parity wall: a machine reading only residue boxes cannot distinguish one prime factor from two, and the even-parity adversary population fills the same boxes with no primes in it — so any census-certified lower bound for twins must survive on a set that could be prime-free, forcing it to zero. The modern route does not climb the wall: it changes the population, sieving \(k\)-tuples and comparing two weighted sums so that some tuple member being prime is forced by a ratio exceeding \(1\) — plus a distribution level for the sifted set beyond what the census alone provides. Diagnosis: this page bounds twins from above at the true order; existence needed a different question, asked of \(k\) tuples at once.

Exercise 5 ◐ — Brun's ledger, by shells. Suppose a certified ceiling says the interval \([2^j, 2^{j+1})\) holds at most \(C \cdot 2^j / j^2\) twin pairs, for some constant \(C\) and all \(j \ge 1\). (a) Show the reciprocal sum over all twin primes converges, bounded by \(K \sum_{j \ge 1} 1/j^2\) for a constant \(K\) you should identify in terms of \(C\). (b) Evaluate \(\sum_{j \ge 1} 1/j^2\) numerically to three decimals and explain, in one sentence each, (i) why the shell decomposition makes the divergent-looking sum converge and (ii) why the analogous sum over all primes diverges anyway.

Hint

Each twin prime \(p\) in shell \(j\) contributes at most \(1/2^j\) to the reciprocal sum; count the pairs first, then sum the shells. For (ii): the all-primes ceiling is \(X/\log X\), not \(X/\log^2 X\) — compare shell sums \(\sum 1/j\) against \(\sum 1/j^2\), with P42.

Worked answer

(a) Shell \(j\) contributes at most \((C \cdot 2^j/j^2) \cdot (1/2^j) = C/j^2\), so the total is at most \(K \sum_{j\ge1} 1/j^2\) with \(K = 2C\) — each member of a pair contributes its own reciprocal. (b) \(\sum_{j \ge 1} 1/j^2 = \pi^2/6 \approx 1.645\). (i) The shell bound \(C/j^2\) decays fast enough that the infinite sum of shells converges — the \(2^j\) in the count and the \(1/2^j\) in the reciprocals cancel exactly, leaving a summable \(1/j^2\); the decomposition converts one enormous sum into a ledger of shrinking ones. (ii) For all primes the same cancellation leaves \(\sim 1/j\) per shell — the harmonic series — and that diverges: the sieve's doubly-logarithmic twin ceiling is precisely what makes the twins' ledger converge where the primes' does not.

Exercise 6 ◐ — Calibrate a budget under fire. You must certify a ceiling for survivors on the interval \([10^6, 2 \cdot 10^6]\), and your error control guarantees each divisor pair contributes at most \(1\) to the remainder — so the remainder's total is at most \(R^2\) for budget \(R\). (a) With the main term's capacity about \(\log R\) deep in budget, write the ceiling's two terms and find, to the nearest power of ten, the largest \(R\) keeping the remainder below one percent of the interval. (b) Evaluate the ceiling's order at that \(R\), and compare with the interval's honest prime-scale \(Y/\log Y\); state which is larger and by what factor. (c) Say in one sentence what doubling \(R\) would do to each term, and why that is the wrong move here.

Hint

Interval \(Y = 10^6\); one percent is \(10^4\), so \(R^2 \le 10^4\). For (b), the capacity is §3's sum \(\sum_{m \le R} \mu(m)^2/\varphi(m)\) over squarefree \(m\) — evaluate it exactly at \(R = 100\), and expect it to sit above the natural logarithm: §3's growth carries a constant too. \(\log 10^6 = 13.8\) to one decimal.

Worked answer

(a) Ceiling: \(Y/V(R) + R^2\). One percent of \(Y = 10^6\) is \(10^4\), so \(R \le 10^2\): budget a hundred, to the nearest power of ten. (b) The capacity at that budget is \(\sum_{m \le 100} \mu(m)^2/\varphi(m) \approx 5.9\) — §3's logarithm plus the constant it already carries at this scale — so the ceiling is about \(10^6/5.9 + 10^4 \approx 1.8 \times 10^5\), against the honest prime-scale \(Y/\log Y = 10^6/13.8 \approx 7.2 \times 10^4\): the certified ceiling is about two-and-a-half times larger — the factor-of-two parity slack of Exhibit A plus a rough budget's tax — exactly the shape Exhibit B predicted. (c) Doubling \(R\) deepens \(V\) only additively (about \(+\log 2 \approx 0.7\), a twelve-percent gain on a capacity of \(5.9\)) while quadrupling the remainder past the one-percent line — quadrupling is the killer: the remainder's \(R^2\) is a budget, and budgets square; the correct move is a bigger interval or better error control, not a deeper budget on the same one.
