The Patterns Atlas · P09 · the ninth move of the spine

Find the invariant

When every attempt fails, stop asking "what am I missing?" and start asking "what can nothing change?" — one quantity no move touches turns a pile of failed tries into a proof that nobody, ever, will succeed.

New word: state. A state is one complete snapshot of the situation: every number on the board, every colour count in the meadow, every coin face on the table. The moves are the rules that turn one state into the next, and the whole process is a walk from state to state. New word: invariant. An invariant is a quantity computed from the state that every allowed move leaves exactly unchanged — not roughly, not usually: exactly, always. New word: conserved. The physicists' word for the same coat: a quantity is conserved by a process when no step of the process can spend it. Same move, two coats; this page wears both.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, and P08 divided by the twins. P08's closing pointer promised exactly this page: a symmetry is a move that preserves something; the something it preserves is the twin concept. P08 hunted the moves and divided by them. This page hunts the something — the quantity sitting dead still while everything visible churns.

Here is the tension this page resolves. P02 and P03 trained one instinct: try small cases, stress the claim, trust the wreckage. But some problems hand you a target that no amount of trying reaches — and the small cases do not "fail," they fail every single time. Sooner or later you owe yourself a question: is this hard, or is it impossible? From the inside, the two feel identical. The good news: one well-chosen conserved quantity separates them forever. If the start and the target disagree on something no move can change, then no sequence of moves — however long, however clever — connects them. That is the strongest sentence in mathematics: not "I could not do it," but "nobody will ever do it." The bad news, stated honestly up front: the invariant does not announce itself. No recipe takes a problem and prints its invariant; finding it is the skill, and a quantity that merely looks conserved is this move's most seductive wrong answer (block 5 collects the wreckage).

2 · Meet the problem

A chameleon convention in a meadow: thirteen red, fifteen green, seventeen blue — forty-five chameleons in all. Whenever two chameleons of different colours meet, both leave the meeting wearing the third colour: red meets green, both walk away blue; green meets blue, both walk away red; blue meets red, both walk away green. Meetings of two same-coloured chameleons change nothing. The question: can the meadow ever become single-colour — all forty-five chameleons wearing one colour?

Try it small first (P02's instinct, and here it genuinely works). One red, one green, one blue: a red meets a green, both turn blue, and the meadow is three blues — done in one meeting. Two red, two green, one blue: red meets green, twice, and the meadow is five blues — done again. The small cases melt; the pattern looks friendly. Now the real meadow: thirteen, fifteen, seventeen. Play some meetings and watch the counts:

\[13, 15, 17 \;\to\; 12, 14, 19 \;\to\; 11, 13, 21 \;\to\; 10, 12, 23 \;\to\; \cdots\]

The counts slide around, the meadow churns — and the single colour never arrives. Different orders of meetings, different choices: same churn, same wall. After a while the honest suspicion dawns: maybe no route exists at all. But "I tried and failed" is not a sentence about the meadow; it is a sentence about you.

Here is the pivot — P08's pointer honoured. Stop watching the chameleons one at a time and watch pairs of colours. Take the red count minus the green count, \(R - G\), and ask what each meeting does to it:

  • red meets green: both counts drop by one — \(R - G\) does not move;
  • red meets blue: red drops one, green gains two — \(R - G\) changes by \(-3\);
  • green meets blue: green drops one, red gains two — \(R - G\) changes by \(+3\).

Every meeting changes \(R - G\) by \(0\) or by \(\pm 3\): always a multiple of \(3\). So the leftover of \(R - G\) on division by \(3\) — in B02's language, the class of \(R - G \pmod{3}\) — never changes, not in any meeting, not ever. The start has \(R - G = 13 - 15 = -2\), which is \(1\) more than a multiple of \(3\). Every single-colour target has leftover \(0\): all blue is \(0 - 0\), all red is \(45 - 0\), all green is \(0 - 45\). The start wears class \(1\); every target wears class \(0\); no meeting ever changes the class. The target is unreachable. Not "we did not find the meetings" — there is no sequence of meetings at all: any sequence preserves the class, and the classes disagree.

And the small cases? The same one quantity explains them. One of each colour: \(R - G = 0\), the target's class — and indeed it succeeds. Two, two, one: again class \(0\), and it succeeds. The friendly small cases were friendly because their class matched; the real meadow was never hard, it was sealed. One quantity, computed once, retired every failed attempt we could ever make.

3 · Name the move

What the meadow taught, stated once and for all.

Pattern P09 — Find the invariant. When a problem hands you a process — a set of allowed moves carrying states to states — and asks whether some target can be reached, do not attack the target. Hunt the quantity the process cannot change: (1) define a quantity on the states; (2) prove that every allowed move preserves it exactly; (3) compute it at the start and at the target. If it disagrees, the target is unreachable — by any sequence of moves, of any length, ever. If it agrees, nothing follows: the invariant is a necessary condition, never a sufficient one. The same move, pointed the other way, pins values down: when the process must end in a single state and the invariant fixes a value there, that value is the answer, on every route at once.

Or, in words: before asking what a process can do, find what it cannot undo. A quantity that survives every move untouched is a wall: states on opposite sides of the wall never meet, and no amount of cleverness tunnels through arithmetic. But the wall only separates — landing on the same side as your target guarantees nothing, and the move is honest about that asymmetry: one disagreement closes the case forever; any number of agreements prove nothing at all.

Box P09.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The quantity must be defined on states, and computable at both ends. "The class of \(R - G \pmod{3}\)" is computed from any meadow snapshot in one subtraction, and every single-colour target carries one too. What breaks: the vague quantity — a feeling that "the colours stay balanced" is not a quantity; if you cannot compute it on a napkin for any state you might reach, it cannot separate anything.
  2. Preservation must hold for every move — proved, not sampled. Three meeting types, three checks of \(R - G\): changes of \(0\), \(-3\), \(+3\). All three, or nothing. A quantity preserved by most moves is preserved by nothing, because the walk is free to use the move you did not check. What breaks: the phantom invariant — block 5's first exhibit survives one of the three meetings and dies on the other two.
  3. The quantity must separate start from target. The meadow's total \(45\) is perfectly conserved — and perfectly useless, because every target also totals \(45\). An invariant earns its keep only by disagreeing. What breaks: the true-but-useless invariant, block 5's second exhibit.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: an impossibility ("can never"), a forced value ("must always"). Problem shape: a process — meetings, replacements, transformations — rather than a one-shot computation; the target sits at the end of a long walk whose individual steps are easy. Tools at hand: the move's arithmetic is visible enough to test candidate quantities against, a few at a time. If the claim is "compute this number" with no process in sight, this is not your page; if the claim is "no walk reaches there," it is almost nobody's page but this one.

4 · The move at work

Three worked examples, three domains, two poses of the move: the wall that blocks, and the value that pins.

Example 1 (algebra) — the board that cannot surprise you

The board holds \(2\), \(3\), \(5\). The move: erase any two entries \(a\) and \(b\), write \(a + b + ab\). Two merges later one entry remains. What is it? Route one: merge \(2, 3\) first — \(2 + 3 + 6 = 11\) — then \(11 + 5 + 55 = 71\). Route two: merge \(3, 5\) first — \(3 + 5 + 15 = 23\) — then \(23 + 2 + 46 = 71\). The third route gives \(71\) as well. Coincidence, or forced?

Add one. \(c + 1 = a + b + ab + 1 = (a + 1)(b + 1)\): on the shifted numbers, the merge move is plain multiplication. So the product of (entry \(+\,1\)) over the whole board is conserved:

\[(2+1)(3+1)(5+1) = 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 = 72 \quad \text{at every moment, on every route}.\]

When a single entry \(N\) remains, its shifted value is the product: \(N + 1 = 72\), so \(N = 71\) — not on the three routes we tried, on every route, past and future. That is the move's second pose: not a wall that blocks a target, but a fixed value the process must land on. The shift itself is an old friend — adding \(1\) to every entry is P05's canonical dressing, the uniform in which the move's true face (multiplication) shows. And invariants are loyal to their move and nothing else: change the rule to \(a + b - ab\), and \(\prod (x+1)\) dies at once — \(2, 3 \to 2 + 3 - 6 = -1\), and the shifted product falls from \(12\) to \(0\). No borrowing between moves.

Moves used: P09 (the conserved product \(\prod (x+1)\), in its value-pinning pose), P05 (the \(+1\) shift that turns the merge into multiplication).

Example 2 (geometry) — the board that cannot be tiled

Remove two opposite corners from a chessboard — \(a1\) and \(h8\) — leaving \(62\) squares. A domino covers exactly two edge-adjacent squares. Can \(31\) dominoes tile the mutilated board?

Try it small (P02): a \(4 \times 4\) board loses its opposite corners \(a1\) and \(d4\), leaving \(14\) squares and \(7\) dominoes. The attempts jam — every one dies the same death — and the invariant below will explain why, at both sizes at once.

Colour the board as chess demands. A domino, covering two edge-adjacent squares, always covers one square of each colour: moving one square changes the row or the column by one, flipping the square's colour. So a tiled board carries equal colour counts — that is the invariant, and the \(4 \times 4\) attempts were dying on it before we looked. Now the census. The full board holds \(32\) and \(32\). The two removed corners, being opposite, are the same colour — \(a1\) sits at row \(1\), column \(1\) and \(h8\) at row \(8\), column \(8\), and both pair an odd with an odd or an even with an even, so both squares share one colour. The mutilated board holds \(30\) of that colour and \(32\) of the other. Not equal — so no tiling exists: \(31\) dominoes would cover \(31\) of each, and only \(30\) of the one exist.

Now check the check (P03's discipline): remove one square of each colour instead — say \(a1\) and \(h1\). The census reads \(31\) and \(31\); the objection vanishes; and a tiling genuinely exists: rows two through eight tile horizontally, four dominoes each, and row one's six survivors \(b1\) through \(g1\) tile horizontally in three pieces. When this invariant objects, nothing exists; when it stops objecting, this door at least is open. The art of choosing a colouring — there are many, and some see what others cannot — is a move of its own further up the ladder: P75.

Moves used: P09 (the colour census), P02 (the small board that showed the failure's shape), P03 (the control case that checks the check).

Example 3 (numbers) — the nine-point filter

Both B01 and B02 promised this page a piece of currency, so let us count it out: a number and its digit sum are congruent mod \(9\) — \(n \equiv\) the sum of its digits \(\pmod{9}\).

Why, in one line of B02: \(10 \equiv 1 \pmod{9}\), so every power \(10^k \equiv 1\), so \(n = d_k 10^k + \cdots + d_1 10 + d_0 \equiv d_k + \cdots + d_1 + d_0\) — the reduce-first rule applied to the powers. Check it on B02's own teaser numbers: \(47 \equiv 4 + 7 = 11 \equiv 2\), and \(2026 \equiv 2 + 0 + 2 + 6 = 10 \equiv 1 \pmod 9\).

As an invariant it catches wrong arithmetic. Multiply \(1234 \times 5678\). Digit sums: \(1234 \to 1+2+3+4 = 10 \to 1\), and \(5678 \to 5+6+7+8 = 26 \to 8\); so by B02's product rule the true product must sit in class \(1 \cdot 8 = 8 \pmod 9\). A candidate answer \(7{,}006{,}650\) has digit sum \(24 \to 6\): \(6 \neq 8\), so it is certainly wrong, whatever the multiplication looked like. The true product \(7{,}006{,}652\) has digit sum \(26 \to 8\) and passes.

The honest price, paid up front: the filter is a detector, not a proof. \(7{,}006{,}661\) is wrong too — it is the truth plus nine — and its digit sum is also \(26 \to 8\): it sails through. Failing the nines test convicts; passing it proves nothing, because adding \(9\) is invisible to the class. P03 said it first: a check that survives a wrong answer is a detector, and detectors are for catching, not certifying.

And the twin tie, which is why this page and P08 share the example: shuffle a number's digits — any shuffle, any order — and the digit sum does not move, because addition does not care about order. The shuffle is P08's symmetry move acting on the digits; the digit sum is exactly the thing it preserves; read mod \(9\), it is this page's invariant. B01's promise — a digit-sum rule that stays put mod \(9\) no matter how the digits are shuffled — paid in full: one quantity, two coats, two pages.

Moves used: P09 (the mod-\(9\) digit-sum invariant, in its error-catching pose), P05 (reduce each factor to its residue — the canonical representative), P08 (the digit shuffle, whose preserved quantity this is).

5 · When the move fails

P03's discipline applies to the move itself: it fails, in five named shapes, and naming the shape is the diagnosis. The first three fool you on the proof side — the quantity is not what you think. The last two fool you on the search side — the quantity is not the lever you need. Every exhibit below is checked, not asserted: each one was run.

The phantom. The claim: "the parity of the red count never changes." Check it against all three meetings. Green meets blue: both walk away red, the red count gains two, parity survives. But red meets green — both walk away blue — sends the red count from \(13\) to \(12\): parity flips. So does red meeting blue. A quantity preserved by some moves is preserved by nothing, because the walk is free to use the move you did not check. Mitigation: the transition audit — every allowed move, one line each, no exceptions; Box P09.A's second demand. Price: a conservation verified on the meetings you happened to think of is a coincidence wearing a proof's clothes. Default: all moves checked, or it is not an invariant.

The useless one. The head count \(45\) is conserved — genuinely, on every meeting, audit three for three. It is also worthless here: the start totals \(45\), and so does every single-colour target, so it agrees with everything and separates nothing. Truth and usefulness are two different audits, and this exhibit passes only the first. Mitigation: before trusting an invariant, compute it on both endpoints; an agreement means it decides nothing. Price: a true-but-useless invariant comforts you while you are still lost. Default: an invariant earns its keep only by disagreeing.

The necessary-but-not-sufficient. A small island starts with three red chameleons and nothing else: state \((3, 0, 0)\). Ask it to reach one of each colour: \((1, 1, 1)\). The class of \(R - G\) is \(3 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) at the start and \(0 \equiv 0 \pmod 3\) at the target: the invariant agrees. And the island is frozen — one colour present, so no meeting of two different colours can ever occur, so nothing ever changes. The invariant is a necessary condition — every reachable state must agree — and never a sufficient one: agreement is not a route. Mitigation: when the invariant agrees, the case is still open — hunt an explicit construction (P03's other half) or a finer invariant that does disagree. Price: agreement tempts you to declare possibility without ever building it. Default: a disagreement closes the case forever; an agreement closes nothing.

The monovariant mistake. Sometimes the process conserves nothing at all — and you conclude the method has nothing to say. But some processes are governed by a quantity that is not fixed yet only ever moves one way: a rank that only rises, an energy that only drains, a board total that only shrinks. A quantity that never turns back is a monovariant — a different lever with a different power: it does not separate states, it terminates processes. It has its own page: P72. Mitigation: audit for conservation first; if every candidate dies, audit for one-way movement before quitting. Price: confusing the two levers makes you discard the move at exactly the problems where its cousin succeeds. Default: fixed quantity first, one-way quantity second, hopeless only after both.

The wrong quantity. The first candidate on the real meadow might be "the red count mod \(3\)." The first red-green meeting kills it — \(13 \to 12\), class \(1 \to 0\) — and the hasty conclusion "there is no invariant here" forfeits the method on a single corpse. The conserved quantity was never a coordinate alone; it was a difference of coordinates, \(R - G \pmod 3\). Most first candidates die; the method survives their deaths. Mitigation: when a candidate dies, combine before quitting — differences, sums, products of shifted coordinates (Example 1's \(\prod (x + 1)\) was exactly a shifted product), then the catalogue in Box P09.B. Price: the death of one quantity is not the death of the idea, but quitting there makes it one. Default: kill candidates quickly, cheaply, and in batches — and never mistake the autopsy of one for the search's end.

Scope, honestly. This move proves impossibilities and pins forced values. It does not construct routes, certify reachability, rank the reachable states, or find the shortest path — agreement is silence, not a yes. And it is only as good as its audit: one unchecked transition (the phantom), one unevaluated endpoint (the useless one), one unbuilt construction (the frozen island), one untried direction (the monovariant), one uncombined quantity (the wrong one), and the move fails without telling you. The audit list in Box P09.A is short for a reason: each line is one of these deaths.

6 · What to try next

The verdict came back; now route it. Three endings, three doors. Disagreement — the invariant blocked the target: write the impossibility down; the case is closed, and closed forever is the whole point of this move. Agreement — the invariant said nothing: the door may or may not be open, and only two things move the question — an explicit construction (P03's other half: build the route, or watch it fail and learn why) or a finer invariant that separates what this one could not. Nothing conserved — every candidate died the audit: before quitting, audit for one-way movement instead — block 5's fourth exhibit — because a quantity that never turns back governs the process even while it changes.

The routes out of this page, each with its own door:

P10 — force a collision. The next page of the spine, and the mirror pose of this one: where the invariant proves that nobody can reach a target, the pigeonhole proves that somebody must — more objects than boxes forces two into one box, and the art is arranging the boxes. Impossibility here; existence there; the spine alternates them deliberately.

P11 — prove by contradiction. Look back at what this page actually argued: assume a route exists; then every step preserves the quantity, so start and target must agree; they do not; contradiction. The invariant argument is a proof by contradiction in miniature — P11 names the general shape and trains it on purpose.

P12 — name the obstruction. The direct heir. When an invariant blocks a target, mathematics does not stop at "no": it promotes the number that refuses to change into a named structure — an obstruction — and classifies everything that number blocks. Far up the ladder, this promotion grows into the machinery behind cohomology and impossibility theorems of every kind. P12 takes the first step of that promotion.

P72 — find the monovariant. The cousin from block 5: the quantity that is never conserved but never turns back. When this move has nothing to say, that one often ends the argument instead.

P75 — coloring arguments. The mutilated chessboard was this move plus one chosen colouring; the choosing is an art of its own, with its own catalogue of paints. When a grid or board resists, P75 is where the palette lives.

M03 — Pick's theorem. The multi-pattern showcase that runs this move end to end: experiment with lattice polygons (P02) until the invariant surfaces — the area minus the interior count minus half the boundary count refuses to move — and the theorem falls out of what refuses to move.

Deferred honestly, with reasons. Two deep relatives of this move wait up the ladder, and both need pages you have not climbed. Energy methods (P29) is the invariant idea at continuous scale: a quantity that only decreases forces a differential equation to settle, the way a conserved quantity forces a walk to stay in its class — it needs the equation-modelling move (P26) first. Localize at a prime (P30) is the digit-sum trick grown up: work one prime at a time, where the invariant is "divisibility by \(p\)," and reassemble — it needs the descent move (P13) first. Both are this page's descendants; neither is reachable yet, and pretending otherwise would violate the ladder's one law.

7 · Lineage

Invariants are old currency — casting out nines was a merchant's check centuries before congruence classes had a name (B02 only dressed it in 19th-century clothes; Gauss, 1801). The mutilated chessboard was posed by the philosopher Max Black in 1946, in Critical Thinking, as a parable about how evidence works: no pile of failed attempts adds up to a proof — one colour census does. Martin Gardner made it famous in his Scientific American columns. And at the research frontier the idea has a theorem with a name attached: Emmy Noether, in her 1918 paper Invariante Variationsprobleme, proved that every symmetry of a physical law forces a conserved quantity — time-translation symmetry is energy conservation; space-translation symmetry is momentum conservation. Noether's theorem is this page and P08 shaking hands: the move that preserves (symmetry) and the quantity preserved (invariant) are two views of one fact. None of this history is load-bearing here — but when a physicist says "energy is conserved," they are running the ninth move of the spine.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the party that cannot happen

At a party with \(25\) people, every person shakes hands with exactly three others. Prove that this party cannot happen — not "is unlikely": cannot.

Hint

Count the handshakes from the people's side. Each handshake gets counted how many times? What does that force the people's-side total to be?

Worked answer

Let \(H\) be the number of handshakes. Counting hand-in-hand pairs from each person's side adds up \(25 \times 3 = 75\); counting from the handshakes' side, each handshake has exactly two participants, so the same total is \(2H\). One total, two descriptions: \(2H = 75\). But \(2H\) is even and \(75\) is odd — the parity of the double-counted total is an invariant of every handshake configuration that can exist, and this demand disagrees with it. No arrangement of handshakes, however creative, satisfies it: \(H = 37.5\) is not a number of handshakes. The invariant pose of this page, on the smallest scale: the evenness of \(2H\) is the quantity no handshake can change, and the target configuration falls on the wrong side of it.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the bigger blackboard

The board of Example 1, scaled up: it holds \(1, 2, 3, \ldots, 10\). Same move: erase any two entries \(a\) and \(b\), write \(a + b + ab\), until one entry remains. What is the final entry? — and state, precisely, who forced it.

Hint

Example 1's shift, verbatim: the move becomes multiplication the moment you add one to everything. What is conserved, and what is its value this time?

Worked answer

The conserved product is \(\prod (x + 1)\), taken over the board: \(2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdots 11 = 11! = 39{,}916{,}800\), at every moment, on every route. When one entry \(N\) remains, \(N + 1\) carries the whole product: \(N = 11! - 1 = 39{,}916{,}799\). Who forced it: not the route, not the choices — the invariant, in its value-pinning pose. Every merge order, past and future, lands on the same number, because every route is trapped on the level set of \(\prod (x + 1) = 11!\), and that level set ends at exactly one single-entry board.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the jug you cannot fill

You hold an empty \(6\)-litre jug and an empty \(9\)-litre jug, a tap, and a drain. Allowed moves: fill either jug from the tap, empty either jug down the drain, pour one jug into the other until the receiving jug is full or the pouring jug is empty. (a) Prove that measuring exactly \(4\) litres into a jug is impossible. (b) Swap the \(6\)-litre jug for a \(5\)-litre one and the impossibility vanishes: produce exactly \(4\) litres.

Hint

For (a): every jug's content, at every moment, is a multiple of one particular number — find it, then audit each move type. For (b): the census stopped objecting; now you must build the route.

Worked answer

(a) Both capacities are multiples of \(3\), and \(\gcd(6, 9) = 3\). Claim: every jug's content is a multiple of \(3\), at every moment. Audit the three move types — fill: content becomes \(6\) or \(9\), a multiple of \(3\); empty: content becomes \(0\); pour from jug \(X\) to jug \(Y\): the transferred amount is the smaller of "what \(X\) holds" and "what \(Y\) lacks", and both quantities are multiples of \(3\) (the capacities are, the contents are, so the difference \(9 - b\) is), so the smaller of two multiples of \(3\) is a multiple of \(3\), and both jugs' new contents stay multiples of \(3\). The invariant holds on every move — so \(4\), which is not a multiple of \(3\), can never appear in either jug. Impossible, forever, on the strength of one audit. (b) With jugs of \(5\) and \(3\), the gcd is \(1\), and the census has nothing to say. The construction: \((0,0) \to (5,0) \to (2,3) \to (2,0) \to (0,2) \to (5,2) \to (4,3)\) — fill the \(5\), pour into the \(3\) leaving \(2\), empty the \(3\), pour the \(2\) across, fill the \(5\) again, and top up the \(3\) from it: the \(5\)-jug now holds exactly \(4\). Part (a) blocked with the invariant; part (b) agreed with it and built the route — the two halves of this move's discipline, side by side.

Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

Back on the meadow of §2: thirteen red, fifteen green, seventeen blue, meetings recolour pairs as before. Three students each hand in an argument that the meadow can never become single-colour. Student A: "The total head count is conserved — every meeting turns two chameleons into two chameleons, so the count stays \(45\) forever. Therefore no single colour can take over." Student B: "The parity of the red count never changes: the meetings move chameleons around, but reds keep their parity. The single-colour targets have the wrong parity, so they cannot be reached." Student C: "The class of \(R - G \pmod 3\) is conserved — every meeting moves it by \(0\) or \(\pm 3\). The start wears class \(1\) and every single-colour target wears class \(0\), so no target is reachable." Referee all three: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

Two different audits exist: is the claimed quantity really conserved (check every meeting type), and if so, does it actually disagree with the target? Each student fails at most one audit — but not the same one.

Worked answer

Student A: true but useless. The conservation is genuine — all three meeting types preserve the head count, audit three for three. But every single-colour target also totals \(45\): the invariant agrees with the targets it is asked to block, and an agreement decides nothing. The word "therefore" in A's argument is carrying a conclusion the quantity cannot support. Verdict: rejected — the fact is right, the argument is empty. Student B: phantom. The conservation is false. Check the three meetings against the red count's parity: green-meets-blue adds two reds (parity survives); but red-meets-green sends \(13 \to 12\) — parity flips — and so does red-meets-blue. One unchecked meeting kills the claim; a quantity preserved by some moves is preserved by nothing. Verdict: rejected — the argument's foundation is not conserved at all. Student C: decisive. The audit passes on all three meetings (\(R - G\) moves by \(0\), \(+3\), \(-3\)), and the quantity separates: start class \(1\), target class \(0\), forever. Verdict: upheld — this is the argument. The referee move itself is P03's (stress-test the claim before trusting it) aimed at invariant claims; the patterns underneath are P09 for the conserved quantity and P03 for the referee's discipline.

Exercise 5 ◐ — the chameleon census

The meadow of §2 was sealed, and the seal read "class of \(R - G\) mod \(3\)". But small meadows sometimes can go single-colour — §2's own \(1,1,1\) and \(2,2,1\) both did. Explore: enumerate small meadows (say, up to six chameleons in all), determine by honest play which single colours each can reach, and conjecture the exact criterion: precisely which meadows can reach which single colours. Then test your conjecture against the frozen island \((3, 0, 0)\) and against §2's meadow.

Hint

For each target colour, one pairwise difference must end at \(0\), and differences only ever move by multiples of \(3\). So which pair of counts would you compare for each target? What does the census say a meadow needs, for each colour, before that target is even possible?

Worked answer

The census (every meadow with up to six chameleons, played out) shows the pattern: for a meadow with at least two colours present, all-blue is reachable exactly when \(R \equiv G \pmod 3\), and cyclically — all-red when \(G \equiv B\), all-green when \(B \equiv R\). Necessity is §2's argument itself: all-blue ends with \(R - G = 0\), and \(R - G \pmod 3\) never moves; cyclically for the other targets. Sufficiency is a construction: to reach all-blue when \(R \equiv G \pmod 3\), first equalize the pair — each meeting with a blue shifts \(R - G\) by \(\pm 3\) (and if no blue exists, one red-green meeting mints two), so walk \(R - G\) to \(0\) — then run red-green meetings until both piles are empty and the meadow is entirely blue. One honest edge, which the census forces into the open: a one-colour meadow is frozen — it has already arrived, and no criterion applies to it; §5's dead island \((3, 0, 0)\) is that edge in person, and it is why the criterion carries the two-colours qualifier instead of smoothing the edge away. §2's \(13, 15, 17\) has all three classes distinct mod \(3\) — no congruent pair, no door, which is the seal of §2 read in full. The exploration's honest ledger: the census is a detector over small cases, and the conjecture it suggests; the necessity is proved by the invariant, the sufficiency by the construction above — three legs, and only together do they carry "exact".
