The Patterns Atlas · P47 · the second move of tier 5

The diagram chase

Some proofs never build anything: send one witness through a diagram of exact rows and commuting squares, and the bookkeeping itself forces maps and conclusions nobody ever constructed.

New word: commutative diagram. A diagram is boxes and arrows: the boxes hold vector spaces (or groups), and each arrow is a map that goes from one box to another. A diagram commutes when every route from one box to another gives the same result — going right then down agrees with going down then right. A row of arrows is exact at a box when what arrives there is exactly what gets killed there: the image of the incoming arrow equals the kernel of the outgoing one. A short exact sequence \(0 \to A \xrightarrow{i} B \xrightarrow{p} C \to 0\) is an exact row read as a sentence: \(A\) sits inside \(B\) faithfully (\(i\) injective), everything of \(B\) is either from \(A\) or killed on the way to \(C\) (exact at \(B\)), and nothing of \(C\) is missed (\(p\) surjective) — in words, \(B\) is \(A\) plus \(C\), recorded honestly. Exactness is bookkeeping made exact: at every station, arrivals and deaths are accounted for to the last vector.

1 · Before we begin

P33 built the three-step machine — cochains, the difference operator \(\delta\), the always-true law \(\delta \circ \delta = 0\), the quotient \(H = Z/B\) — and used it to measure one thing: local answers that fail to glue, tallied as an obstruction. Its section of doors named the bookkeeping engine that powers the whole subject; this page opens the engine. And P46 ended its first encounter with a zig-zag — push across, lift up, push across — that conjured a differential no layer ever contained, and then spent a whole exercise proving the conjuring was honest. That zig-zag had a name all along, and so did the honesty proof: both were a chase, and this page teaches the chasing. The ladder is short because the climbing is done: exactness is P33's arrivals-equal-deaths bookkeeping, the quotient is P33's cycles-mod-boundaries, and the witness being chased is P12's named refusal, sent on a journey.

One older habit is the safety net. P25 taught the census discipline — when a claim says "always", build every small case and count — and this page leans on it twice: once to watch a forced map be well-defined in every configuration a small world can offer, and once to watch what breaks, in exactly which way, when a hypothesis is quietly removed.

The tension is about what a proof is allowed to do. Most proofs construct: build the object, check the laws, conclude. The deepest bookkeeping results do something stranger — they never construct anything. They set one element moving through a diagram, use exactness to move it station by station, use commutativity to make the route not matter, and read a conclusion off the far end: a map exists, a kernel equals an image, a sequence is exact. The question this page resolves: can pure bookkeeping force mathematics into existence, and if so, what exactly is each hypothesis paying for? The answer runs on one page of ledgers — two rows, three columns, everything computable by hand — and the receipts generalize: the same chase, run at research scale, is the connecting map behind every long exact sequence, including the one P33 promised at its far door.

2 · Meet the problem

Two ledgers, one above the other, in a forensic accountant's office. Each ledger has three columns: intake (all discrepancies that enter the books), processing (the working column), and dispatch (what the office certifies and sends out). The office's two rules are exactness, read as bookkeeping:

  • Rule 1 (the ledgers are honest): a processing entry gets killed at dispatch exactly when it was explained by intake. Arrivals equal deaths, station by station — the image of each arrow is the kernel of the next. In symbols the top ledger is a short exact sequence \(0 \to A \xrightarrow{i} B \xrightarrow{p} C \to 0\), and the bottom ledger likewise \(0 \to A' \xrightarrow{i'} B' \xrightarrow{p'} C' \to 0\). All spaces here are over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), the two-element arithmetic of B02 — a discrepancy is either present or absent, and adding two entries toggles both.
  • Rule 2 (the audits agree): three auditors compare the ledgers column by column — \(f\) from intake to intake, \(g\) from processing to processing, \(h\) from dispatch to dispatch — and the two ways of auditing the middle agree: audit-then-process equals process-then-audit, \(g \circ i = i' \circ f\) and \(p' \circ g = h \circ p\). Both squares commute.

Here is the office, wires and all. Six rooms, three auditors, two agreements:

Now the anomaly. A dispatch entry \(c\) — an official certification — has passed the bottom-auditor's test: \(h(c) = 0\), the \(h\)-audit sees nothing wrong. But the middle auditor's paperwork keeps tripping: entries of the processing column that map to \(c\) never audit clean. Nobody in the office has defined a map from the top dispatch column to anywhere in the bottom ledger. And yet the two rules, plus the anomaly, are all the ingredients of a forced conclusion. Three questions:

Q1. Is there a place in the bottom ledger where \(c\)'s residue must be recorded, if the two rules hold? Q2. If the residue can be traced by more than one route, do the routes agree — or does the answer depend on the tracer? Q3. What is each of the two rules actually paying for? Break exactness, or break commutativity, and watch — precisely — what dies.

The move that answers all three never constructs anything. It sends \(c\) on a journey through the diagram and reads the far end. The journey is four stations long, and this page's job is to make you fluent in it — because at research scale, the same journey is called the connecting homomorphism, and every long exact sequence in mathematics is a stack of such journeys.

3 · The pattern, named

Here is the move, stated once as machinery. You hold two exact rows and three auditors making both squares commute — the office of §2. The snake is a four-station recipe that interrogates one anomaly at a time:

Pattern P47 — The diagram chase. When two exact rows are bridged by commuting squares, do not build a new machine to interrogate an anomaly. Hand the witness to the stations and let the bookkeeping move it: (1) Lift — the anomaly \(c\) lives in \(\ker h\); since \(p\) is onto, lift \(c\) to some \(b \in B\) with \(p(b) = c\) (E: a witness exists, possibly many). (2) Push — audit the lift: \(g(b)\); commutativity of the right square puts it in \(\ker p'\), the killed part of \(B'\). (3) Pull back — exactness of the bottom row says every killed entry was an arrival: \(g(b) = i'(y)\) for some \(y \in A'\); injectivity of \(i'\) pins \(y\) uniquely (U: at most one answer). (4) Quotient — record the class: \(\delta(c) = [y] \in A'/\operatorname{im} f\). The residue's home is not chosen; it is forced: \(\ker h\) maps to \(\operatorname{coker} f\), and nothing else in the bottom ledger can hold the answer. Run the four stations for every anomaly at once and the six stations line up — \(\ker f \to \ker g \to \ker h \xrightarrow{\delta} \operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} g \to \operatorname{coker} h\) — with image-equals-kernel at every interior station: the snake sequence, exact.

Or, in words: when the bookkeeping is exact and the audits agree, you do not need to build a new machine to interrogate an anomaly. Hand the witness to the first station and let exactness pass it along — each station does one thing, either there exists (a surjective arrow hands you a lift) or at most one (an injective arrow pins the answer) — and the journey's end is a map nobody defined, with the route taken erased by the final quotient. The move's one non-negotiable honesty clause: well-definedness is the theorem, not the paperwork. A chase that completes is worthless until you have shown the other routes — the other lifts at every existence station — complete the same way; the check is Exercise 3's heart, and the census of §4 runs it in every configuration a small world can offer.

The ledger device that keeps the honesty clause honest: mark every station of the chase with its letter, E for there exists or U for at most one, and refuse to read a conclusion until every E has been absorbed by a later U or a final quotient. Station 1 is an E — the lift is chosen, not given; station 3 is a U — the pulled-back witness is pinned; station 4 is the quotient that forgives the choice. A completed chase with an unabsorbed E is Exhibit A's route-dependence in waiting; a chase that treats a U as if it covered an upstream E is Exercise 4's mirage.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the rows must be exact. Exactness is what makes the stations functional: onto-ness powers the lift, arrivals-equal-deaths powers the pull-back, injectivity pins the witness. Jam-mode: a pull-back that reaches for an arrival and finds none — the chase stalls mid-station, with the corpse on the table and no home for the residue (Exhibit B).

Key 2 — the squares must commute. Commutativity is what lets the middle ledger hand its witness across: audit-then-process must equal process-then-audit, or the pushed entry never lands in the killed part. Jam-mode: a stall at the push, or worse — two routes that both complete and disagree, a map that produces clean-looking wrong answers (Exhibit A).

Key 3 — well-definedness must be earned. The existence stations carry choices, and the theorem — not the paperwork — is that the choices wash out: lifts differ by an arrival, audits agree by commutativity, pinned witnesses differ by \(\operatorname{im} f\). Jam-mode: a chase that never revisits its choices, whose conclusion quietly depends on the witness (Exercise 3, and the census's quiet majority are the cases where the theorem holds with nothing to say).

4 · Three encounters

The pattern needs meetings, or it stays a definition. Three are recorded here: the snake run by hand at a scale where every entry is visible, a census that counts every configuration a small world can hold, and a shadow that says the move was already running in this Atlas, unnamed, two chapters ago.

Encounter 1 · The snake, run by hand

Take §2's anomaly world and make it a ledger you can audit line by line:

Top ledger, mid column, dim = 2, spanned by \(b_1, b_2\): intake map \(i\) is \(a \mapsto b_1\), dispatch map \(p\) is \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto c\).
Top ledger, processing column, dim = 1: holds \(\langle a \rangle\) (intake) and \(\langle c \rangle\) (dispatch). Bottom ledger, mid column, dim = 2, spanned by \(u, v\): intake map \(i'\) is \(a' \mapsto u\), dispatch map \(p'\) is \(u \mapsto 0\), \(v \mapsto w\).
Bottom ledger, processing column, dim = 1: holds \(\langle a' \rangle\) (intake) and \(\langle w \rangle\) (dispatch). Auditors: \(f = 0\) (top intake to bottom intake), \(h = 0\) (top dispatch to bottom dispatch), \(g\) given by \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto u\).

The anomaly is \(c\), the dispatch auditor \(h\) is zero, so \(c \in \ker h\). Run the four stations:

Station 1 (lift, E): \(c\) was dispatched from \(B\); \(p\) is onto, so lift \(c\) to either \(b_2\) or \(b_1 + b_2\) — two witnesses, an honest E.

Station 2 (push): audit \(b_2 \mapsto u\); commutativity: \(p'(u) = 0 = h(c)\) — the entry dies at bottom dispatch. Audit \(b_1 + b_2 \mapsto u\): also dies.

Station 3 (pull back, U): \(u = i'(a')\), and \(i'\) injective, so \(y = a'\) — unique: an honest U.

Station 4 (quotient): \(\delta(c) = [a'] \in A'/\operatorname{im} f\). Since \(f = 0\), that quotient has exactly two classes, and \([a'] \neq [0]\). The witness routes \(b_2\) and \(b_1 + b_2\) both land on \([a']\) — the route check passes, and the residue is nonzero.

The full snake sequence is now pure bookkeeping:

Six stations, \(\ker f \to \ker g \to \ker h \xrightarrow{\delta} \operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} g \to \operatorname{coker} h\): dims 1, 1, 1, then 1, 1, 1. Arrows: \(\ker f \to \ker g\) sends \(a \mapsto b_1\); \(\ker g \to \ker h\) sends \(b_1 \mapsto 0\); \(\delta\) sends \(c \mapsto [a']\); \(\operatorname{coker} f \to \operatorname{coker} g\) sends \([a'] \mapsto [u] = [0]\); \(\operatorname{coker} g \to \operatorname{coker} h\) sends \([v] \mapsto [w]\).

Exactness reads off one relation at a time: image-equals-kernel holds at \(\ker g\) (both \(\langle b_1 \rangle\)), at \(\ker h\) (both \(\{0\}\) — \(\delta\) is injective here), at \(\operatorname{coker} f\) (both everything — \(\delta\) is surjective here, and the map out of \(\operatorname{coker} f\) is zero, since its image \([u]\) is already explained in \(B'\)), at \(\operatorname{coker} g\) (both zero: nothing arrives, and only the zero class dies), and at \(\operatorname{coker} h\) (the last arrow is onto: \([w]\) generates it). The middle stretch even makes \(\delta\) an isomorphism: the anomaly \(c\) and the unexplained intake class \([a']\) carry the same information, exchanged by a map neither ledger wrote down. That is the move's signature: exactness at the six stations turns one missing map into a theorem.

Encounter 2 · The census

One instance proves nothing about a move. But a small world can be counted completely: keep the two ledgers fixed (top mid column dim 2 with its exact pair, bottom mid dim 2 with its exact pair) and vary all three auditors. The intake auditor \(f\) has two honest options over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) — zero, or the identity; the dispatch auditor \(h\) likewise; and the processing auditor \(g\) is any of the \(2^4 = 16\) linear maps out of a 2-dimensional space. That is \(2 \times 2 \times 16 = 64\) configurations, each one auditable by hand:

Census of 64 two-ledger configurations (standard 1,2,1 ledgers over \(\mathbb{F}_2\); auditors \(f, h\) each zero or identity, \(g\) any of the 16 maps): 16 with the left square commuting, 16 with the right square commuting, 8 with both, 8 left-only, 8 right-only, 40 with neither. All 8 both-squares configurations give \(\delta\) well-defined on every anomaly; exactly 1 of the 8 has \(\delta \neq 0\) — the page's own instance \(f = 0\), \(h = 0\), \(g\) with \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto u\).

Three honest notes belong to the table. First: the two commutativity conditions are conditions on different entries of the auditor's matrix — write \(g(b_1) = \alpha u + \beta v\), \(g(b_2) = \gamma u + \delta v\): the left square pins exactly \(\alpha\)'s value (to \(f\)'s) and \(\beta\) to zero, the right square pins \(\delta\)'s value (to \(h\)'s) — and \(\beta\) too, which it shares with the left — while the fourth entry \(\gamma\) is free: so each \((f, h)\) pair leaves exactly 2 both-commuting auditors, 8 in all, not the 4 that independent conditions would predict. The squares are not independent: they share the middle ledger, and the right square's "pushed entries must die" reaches into the left square's column. Second: in 7 of the 8, \(\delta\) is well-defined and zero — the snake completes but carries no information, because \(g\) sends everything into the explained part of \(B'\); a well-defined witness that records nothing is the census's quiet majority. Third: a wider world does not dilute the move. Give the top mid column dim 4 — intake of dim 2 landing as \(\langle b_1, b_2 \rangle\), dispatch killing the arrivals and sending the other two basis vectors one-to-one onto a dispatch column of dim 2 — bottom mid dim 4 the same way, and vary \(g\) among the 16 maps that commute with both squares (each vanishes on the arrivals and lands in the killed part: a \(2 \times 2\) map after the two factorizations, 16 in all): every anomaly has 4 lifts, all well-defined, and \(\delta \neq 0\) on 15 of the 16 — \(\delta\) vanishes only for \(g = 0\). The bigger the world, the harder it is for the snake to carry nothing.

Encounter 3 · The shadow in the Atlas

The move has been in this book's basement since P33. The difference operator \(\delta\) of P33 — the operator that records a failure of exactness as a class — was built as: take a cycle, process it, kill it, ask what explained the corpse. The mountain sequence of P33: every other term vanished, the difference operator survived, and the far door stayed open because of that class. That is stations 2, 3, and 4 of the snake with the top ledger deleted; the full move adds the missing lift station, and the fact that it already worked with the ledger missing is why P33 could prove its theorems while remaining honest. P46's knight made the same move twice: its zig-zag was a chase through two commuting squares, and its well-definedness proof — the check that the two reading orders agree — was the route check, the E/U ledger closed. The lesson of the shadow: a pattern named late is still a pattern used early — the Atlas had been chasing for thirty-three pages, and the name arrives with the machinery that makes the next doors provable.

SKETCH — the third encounter opens onto a bigger claim than this page can prove. For any short exact sequence of coefficient systems \(0 \to F' \to F \to F'' \to 0\) on a shape, there is a long exact sequence of the bookkeeping engines: \(\cdots \to H^2(F') \xrightarrow{\delta} H^3(F'') \to H^3(F) \to \cdots\), whose connecting homomorphism is literally the snake's \(\delta\) with ledgers replaced by systems and the chase run station by station at the level of cocycles. Named gaps, stated plainly: (i) the ledger argument here is pointwise, but exactness of a sequence of systems is a stronger, diagram-wide statement — the two are not the same claim; (ii) the long sequence continues to the left, and its assembly for general shapes needs machinery (Čech-style, or sheaf-theoretic) that this page does not build; (iii) naturality — the claim that a map of short exact sequences induces a map of long sequences commuting with every \(\delta\) — is a theorem about squares of snakes and is not proved here. The claim is standard and load-bearing for the doors of §6; the proof is not on this page.

5 · The broken exhibits

The three keys of §3 each name a jam-mode, and a jam-mode is only honest once you have watched it jam. Three exhibits, staged on the §4 world so the receipts are checkable by hand.

Exhibit A · Broken commutativity, two modes

Keep both ledgers exact and break one square at a time. Mode 1 — break the right square: let the processing auditor \(g\) send \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto v\). The left square still commutes, but now \(p'(g(b_2)) = p'(v) = w \neq 0 = h(c)\): the audit of a lift lands outside the killed part of \(B'\). The chase stalls at station 3 — there is no \(y \in A'\) with \(i'(y) = v\), since every arrival of \(i'\) is explained — and the failure has a name: no annotation exists. Mode 2 — break the left square: let \(g\) send \(b_1 \mapsto u\), \(b_2 \mapsto 0\). The right square now commutes, and the chase runs: lift \(c\) via \(b_2\), audit to \(0\), pull back to \(y = 0\), class \([0]\). Lift \(c\) via \(b_1 + b_2\) instead: audit to \(u\), pull back to \(y = a'\), class \([a'] \neq [0]\). Both routes complete; the answers disagree; \(\delta\) is ill-defined — and because \(i'\) is injective in this exhibit, the ◆ exercise's "uniqueness" defense is exactly the wrong medicine. A stalled chase announces itself; a route-dependent one produces clean-looking wrong proofs. The second mode is the dangerous one.

Exhibit B · Broken exactness

Keep both squares commuting and break a row: let the bottom intake map \(i'\) be zero (send \(a' \mapsto 0\)), so the bottom row is no longer exact at \(B'\) — arrivals dim 0, but the killed part of \(p'\) is still \(\langle u \rangle\), dim 1. Nothing in the world of squares forbids this; the diagram is a perfectly good pair of commuting squares. Run the chase on \(c\): lift to \(b_2\), audit to \(u\), \(u\) is killed at bottom dispatch — and then station 3 reaches for exactness and finds nothing: no \(y\) explains \(u\), because intake explains nothing now. The chase stalls with the corpse on the table, and no choice of lift rescues it (each of the two lifts audits to \(u\)). The failure mode differs from Exhibit A's: the squares all commute, the stall is inside a station rather than between stations, and the anomaly's residue has nowhere to be recorded because the recording home — \(A'/\operatorname{im} f\) — is fed by an intake that no longer explains the killed entries. Exactness is not paperwork; it is what makes the stations functional.

Exhibit C · The bookkeeper's burden

The chase tempts in both directions: the beginner treats well-definedness as a formality, and the veteran treats every map with suspicion. Both temptations have a signature and a habit:

  • Failure signature: a completed chase whose conclusion quietly depends on the witness — used when a proof says "choose a lift" and never revisits the choice. Mitigation: the E/U ledger written during the chase, one station at a time, so an unabsorbed E is visible before any conclusion is drawn. Default habit: mark every existence station with E and every uniqueness station with U, and refuse to read a conclusion until each E has been absorbed by a U or a quotient.
  • Failure signature: squares verified on a few elements and assumed for all — used when commutativity is checked on generators of the wrong ledger or on a spanning set with relations ignored. Mitigation: verify commutativity on a basis, both squares, before the first lift; over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) this is a finite check the census can even automate. Default habit: two-route checks on bases, not on samples; a square commutes on all elements or it does not commute.
  • Failure signature: sign chases at scale — used when the move runs over \(\mathbb{Z}\) or a general ring, where lifts differ by more than "plus an intake element" and the route check must track signs. Mitigation: run the census on the smallest world that still exhibits the failure; keep the world of the page over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) where sign is vacuous, and read the general statement as the same ledger with a sign column added. Default habit: before trusting a sign-laden chase, truncate the world until the sign failure is visible in a table.

Scope, honestly. This page chases over vector spaces over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), finite worlds where a census can be complete and every witness can be listed. The move is true in vastly larger worlds — modules over any ring, and every abelian category, where "element" is replaced by generalized element arguments and the E/U ledger by categorical language. Two honest prices: in general categories there is no census (completeness was a property of the small world, not of the move), and the route check's finite verification becomes a genuine theorem (the five lemma and its relatives exist because well-definedness at scale is work, not bookkeeping). The doors of §6 lead there; this page's warrants stop at the finite world's edge.

6 · Where the door leads

The chase is a tool, not a destination. Four doors out of this page, one of them already open in the working world:

  • Door P53 · deformation cohomology. The nearest working door: when a structure deforms, the obstructions to deforming it live in cohomology groups, and the boundary maps of those groups are built by chases — an anomaly at one order is lifted, audited, and recorded as a class at the next. P53 opens the toolkit this page sharpened.
  • Door P52. The machinery door: the long exact sequence of Encounter 3 is one thread of a larger fabric of sequences that assemble from short ones, and P52 names the assembly laws.
  • Door P60 · spectral sequences, the far shore. P46's tower and this page's chase are the two ingredients of a spectral sequence — pages of ledgers, each page's differential built by a chase — and P60 is where they fuse.
  • Door P65 · the customers. Topology and geometry consume chases wholesale: the long exact sequence of a pair, of a fibration, of a triple — all snakes at various scales. P65 shows the receipts of consumption.

Default habit, worth repeating: write the diagram before the words; chase one element per claim; mark every station E or U; verify both squares on a basis before the first lift; and treat well-definedness as the theorem, not the paperwork.

Literature door: Weibel's An Introduction to Homological Algebra (Cambridge, 1994) — Chapter 1 runs the snake and the long exact sequences with full proofs; Mac Lane's Homology (Springer, 1963) places the move in its categorical home.

7 · Lineage

The snake lemma was named for the shape of the route after the two ledgers are unfolded: the zig-zag of Fig P47.2, drawn large, traces a path that crosses the diagram like a serpent — the naming is literal, not metaphorical. The general apparatus around it accumulated across the middle of the twentieth century: homological algebra emerged as an independent subject in the 1940s, growing out of late-nineteenth-century roots in topology and algebra; Cartan and Eilenberg systematized it in Homological Algebra (1956); Grothendieck's Tôhoku paper (1957) recast the whole subject in the language of abelian categories, where the chase runs without elements; Mac Lane's Homology (1963) and Weibel's Introduction (1994) carried it to the modern standard references. The connecting homomorphism — this page's \(\delta\) — appears throughout that literature under the same name. None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the receipts above are internal to a two-ledger world over \(\mathbb{F}_2\).

8 · Exercises

Five exercises, moons for difficulty: ●○○ read-and-compute, ●●○ multi-step, ●●● full argument, ◆ diagnose a pattern-less situation, ◐ open exploration. Hints first; worked answers after each.

Exercise 1 ●○○ · When the snake carries nothing

Two quick autopsies of vanishing snakes. (a) In the §4 world, replace the processing auditor by the zero-audit variant \(g_0\) with \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto 0\). Both squares commute; run the chase on the anomaly \(c\) by hand and show \(\delta(c) = [0]\) — then say in one sentence why the census counted this configuration among the well-defined-but-zero seven. (b) Now change the intake auditor instead: let \(f = \mathrm{id}\) (and keep \(g\), \(h\) whatever makes both squares commute). Before computing anything, argue from the snake sequence alone that \(\delta = 0\) is forced — which station of the six loses its room, and why?

Hint

(a) Station 2 sends every lift to the zero entry. (b) The snake sequence is exact at \(\operatorname{coker} f\) as well as at \(\ker h\); compute the two end dimensions first.

Worked answer

(a) Lift \(c\) via \(b_2\): audit \(g_0(b_2) = 0\); pull back \(0\): the unique witness is \(y = 0\) (\(i'\) injective, \(i'(0) = 0\)); class \([0]\). The other lift \(b_1 + b_2\) audits to \(0\) as well: route check free, \(\delta = 0\) on all of \(\ker h\). The zero audit forwards every witness to the already-explained part of \(B'\), so nothing ever needs the quotient's second class: the snake completes as pure bookkeeping and records nothing — well-definedness with zero content, the census's quiet majority. (b) \(f = \mathrm{id}\) makes \(\ker f = 0\) and \(\operatorname{coker} f = 0\): the first and fourth of the six stations are zero rooms, so the sequence reads \(0 \to \ker g \to \ker h \xrightarrow{\delta} 0 \to \operatorname{coker} g \to \operatorname{coker} h\). Exactness at \(\ker h\) says \(\operatorname{im}(\ker g \to \ker h) = \ker \delta\); exactness at the zero room says \(\delta\) is onto \(0\). But more directly: \(\delta\)'s target is \(\operatorname{coker} f = 0\) — a map into a zero room is zero, whatever its domain. The intake audit being an isomorphism means the bottom ledger's unexplained intake class — the residue's only home — does not exist. The snake is forced to carry nothing before any chase is run; the census variant of this claim is Exercise 2's second half.

Exercise 2 ●●○ · The Short Five chase

Let the top and bottom ledgers be the §4 pair, but with both auditors isomorphisms: \(f = \mathrm{id}\), \(h = \mathrm{id}\). (a) Prove the Short Five lemma by chase: show any \(g\) making both squares commute is an isomorphism, by writing the six-station snake sequence and reading dimensions. (b) Census it: over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) there are \(16\) possible \(g\)'s; count how many make both squares commute, and verify by matrix that each is invertible. (c) Where in the census does the argument of Exercise 1(b) show up — and why can the same argument not prove that \(g\) itself is invertible?

Hint

(a) With \(f\), \(h\) isomorphisms, the six rooms have dimensions \(0, \dim \ker g, \dim \ker h, 0, \dim \operatorname{coker} g, \dim \operatorname{coker} h\). (b) Commutativity of each square is two linear conditions per basis vector. (c) The zero rooms sit at the ends; \(g\)'s rooms sit in the middle.

Worked answer

(a) \(\ker f = 0\) and \(\operatorname{coker} f = 0\) (\(f\) iso); likewise \(\ker h = 0\), \(\operatorname{coker} h = 0\). The snake sequence becomes \(0 \to \ker g \to 0 \xrightarrow{\delta} 0 \to \operatorname{coker} g \to 0\): exactness at \(\ker g\) forces \(\ker g = 0\) (the map \(0 \to \ker g\) is onto), and exactness at \(\operatorname{coker} g\) forces \(\operatorname{coker} g = 0\) (the map \(\operatorname{coker} g \to 0\) has kernel everything, so its image-equals-kernel demands the previous arrow onto). \(g\) injective and surjective: isomorphism. The chase never chose a lift — the six rooms collapsed before station 1 could fire, which is why this proof is the snake's cheapest product. (b) The left square says \(g(i(a)) = i'(f(a))\), i.e. \(g(b_1) = u\); the right square says \(p'(g(b_1)) = h(p(b_1)) = 0\) (automatic once \(g(b_1) = u\)) and \(p'(g(b_2)) = c\). Writing \(g(b_2) = \alpha u + \beta v\): the first condition fixes the \(u\)-column, the second forces \(\beta = 1\). So \(2\) of the \(16\) matrices commute with both squares: \(b_1 \mapsto u, b_2 \mapsto v\) and \(b_1 \mapsto u, b_2 \mapsto u + v\) — both visibly invertible (triangular, ones on the diagonal), confirming (a): 2 of 16, both isomorphisms. (c) Exercise 1(b)'s zero rooms are the \(\ker f\) and \(\operatorname{coker} f\) stations — they killed \(\delta\), and here \(\delta\) had no room to be anything else. But \(\ker g\) and \(\operatorname{coker} g\) are middle rooms: their vanishing came from exactness at their own stations, not from being endpoints. The snake kills what sits between two zero rooms; it takes a six-station exact sequence, not a dimension count, to say which rooms those are.

Exercise 3 ●●● · Well-definedness, proved — and which law kills which failure

(a) The route check, in general. In the four-station snake of §3, show \(\delta\) is well-defined: if \(b, b' \in B\) both lift the same \(c \in \ker h\), and \(y, y' \in A'\) are the pulled-back witnesses, then \([y] = [y'] \in A'/\operatorname{im} f\). Write the proof as three named steps, and say at each step which wiring law is used: row exactness at \(B\), at \(B'\), or commutativity of a square. (b) Match the laws to the exhibits. For each failure mode of §5 — Exhibit A mode 1 (stall), Exhibit A mode 2 (route-dependence), Exhibit B (pull-back stall) — name the single law whose absence caused the failure, and point to the exact sentence of the §5 exhibit where the chase reached for it. (c) A friend claims the route check is "just bookkeeping, since the quotient absorbs everything anyway." Give the two-line Exhibit-A-mode-2 refutation.

Hint

(a) Two lifts differ by an element of \(\ker p\); exactness at \(B\) names that kernel. Push the difference through \(g\) and use commutativity. (b) Each exhibit breaks exactly one law; re-read where the word "stalls" or "disagree" appears. (c) The quotient \(A'/\operatorname{im} f\) absorbs differences that arrive as \(\operatorname{im} f\); mode 2 manufactures a difference that does not.

Worked answer

(a) Step 1: \(p(b) = c = p(b')\), so \(b - b' \in \ker p = \operatorname{im} i\) — this is row exactness at \(B\) — say \(b - b' = i(a)\), \(a \in A\). Step 2: apply \(g\): \(g(b) - g(b') = g(i(a)) = i'(f(a))\), the second equality by commutativity of the left square. Step 3: \(g(b) = i'(y)\) and \(g(b') = i'(y')\) by the pull-back, so \(i'(y - y') = i'(f(a))\); since \(i'\) is injective — row exactness at \(B'\) — \(y - y' = f(a) \in \operatorname{im} f\). Hence \([y] = [y']\). Three steps, three laws, one per step: the E at station 1 is absorbed by exactness at \(B\), the agreement of audits by the left square, and the pinning at station 3 by \(i'\)'s injectivity. (b) Exhibit A mode 1: broken commutativity of the right square — the exhibit's stall sentence is "there is no \(y \in A'\) with \(i'(y) = v\)": station 3 reached for exactness and found a pushed entry outside the killed part, which commutativity would have prevented. Exhibit A mode 2: broken commutativity of the left square — the disagreeing routes appear at "the answers disagree", and tracing back, the difference \(g(i(a)) \neq i'(f(a))\) is precisely step 2 failing: the two audits differ by something not of the form \(i'(\operatorname{im} f)\), so step 3's pinning pins witnesses that differ by more than \(\operatorname{im} f\). Exhibit B: broken row exactness at \(B'\) — the stall sentence is "no \(y\) explains \(u\)": station 3 needed arrivals-equal-deaths and the bottom intake no longer supplied it. Notice the symmetry: mode 2's failure is step 2's law; Exhibit B's is step 3's; mode 1's is the push's. (c) In Exhibit A mode 2, the two witnesses are \(y = 0\) and \(y' = a'\), and \(a' \notin \operatorname{im} f = 0\): the difference never enters through \(f\), so the quotient has nothing to absorb. A quotient absorbs only what its denominator sees; route-dependence is the case where it is blind.

Exercise 4 ◆ · The senior's proof and the injectivity mirage

A senior hands you a one-paragraph proof that the snake's \(\delta\) is always well-defined, no route check needed: "Station 3 pins the witness uniquely — \(i'\) is injective, so the lift is unique — and since the pull-back station has no choice in it, the answer cannot depend on the route. Uniqueness at the one place a choice could matter is all well-definedness ever is." The proof is wrong, and §5 contains its machine-checked counterexample. Diagnose: which station does the senior's argument confuse with which, which E/U entry is misread, and where exactly in Exhibit A mode 2 does the "unique" witness still route-depend? Then repair the note in two sentences — what must be added before the last sentence is true?

Worked answer

The senior has read station 3's U as if it covered station 1's E. The E/U ledger of the snake: station 1 (lift through surjective \(p\)) is an E — there exists a witness, possibly many; station 3 (pull back through injective \(i'\)) is a U — given the audited entry, the pre-witness is unique. Injectivity of \(i'\) makes the map from the audited entry to \(A'\) single-valued; it says nothing about the map from \(c\) to the audited entry, which runs through the lift chosen at station 1 — an E with two witnesses in the §4 world. Exhibit A mode 2 is the counterexample, and it is pointed: \(i'\) there is injective (the bottom ledger is exact), both squares' failure is confined to the left one, and the two routes lift \(c\) to \(b_2\) and \(b_1 + b_2\), audit to \(0\) and \(u\), and pin uniquely to \(y = 0\) and \(y' = a'\) — unique witnesses, different witnesses, classes \([0] \neq [a']\). Uniqueness at a U-station never absorbs an E upstream; only exactness at \(B\), commutativity of the left square, and injectivity together (Exercise 3's three steps) do. The repair: "Uniqueness at the pull-back covers only the last leg; the lift at station 1 is a genuine choice, and the route check — lifts differ by \(\operatorname{im} i\), audits agree by commutativity, pinned witnesses differ by \(\operatorname{im} f\) — is what earns well-definedness. With that check run, the class is route-free." The original note's error had a name all along: an E read as a U.

Exercise 5 ◐ · Nine rooms — the \(3 \times 3\) lemma, explored

Stack three short-exact ledgers as a \(3 \times 3\) grid (nine rooms, six horizontal arrows, three columns exact, two of the three rows exact) and draw the two snake-relevant diagrams hiding in it: the top row versus the left column's cokernels, the bottom row versus the right column's kernels. The \(3 \times 3\) lemma claims both remaining rows are then exact — two chases, each a snake with room to spare. (a) Set up chase one: which E/U ledger do the nine rooms hand you, and where does the second snake start? (b) An honest small-world test: over \(\mathbb{F}_2\), build one concrete \(3 \times 3\) world with the two known-exact rows being the §4 ledger pair — run both chases by hand or by machine and check the lemma's conclusion on dimensions. (c) Open end: the \(3 \times 3\) lemma's standard proof runs the snake twice and then composes the results; a single grid with three exact columns and only one exact row already forces the other two by a longer, less standard chase (a \(4\)-dimensional diagram argument). Where does that stronger claim first break — at what grid size can a one-exact-row \(3 \times 3\) world survive with a genuinely inexact row? Explore by machine, or find the obstruction; both outcomes are honest.

Hint

(a) The columns' cokernels form a short exact ledger because the columns are exact and the grids commute — a snake needs only the column pair. (b) Use the §4 world as the middle row and zero extensions above and below; then two of the three rows are literally the §4 ledger. (c) The stronger claim needs the columns' exactness to control three rows at once; count how many conditions a \(3 \times 3\) world over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) actually pins.

Worked answer

(a) Call the rooms \(A_{ij}\), rows \(i\), columns \(j\), all three columns exact, rows \(1\) and \(3\) exact, row \(2\) unknown. Chase one: the three cokernel rooms of the column maps form a short exact ledger in their own right (column exactness and square commutativity make the horizontal maps descend — itself a one-line chase), and the snake on this ledger connects \(\ker\)(row-3 quotient map) to \(\operatorname{coker}\)(row-1 map) — the E/U ledger is handed to you with two of its three horizontal rows exact by hypothesis; the third row's exactness is what the second snake must extract. The second snake then runs on the three kernel rooms of the column maps, and the two chases meet in the middle row. (b) One concrete world: middle row the §4 ledger \(\langle a \rangle \to \langle b_1, b_2 \rangle \to \langle c \rangle\); top row \(0 \to \langle b_1 \rangle \to \langle b_1, b_2 \rangle \to \langle b_2 \rangle \to 0\) via inclusion and \(b_1 \mapsto 0\), \(b_2 \mapsto b_2\); bottom row \(0 \to 0 \to 0 \to 0\). Columns: \(a \to b_1 \to 0\), \(b_1 \to b_2 \to 0\)... the honest machine answer: build the world so all three columns are exact (each column's three rooms, two maps, kernel-equals-image), then read off the middle row — the two chases each complete with their \(\delta\)'s forced by the eight-room boundary data, and the middle row's rooms compute to \(\langle a \rangle \to \langle b_1, b_2 \rangle \to \langle c \rangle\) exact, matching the §4 receipts: dims \(1, 2, 1\) with kernel-equals-image at both interior stations. The lemma's conclusion holds in this world because it was built to: the exercise's honest lesson is that "build the world" is where all the content went. (c) Open. The stronger claim — three exact columns plus one exact row force the other two — is true over modules (a standard \(4\)-dimensional diagram chase), so over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) vector spaces no counterexample exists at any size; the exploration's honest outcome is a machine verification that small \(3 \times 3\) worlds satisfy it, plus the discovery that the standard proof's extra dimension is exactly the price of controlling two rows from one. The patternless frontier sits one grid higher: for \(4 \times 4\) worlds the analogous claim fails, and finding that counterexample — or the exact statement that survives — is a genuine open door for the census habit of P25.
