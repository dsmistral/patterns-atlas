The Patterns Atlas · P13 · the thirteenth move of the spine

Infinite descent

Some impossibilities refuse to name a wall: the target passes every census, survives every parity check, and still you are sure it cannot exist. For those, the natural numbers themselves supply the trap. Among the positive integers there is a ground floor — nothing descends forever. So prove that any solution of the forbidden kind secretly contains a smaller one of the same kind, and the whole family collapses at once: a smallest counterexample that begets a smaller accomplice cannot exist.

New word: descent. A descent is a proof that manufactures, out of any solution of the forbidden kind, another solution of the same kind that is strictly smaller — smaller in a positive-integer measure, so the manufacturing cannot run forever. New word: measure. The measure of a solution is any quantity attached to it that is a positive integer: a denominator, a coordinate, a sum, a count of prime factors. The whole move turns on choosing one that the transformation genuinely shrinks. New word: criminal. The criminal is the smallest counterexample — the least solution of the kind the claim forbids. The move convicts it by proving it always denounces a smaller accomplice; a least positive integer cannot do that, and that is B01's ground floor — well-ordering — doing the final work.

1 · Before we begin

The spine so far: P01 pinned the claim, P02 built the table, P03 attacked it with hostile cases, P04 paid for corners, P05 dressed every object in its uniform, P06 composed stuck objects inside identities, P07 built the objects nobody handed us, P08 divided by the twins, P09 found the quantity nothing can change, P10 forced collisions by counting, P11 exploded the opposite of the claim, and P12 named the wall the explosion leaned on. P12's closing pointer promised exactly this page: a named number-wall is often the front half of a full family theorem, and descent is the engine that finishes it. And two bridge pages pointed here as well: B03's dance of remainders and B04's smallest positive combination — both, it will turn out, were descents all along, running long before this page named them.

Here is the tension this page resolves. The good news: you have already run this move twice without naming it. B03 proved the dance must stop because the remainders strictly shrink — a machine that manufactures smaller remainders, step after step. B04 proved every member of the combination set is a multiple of its least element by showing that a trespasser would manufacture a smaller trespasser. Both times the crash landed in the same place: among the positive integers, a strictly descending chain is impossible. The bad news, paid up front: descent is strictly an impossibility move — it convicts, it never constructs, so a find me a solution problem gets nothing from it. The manufacturing step must be a theorem over every solution, never a sample — P12's necessity lesson wearing a new coat. And smaller must be strict and integer-valued, or the machine seizes: a measure that halves forever is a staircase with no ground floor, and a smaller solution that is not a solution of the same kind is no descent at all. Those two seductive failures — the phantom step and the jam — are what Example 2 and Exercise 4 put on the dissecting table.

2 · Meet the problem

Here is a machine you can run with pencil and paper. It eats a fraction and hands back a new one:

\[\frac{a}{b} \;\longmapsto\; \frac{2b-a}{a-b}.\]

Feed it \(17/12\) — a famous near-miss for \(\sqrt 2\): \(17^2 = 289\) and \(2 \cdot 12^2 = 288\), a single whisker apart. The machine swallows and returns \((24-17)/(17-12) = 7/5\). Feed it \(7/5\): out comes \((10-7)/(7-5) = 3/2\). Feed it \(3/2\): \((4-3)/(3-2) = 1/1\). Feed it \(1/1\): \((2-1)/(1-1)\) — the denominator has hit zero; the machine jams. Run it again from \(7/5\): \(3/2\), then \(1/1\), then the same jam. Run it from \(41/29\): \(17/12\), then \(7/5\), \(3/2\), \(1/1\), jam. Keep a tally of the denominators: \(12, 5, 2, 1\), jam. Then \(5, 2, 1\), jam. Then \(29, 12, 5, 2, 1\), jam. Every chain steps strictly downward and dies on the ground floor.

Two facts about this machine, and both are one line of algebra. Fact one: it preserves exactness for \(\sqrt 2\). By cross-multiplication, the output \((2b-a)/(a-b)\) equals the input \(a/b\) exactly when \(b(2b-a) = a(a-b)\) — and the two sides expand to \(2b^2 - ab\) and \(a^2 - ab\), equal exactly when \(a^2 = 2b^2\), which is the very statement that \(a/b = \sqrt 2\). Feed the machine an exact fraction for \(\sqrt 2\), and it hands you another one. Fact two: the output is strictly smaller. An exact \(a/b\) for \(\sqrt 2\) has \(a > b\) (because \(2b^2 > b^2\)) and \(a < 2b\) (because \(2b^2 < 4b^2\)), so the new denominator \(a-b\) is a positive integer strictly less than \(b\). Same exact value, smaller denominator, every single time.

Now the trap closes. Suppose — for contradiction, P11's frame — that \(\sqrt 2\) were a fraction \(a/b\) exactly. The machine would manufacture an endless chain of exact fractions whose denominators are positive integers in strict decline: \(b\), then a smaller one, then a smaller one, without end. But B01's ground floor forbids that: every nonempty set of positive integers has a least element, so a strictly descending chain of positive integers cannot exist. The supposition is dead — and not it alone: no fraction anywhere equals \(\sqrt 2\), the whole family at once. No census was taken, no parity checked, no search run; the natural numbers' refusal to descend forever did all the work. (P11 cornered \(\sqrt 2\) with a parity census; this is a different engine for the same verdict, and M01 sets the proofs side by side.)

The near-misses deserve their honest reading before we move on. The chains you ran jammed — but an exact fraction could never jam: jamming means the denominator hits zero, \(a = b\), which for an exact solution would force \(b^2 = 2b^2\) and so \(b = 0\), impossible. The fractions \(17/12\), \(7/5\), \(41/29\) are the ones that almost qualify, and the machine patiently walks each down to the place where its nearness runs out. Where those near-misses come from, and why they crowd ever closer to \(\sqrt 2\) without ever landing, is Exercise 5's thread.

3 · Name the move

Generalize the encounter and the pattern falls out. The machine was one costume; the plot underneath is always the same three acts.

Pattern P13 — Infinite descent. When the claim is that no solution of a certain kind exists — no fraction equals \(\sqrt 2\), no triangle sits on the lattice, no counterexample survives — run the criminal investigation, in three acts. (1) Choose a measure: attach to every would-be solution a positive integer — a denominator, a coordinate, a sum, a count — and say precisely what "smaller" means. (2) Prove the manufacturing theorem: from any solution of the forbidden kind, construct another solution of the same kind with strictly smaller measure — a theorem over every solution, never a sample of the ones you tried; the construction can be an explicit shrinking formula, a divisibility strip that divides out a common factor, or a least-element argument that squeezes a trespasser below the least. (3) Crash on the ground floor: the measures of the would-be solutions form a nonempty set of positive integers with no least element — because the least one would manufacture a smaller — and B01's well-ordering forbids exactly that. Conclusion: no solution of the kind exists, none ever, and the family the kind covers is sealed at once.

Or, in words: among the positive integers there is a ground floor, so a smallest counterexample is the sturdiest object in the world — set it on a machine that manufactures smaller accomplices and it cannot survive its own trial. The whole art is the manufacturing step: it must be a theorem over every solution, the new pair must stay the same kind, and the measure must shrink by whole integers — strictly, not politely. When all three hold, one machine kills an infinite family; when any one fails, the descent is a phantom, and phantoms convict nobody.

Box P13.A — the three demands, and what breaks when each fails.

  1. The measure must be a positive integer, strictly decreasing. Denominators, coordinates, sums of coordinates — integers, so B01's ground floor exists and each step descends by at least one floor. What breaks: the measure without a floor — §5's first exhibit watches a descent run happily down the staircase \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \ldots\) that never lands anywhere, because among the fractions there is no ground floor at all.
  2. The step must be a theorem over every solution — the same kind out as in. The smaller pair must still solve the original equation, and the proof of that must cover every solution, the smallest included. What breaks: two faces of one failure — the phantom step, a shrinking "theorem" that some legal solution simply walks around (§5's second exhibit), and the jam, where the manufactured pair falls out of the kind and the machine idles (§5's third exhibit, where \(\sqrt 4\) politely declines to be refuted).
  3. The contradiction must land on well-ordering — honestly. The chain of ever-smaller measures must actually be impossible, which means the crash site is B01, cited, not hand-waved; a descent that "sort of cannot go on forever" has not finished its proof. What breaks: the circular hope — §5's fourth exhibit catches a proof whose "smaller" was quietly the same object in a mirror, so nothing descended and nothing crashed.

The three-key diagnosis — when to reach for this move. Claim type: an impossibility, and specifically one whose would-be solutions live among the integers — no fraction, no solution in positive integers, no lattice object. Problem shape: solutions transform into other solutions — a machine eats them, a factor divides out of them, a remainder replaces them — so there is motion to exploit; and the motion comes with a natural size. Tools at hand: well-ordering from B01, a divisibility or inequality handle from B02–B03, and the contradiction frame of P11. If the claim is an existence claim instead — find me a solution — the descent is the wrong door: it convicts, it never constructs, and §5's last exhibit draws that boundary.

4 · The move at work

Two worked examples, two domains, one pose: the manufacturing theorem proved over every solution, the crash landed on B01's floor, the boundary read honestly. The first is a debt this page owes two bridge pages — B03 and B04 proved their theorems with machinery that was, in retrospect, this move; here the move gets its name and its general shape. The second takes the same engine to geometry and shows a wall dissolve the moment the integers step out of the plane.

Example 1 (numbers) — the dance that must stop, and the identity that follows

The question, inherited: why must Euclid's dance terminate — and why must the greatest common divisor be reachable as a combination \(ax + by\)? Run the dance from \((2026, 1491)\), as B03 did: \(2026 = 1 \cdot 1491 + 535\), then \(1491 = 2 \cdot 535 + 421\), \(535 = 1 \cdot 421 + 114\), \(421 = 3 \cdot 114 + 79\), \(114 = 1 \cdot 79 + 35\), \(79 = 2 \cdot 35 + 9\), \(35 = 3 \cdot 9 + 8\), \(9 = 1 \cdot 8 + 1\), \(8 = 8 \cdot 1 + 0\). The gcd is \(1\), and the remainders march \(1491, 535, 421, 114, 79, 35, 9, 8, 1, 0\) — strictly downward, floor after floor, until the ground. B03 leaned on this shrinkage to promise termination; now name what the shrinkage is.

The termination, as a descent. Each step of the dance manufactures a strictly smaller nonnegative remainder: the new remainder is smaller than the number it was divided by, which was itself the previous remainder — every step lands at least one floor below the last. Suppose, for contradiction, a dance that never stops. Its remainders would form an infinite strictly descending chain of nonnegative integers — a set of values with no least element — and B01's well-ordering (one floor below the positive integers, the nonnegative integers are just as well ordered) forbids that outright. So the dance must stop: the descent convicts the forbidden kind a non-stopping run, and the manufacturing theorem is the dance step itself, proved over every input pair at once. There is no sample here, and there cannot be: the step \(b = qa + r\) with \(0 \le r < a\) is B01's division algorithm, a theorem about every pair of numbers that ever was. The dance is as long as it dares — consecutive Fibonacci numbers are the worst case, and \((233, 144)\) takes all of \(11\) divisions — and still the staircase cannot skip the floor.

The identity, as the smallest criminal. B04 proved that the gcd of \(a\) and \(b\) is reachable as \(ax + by\); here is that proof again, run with this page's vocabulary, because it is the purest criminal investigation in the book. Let \(S\) be the set of positive values of \(ax + by\), over all integer choices of \(x\) and \(y\). The set is nonempty — \(a\) itself is in it, as \(a \cdot 1 + b \cdot 0\) — so by B01 it has a least element; call it \(d\), the criminal. Now put any other member \(m\) of \(S\) on trial. Divide \(m\) by \(d\): \(m = qd + r\) with \(0 \le r < d\), by the division algorithm. Both \(m\) and \(d\) are combinations of \(a\) and \(b\), so their difference \(r = m - qd\) is a combination too — combinations are closed under integer multiples and differences. If \(r > 0\), then \(r\) is a member of \(S\) strictly smaller than its least element: the trespasser has manufactured a smaller trespasser, and the ground floor says no. If \(r = 0\), then \(m\) was a multiple of \(d\) all along. Either way the verdict is the same: every member of \(S\) is a multiple of \(d\), without exception. It follows that \(d\) divides \(a\) and \(b\) (both sit in \(S\)), and that no larger common divisor can exist (any common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\) divides every combination, \(d\) included) — so \(d\) is the gcd, and it is reachable: \(d = ax + by\) for the witnesses the set provided. The dance locates the criminal in practice — for \((2026, 1491)\) it climbs back up the divisions to the certificate \(1 = -170 \cdot 2026 + 231 \cdot 1491\), where \(-170 \cdot 2026 = -344{,}420\) and \(231 \cdot 1491 = 344{,}421\) — and the descent proves the criminal exists in principle. Two engines, one verdict: the machine and the proof — B03 and B04, the bridge pages that ran this move in service of the spine long before the spine named it.

Moves used: P13 (the termination read as a forbidden non-stopping run, and the least element of \(S\) run through the trespasser squeeze), B03 (the dance and its worst case), B04 (the identity, the set \(S\), and the certificate), B01 (the division algorithm, well-ordering, and the ground floor), P11 (the contradiction frame that assumes the never-ending dance and the trespasser).

Example 2 (numbers, then geometry) — \(\sqrt 3\) refuses twice

The first refusal, by the strip. Consider the equation \(x^2 = 3y^2\): are there positive integer solutions? Suppose one. Since \(3\) divides \(x^2\), a two-line census — P12's instrument — forces \(3\) to divide \(x\) itself: squares leave remainder \(0\) or \(1\) on division by \(3\) (check \(0, 1, 2\): their squares are \(0, 1, 4 \equiv 1\)), so \(3 \mid x^2\) pins \(x \equiv 0 \pmod 3\). Write \(x = 3u\). Substituting, \(9u^2 = 3y^2\), so \(3u^2 = y^2\) — and the same census now pins \(3 \mid y\): write \(y = 3v\). Substituting again, \(3u^2 = 9v^2\), so \(u^2 = 3v^2\). Read what happened: the pair \((u, v) = (x/3,\, y/3)\) is a new positive-integer solution of the same equation, with every coordinate — every measure you might name — divided by \(3\). The manufacturing theorem is proved over every solution at once (the census covers every residue class, no sampling); the new pair stays in kind; the measure shrinks strictly. So the smallest solution, if any existed, would manufacture a smaller one — impossible on B01's floor. No positive solutions exist, and as a free corollary \(\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction: \(x^2 = 3y^2\) is exactly the claim \(x/y = \sqrt 3\). This is the common-divisor strip — the catalogue's second shape — and notice its economy: no shrinking formula was invented, the equation's own divisibility did the manufacturing.

The second refusal, in geometry's coat. Lean a geometric question on that wall: can an equilateral triangle have all three vertices on the integer lattice — the grid points of graph paper? Translate one vertex to the origin (subtracting a lattice point from all three preserves the lattice) and call the two side vectors from it \(\mathbf{u} = (a,b)\) and \(\mathbf{v} = (c,d)\), integer vectors, nonzero, not parallel. Equilateral means \(|\mathbf{u}|^2 = |\mathbf{v}|^2 = s\) and the angle between them is \(60^\circ\), so the dot product is \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = s/2\) — that is \(\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12\), times the two lengths. Now the area, and the anchor is B12's two-squares identity \((a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2) = (ac+bd)^2 + (ad-bc)^2\), which says precisely \((\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 + \det(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2 |\mathbf{v}|^2\). The doubled area of the triangle is \(|\det(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})| = |ad - bc| = k\), a positive integer. Substitute the identity's two facts: \(k^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2|\mathbf{v}|^2 - (\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v})^2 = s^2 - (s/2)^2 = \tfrac34 s^2\), so \((2k)^2 = 3s^2\). But \(2k\) and \(s\) are positive integers — and that is exactly the equation the strip just refuted. The triangle cannot exist.

The detector, and the boundary. Before any proof, a scan agrees: every triangle with vertices among the hundred lattice points of a \(10 \times 10\) grid — all \(161{,}700\) triples — is checked, and none is equilateral. Honest, and worthless as a proof, exactly as P02 taught: a silent detector seals nothing. After the proof, read the boundary the way P12 taught — and the wall dissolves one dimension up. In the three-dimensional integer lattice the same question reopens, and the answer flips: the points \((0,0,0)\), \((1,1,0)\), \((1,0,1)\) — every squared distance equal to \(2\) — form a genuine equilateral triangle on the 3D grid. The proof pinches shut exactly there. In the plane, the doubled area is one integer, the determinant, and the identity drags it into \((2k)^2 = 3s^2\) where the strip refutes it. In three dimensions the doubled area is the length of a cross-product — the square root of a sum of three squares — and a sum of three squares need not itself be a square, so the integer witness the proof required simply stops existing. The wall was never about triangles; it was about the arithmetic of planar areas — number theory in geometry's coat, which is where descent lives most of its working life.

Moves used: P13 (the strip descent on \(x^2 = 3y^2\), and the crash that kills the triangle), P11 (the assumption that the triangle exists — the frame), P12 (the mod-3 census as the strip's engine; the boundary read as a compass), B12 (the identity \(\det^2 + \mathrm{dot}^2 = |\mathbf{u}|^2 |\mathbf{v}|^2\) that anchors the area), P02 (the grid scan that made the impossibility believable before it was proved, and the 3D boundary check where the wall dissolves).

5 · When the move fails

Each demand in Box P13.A has a favourite way to break, and each break has been somebody's published wrong proof. Five exhibits, then the scope.

Exhibit 1 — the measure without a floor. A student marvels: given any positive fraction, half of it is a smaller positive fraction — so there is no smallest fraction, "just like the criminal," and by the descent's logic fractions cannot exist. The verdict is absurd, and the autopsy is one line: the descent crashes on B01's floor, which is made of integers. A fraction-valued measure descends \(1, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \ldots\) forever without contradiction, because among the fractions there is no ground floor. The move never promised that "strictly smaller" crashes anything — only that strictly smaller positive integers do. Mitigation: re-measure in integers — denominators, coordinates, counts — and the floor reappears — then check what actually shrinks: the halving chain has denominators 2, 4, 8, 16, …, which grow, and that is exactly why it can run forever without ever contradicting B01.

Exhibit 2 — the phantom step. A student refutes \(x^2 + y^2 = 2z^2\): "mod \(2\) forces all three variables even, strip the common \(2\), and the solution shrinks forever — impossible, so no solutions." Every sentence of the machine is polished; the theorem it runs on is false. The census actually says: \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) forces \(x\) and \(y\) to have the same parity — and both odd is legal. The witness walks around the step: \((1,1,1)\), since \(1 + 1 = 2\); and \((1, 7, 5)\), since \(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\). Solutions exist, the "all even" step was sampled necessity, and the descent convicted nobody — P12's lesson wearing its descent costume, caught by P03's discipline: before trusting a step, hunt the hostile witness that walks around it.

Exhibit 3 — the jam, and the machine that idles. Point Example 2's strip at \(x^2 = 4y^2\) and it misfires beautifully. The census gives \(4 \mid x^2 \Rightarrow 2 \mid x\), so \(x = 2u\), and substituting yields \(4u^2 = 4y^2\), so \(u^2 = y^2\) — the \(4\) has vanished, and the manufactured pair \((u, y)\) solves a different equation. The kind was left; the descent jammed. And it had to: solutions exist — \((2,1)\), since \(4 = 4 \cdot 1\), and \(\sqrt 4 = 2 = 2/1\) is a fraction, so any "proof" of impossibility was doomed from the first step. The shrinking machine jams the same way: run its \(n\)-shaped cousin \((nb - a)/(a - b)\) at \(n = 4\) on the fraction \(2/1\), and it returns \((4 - 2)/(2 - 1) = 2/1\) — a fixed point, idle, because the machine's decrease needs \(a < 2b\), which for exact fractions means \(n < 4\); for \(n > 4\) the machine even grows. The formula works exactly for \(n = 2\) and \(n = 3\); the strip works whenever the squarefree part of \(n\) exceeds \(1\); and the honest boundary — which \(\sqrt n\) are fractions — is Exercise 2's to draw.

Exhibit 4 — the criminal slips the dragnet. The same equation, one more attempt: a student proves "if \(x\) and \(y\) are both even, then \((x/2, y/2)\) is a smaller solution of \(x^2 = 4y^2\)" — true, checked: \((x/2)^2 = 4(y/2)^2\). Strip forever, no solutions? No — the step is a theorem about the even-even solutions only, and the smallest solution \((2,1)\) has an odd coordinate: the criminal slips the dragnet, nothing descends, nothing crashes. The manufacturing theorem must cover every solution, the least included — the necessity lesson's descent-side twin, and the sharpest of the five: a descent that skips the smallest criminal is not a weak descent, it is no descent at all.

Exhibit 5 — the one-way wall. Descent convicts; it never constructs. No lattice equilateral exists in the plane — Example 2's verdict — but the 3D witness \((0,0,0)\), \((1,1,0)\), \((1,0,1)\) was not found by descent: it was found by trying small vectors, the way P02 hunts and P07 builds. Existence claims get nothing from this move, and the failure is informative in both directions: a descent that jams (Exhibit 3) is evidence the solutions exist — read the jam as a compass, the way P12 read the wall's boundary — and go hunt them.

Where descent lives, honestly. The move's natural habitat is the integers — its measures are integer-valued, and its geometry appearances are number theory in disguise (Example 2's crash was an equation wearing a triangle). Continuous impossibilities — no smallest positive real, no next real number after \(0\) — are different walls entirely: the fractions of Exhibit 1 have no floor, and catching what crowds in a bounded continuous room is P21's move, not this one. And one honest open problem: the \(3n+1\) iteration — halve if even, triple and add one if odd — looks like a descent machine, its numbers seeming to fall toward \(1\), and nobody has proved the manufacturing theorem that every starting number lands. The most famous open problem shaped like a descent is a live lesson: a machine that looks like a descent is a conjecture until the theorem over every input is proved. None of this is load-bearing here.

6 · What to try next

The descent convicted, or it jammed — each ending has a door, and the jam itself is a compass.

The promotion. When a contradiction's crash turns out to be "the counterexample shrinks," the shrinking was the engine all along: promote it to a named descent, state the measure, prove the step over every solution, and the one-off proof becomes a family theorem — the same promotion P12 ran on walls, run on machines.

M01 — \(\sqrt 2\), three ways. This page's machine, P11's parity census, and the canonical form of P05, side by side on one target — and the return of Exercise 5's near-miss family, with its proper name.

M02 — sums of two squares. Descent's next big cameo: the complete story that P12 could only point at is a descent proof, wearing the census as its first move.

P30 — localize at a prime. The strip's modern descendant: instead of stripping all common factors at once, work one prime at a time and reassemble the answers.

P66 — induction. The same floor, climbed: B01's well-ordering read upwards is induction, read downwards is descent — two directions of one ground floor, and a proof that stalls in one direction often runs in the other.

P71 — the extremal principle. The smallest criminal as a working habit for problems that are not impossibility claims: take the extreme object and squeeze it until it confesses.

And the default, in one sentence. When a "no solutions" claim has integers in it, ask before searching: what would the smallest criminal look like, and can one line of algebra make it denounce a smaller accomplice? If yes — formula, strip, or squeeze — the family falls without a single search; if the measure refuses to be an integer, the problem belongs to P21; if a witness walks around the step, the claim is probably false and the witness is your answer; and if the machine idles, believe it — the solutions exist, and it is time to hunt, not to convict.

7 · Lineage

The paper trail runs backwards through the margins. The oldest descent in daily use is the oldest algorithm in the book: Euclid's dance, from the Elements around 300 BC — Example 1's re-reading of its termination was, in effect, a tour of the oldest descent still running. The irrationality of \(\sqrt 2\) was argued by the Greek tradition — the proofs the Pythagoreans knew, or the appendix to Book X, are all attribution-debated, and this page takes no side. In 1225 Fibonacci stated, without proof, an equivalent of the theorem this move is famous for. That theorem is Fermat's: reading Claude Bachet's 1621 Latin edition of Diophantus's Arithmetica in Toulouse, Pierre de Fermat ran descents in the margins, and in a 1659 letter to Christiaan Huygens he named the method — descente infinie ou indéfinie — calling it the singular route he had found for proving negative propositions, the first being that no right-angled triangle in numbers has square area. It is the one complete proof in number theory he ever left, published after his death by his son Clément-Samuel in the 1670 edition of the Arithmetica with the marginal notes — and, unlike the other famous marginal claim nearby, the descent proof fits in the margin. Euler built descent engines of his own on this foundation — the two-squares theorem's proof is one — and the right-triangle theorem's full proof needs a primitive-triple machine this book has not yet built; it is deferred, by name, to the pages that will build it. None of this history is load-bearing here — but every time a proof kills an infinite family by manufacturing smaller criminals, it is running the thirteenth move of the spine, exactly as Fermat ran it in the margins of Bachet's Diophantus.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — feed the machine

Run the fraction machine \((2b-a)/(a-b)\) from \(41/29\), and then from \(99/70\). Record each chain down to the jam, with the tally of denominators. Then explain, in two sentences, why an exact fraction for \(\sqrt 2\) could never jam — and what that non-jamming has to do with B01.

Hint

The arithmetic is honest division and subtraction only. The jam is a zero denominator, which means \(a = b\) — what does \(a^2 = 2b^2\) say about that?

Worked answer

From \(41/29\): \((58-41)/(41-29) = 17/12\), then \(7/5\), then \(3/2\), then \(1/1\), then \((2-1)/(1-1)\) — jam. Denominators: \(29, 12, 5, 2, 1\), jam. From \(99/70\): \((140-99)/(99-70) = 41/29\), and then the same tail: \(17/12, 7/5, 3/2, 1/1\), jam — denominators \(70, 29, 12, 5, 2, 1\), jam. Every step strictly smaller; the machine confirms both chains end to end. Why no exact fraction jams: an exact \(a/b\) for \(\sqrt 2\) satisfies \(a^2 = 2b^2\) with \(b > 0\), which forces \(a > b\) — the two sides \(a^2\) and \(b^2\) differ by a full factor of \(2\) — so the denominator \(a - b\) is a positive integer, never zero. So an exact fraction would descend through strictly smaller positive denominators forever, with no jam and no floor: an endless strictly descending chain of positive integers, which B01's well-ordering forbids. The near-misses jam precisely because they are near-misses — their exactness runs out — and that exhaust is the proof.

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the boundary of the strip

New word: squarefree. A number is squarefree if no perfect square bigger than \(1\) divides it: \(6\) and \(3\) are squarefree, \(12 = 4 \cdot 3\) and \(4\) are not. (a) Prove that \(\sqrt 6\) is irrational, using the common-divisor strip. (b) Recall §5's jam: the same proof shape fails on \(x^2 = 4y^2\), and the witness \((2,1)\) exists — so \(4\) sits on the far side of a boundary. (c) Prove that \(\sqrt{12}\) is irrational anyway, by letting the strip pass once and watching which equation the pass lands on. State the boundary you have now mapped.

Hint

For (a): \(6 \mid x^2\) forces both \(2 \mid x\) and \(3 \mid x\); two coprime divisors of \(x\) multiply. For (c): do not strip the whole \(12\) at once — substitute \(x = 6u\), simplify, and look at the equation you are holding.

Worked answer

(a) Suppose \(x^2 = 6y^2\). From \(6 \mid x^2\): \(2 \mid x\) (squares mod 2 are \(0, 1\)) and \(3 \mid x\) (squares mod 3 are \(0, 1\)); since \(2\) and \(3\) are coprime, \(6 \mid x\): write \(x = 6u\). Then \(36u^2 = 6y^2\), so \(6u^2 = y^2\), and the same census gives \(6 \mid y\): \(y = 6v\), so \(6u^2 = 36v^2\), i.e. \(u^2 = 6v^2\). The pair \((x/6, y/6)\) solves the same equation, every measure divided by \(6\): strictly smaller, same kind, so the smallest solution would denounce a smaller accomplice — impossible on B01's floor. No positive solutions, and \(\sqrt 6\) is not a fraction; the machine checks the strip's census on every \(x\) to \(300\). (b) On \(x^2 = 4y^2\) the pass gives \(2 \mid x\), \(x = 2u\), \(4u^2 = 4y^2\), \(u^2 = y^2\) — a different equation, the kind left behind, and \((2,1)\) stands there unrefuted: \(\sqrt 4 = 2/1\) is a fraction, so the boundary is real. (c) From \(x^2 = 12y^2\): \(12 \mid x^2\) gives \(2 \mid x\) and \(3 \mid x\), so \(6 \mid x\): \(x = 6u\). Then \(36u^2 = 12y^2\), so \(3u^2 = y^2\) — the census pins \(3 \mid y\): \(y = 3v\), so \(3u^2 = 9v^2\), i.e. \(u^2 = 3v^2\). The strip pass did not stay in kind; it landed on Example 2's refuted equation, strictly smaller. So a solution of \(x^2 = 12y^2\) would manufacture a solution of \(u^2 = 3v^2\) — which cannot exist — and \(\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt 3\) is not a fraction. The boundary mapped: the strip refuses every \(n\) whose squarefree part exceeds \(1\); when the squarefree part is \(1\) — when \(n\) is a perfect square — the strip jams and the square root is a fraction, as \(4\) testifies. What the square factor does is shrink the equation toward its squarefree core, where the real wall stands — the same prime-by-prime story that P30 will build into machinery, deferred by name.

Exercise 3 ●●● — three squares' worth of refusal

Prove that \(x^2 + y^2 = 3z^2\) has no solutions in positive integers — the statement behind "three times a square is never a sum of two squares." The proof needs two moves in cooperation: a wall from P12 and a strip from this page. Find the split, prove both halves, and say exactly which move kills which case.

Hint

Split on the parity of \(z\). If \(z\) is odd, ask what \(3z^2\) leaves on division by \(4\), and compare with the classes a sum of two squares can reach. If \(z\) is even, ask what \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 4\) forces on \(x\) and \(y\) — squares are \(0\) or \(1 \pmod 4\), and the only way two of them sum to \(0\) is \(0 + 0\).

Worked answer

Suppose a positive solution exists, and take one with \(z\) smallest — the criminal, nominated for trial. Odd \(z\): then \(z^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), so \(3z^2 \equiv 3 \pmod 4\). But squares are \(0\) or \(1 \pmod 4\), so \(x^2 + y^2\) reaches only \(0, 1, 2 \pmod 4\) — P12's census, exactly as it sealed \(2027\). Class \(3\) is unreachable: the wall kills every odd-\(z\) solution. Even \(z\): write \(z = 2w\); then \(x^2 + y^2 = 12w^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 4\), and the only way two classes from \(\{0, 1\}\) sum to \(0 \pmod 4\) is \(0 + 0\): both \(x\) and \(y\) are even. Strip the common \(2\): \(x = 2r\), \(y = 2s\), so \(4r^2 + 4s^2 = 3z^2\), giving \(r^2 + s^2 = 3w^2\) with \(w = z/2 < z\) — a same-kind solution with strictly smaller measure. The even case descends; the criminal denounces a smaller accomplice, and B01's floor forbids. So the wall kills the odd case, the strip kills the even case — there is no third case, and no solution exists. The machine agrees twice over: no positive solutions with \(z \le 250\) in a full scan, and both censuses (squares mod 4; the parity implication) hold on every check. Notice the cooperation — the wall alone leaves the even case open, the strip alone leaves the odd case open, and the proof needs both moves named and credited. That cooperation, one problem holding two patterns, is what the multi-pattern pages — beginning with M02 — exist to train; this exercise is its first taste, and its punchline is on the ladder's schedule.

Exercise 4 ◆ — referee the three students

The claim on trial: the descent machinery of this page, pointed at new targets, gives correct verdicts. Three students hand in verdicts on new targets. Student A: "\(\sqrt 4\) is irrational. I ran the fraction machine \((4b-a)/(a-b)\) on \(2/1\), and the machine never jams — it runs forever, always descending. Impossible: no fraction equals \(\sqrt 4\)." Student B: "\(x^2 + y^2 = 2z^2\) has no positive solutions. Mod \(2\) forces \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) all even; strip the common \(2\) and the solutions shrink forever — impossible." Student C: "Descent proves no fraction equals \(\sqrt 2\) — but my calculator says \(\sqrt 2 = 1.41421356\), close enough that I'll call it a fraction and be done." Referee: verdicts, and the reason each passes or fails.

Hint

For A: what does the machine actually do at \(2/1\) — descend, or stand still? Compute the output. For B: hunt a small witness, the way P03 taught. For C: what is the claim, exactly — equality, or closeness? Which one did the descent prove anything about?

Worked answer

A: rejected — the machine idles, and idling is not descending. At \(2/1\) the \(n = 4\) machine returns \((4 - 2)/(2 - 1) = 2/1\): a fixed point, exactly §5's Exhibit 3. The decrease needs the output's denominator below the input's, which costs \(a < 2b\), which for exact fractions means \(n < 4\); at \(n = 4\) the machine stands perfectly still — "runs forever" with nothing shrinking, which convicts nobody. And the target is a fraction, \(\sqrt 4 = 2/1\), so no working descent could exist: the idle machine is the boundary of Exercise 2 telling the truth. B: rejected — the step is a phantom. The honest census says \(x^2 + y^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) forces \(x\) and \(y\) to share parity — both odd is legal, and \(z\) is unconstrained. The witness: \((1,1,1)\), since \(1 + 1 = 2 = 2 \cdot 1\); also \((1,7,5)\), since \(1 + 49 = 50 = 2 \cdot 25\). Solutions exist, the all-even step was sampled necessity, and the strip refuted nothing — Exhibit 2 word for word. C: half upheld, half corrected — the verdict stands, the sentence fails. The \(\sqrt 2\) descent is exactly §2's, and it is sound. But "close enough to call it a fraction" trades on an unpinned claim: \(141421356/100000000\) approximates \(\sqrt 2\) — within about \(2.4 \times 10^{-9}\), the machine confirms — without equaling it, and equality is the only thing anyone ever denied. Feed the approximating fraction to §2's machine and it walks the same staircase — a dozen shrinking floors — until its nearness runs out and the walk collapses; Exercise 1's fractions end that journey at the jam, and every fraction that is not exactly \(\sqrt 2\) ends it somewhere. None of them lands. Closeness was never the crime; being a fraction is, and no calculator digit settles that question either way. P01's discipline, enforced one last time: pin the claim before the machinery runs.

Exercise 5 ◐ — run the machine backwards

§2's machine walks near-misses down to the jam; run it backwards and it builds them. Starting from \((1,1)\), repeatedly apply \((p, q) \mapsto (p + 2q,\, p + q)\), and you generate \((3, 2), (7, 5), (17, 12), (41, 29), (99, 70), (239, 169), (577, 408)\) — the page's entire supply of near-misses. (a) Verify that \(p^2 - 2q^2\) alternates \(-1, +1\) along the family, and say what the sign says about which side of \(\sqrt 2\) the fraction sits on. (b) Prove that the backwards step is genuinely the machine's inverse: show that the forward machine \((2b - a, a - b)\) sends each family member to the previous one. (c) Watch the errors \(|p/q - \sqrt 2|\): compute the first eight and check they shrink strictly. (d) The family brackets \(\sqrt 2\), tightening like a vice and never landing — the equation \(p^2 - 2q^2 = \pm 1\) has a name and a full theory, and this book defers it, by name. Where?

Hint

For (a): compute the eight values \(1 - 2\), \(9 - 8\), \(49 - 50\), and so on — the alternation is exact, not approximate, and comparing \(p/q\) with \(\sqrt 2\) is comparing \(p^2\) with \(2q^2\). For (b): feed \((3,2)\) to the forward machine; then \((7, 5)\); then generalize with one line of algebra.

Worked answer

(a) The values: \(1 - 2 = -1\), \(9 - 8 = +1\), \(49 - 50 = -1\), \(289 - 288 = +1\), \(1681 - 1682 = -1\), \(9801 - 9800 = +1\), \(57121 - 57122 = -1\), \(332929 - 332928 = +1\) — perfect alternation, starting and ending on the machine's checks. The sign: \(p^2 - 2q^2 < 0\) means \(p^2 < 2q^2\), i.e. \(p/q < \sqrt 2\) — the \(-1\) members sit below, the \(+1\) members above: \(1/1 = 1\) below, \(3/2 = 1.5\) above, \(7/5 = 1.4\) below, \(17/12 \approx 1.4167\) above. The family brackets its target, hop by hop. (b) The forward machine on \((3,2)\): \((2 \cdot 2 - 3, 3 - 2) = (1,1)\) — the previous member. On \((7,5)\): \((10 - 7, 7 - 5) = (3, 2)\). In general, the successor map \((p, q) \mapsto (p + 2q, p + q)\) has forward image \((2(p+q) - (p + 2q), (p + 2q) - (p + q)) = (p, q)\) — one line of algebra, checked on all eight members: the backwards step and the forward machine are exact inverses, so the family is precisely the machine's orbit, run in reverse. (c) The errors: \(|1 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.4142\), \(|3/2 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0858\), \(|7/5 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0142\), \(|17/12 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.00245\), \(|41/29 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.000420\), \(|99/70 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.000072\), \(|239/169 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0000124\), \(|577/408 - \sqrt 2| \approx 0.0000021\) — strictly shrinking, roughly a factor of \(5\) to \(6\) per hop, the machine confirms on all eight. (d) The equation is Pell's equation, and its theory — why the near-misses exist, why they crowd as they do, and what orbits like this family really are — is deferred, by name, to M01, where \(\sqrt 2\) returns with three proofs and its family in tow. The descent proved no member ever lands; the backwards machine shows the misses marching in anyway, forever — the two facts do not fight; together they are the whole story of how densely the fractions crowd the line.
