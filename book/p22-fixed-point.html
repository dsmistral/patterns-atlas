The Patterns Atlas · P22 · the fifth move of tier 2

Iterate to a fixed point

A builder needs the diagonal of a unit square and refines one guess by one rule: average your guess with the number divided by it. Hit after hit the card settles toward 1.4142135624, and the claim this page teaches sounds like folklore: repeat a rule like that forever, and the guesses cannot help landing on the answer. The finite tools all decline the warranty. P02's tables stop where the data stops, and forever is not on any card; P15's walls trap digits but assume the sequence already tamed — its own Babylonian contrast ran this very card and deferred the receipt to exactly here; P21's machine catches crowds, but promises neither that the catch is unique nor what it costs to reach. The move: name a room the map returns into, prove the pull — each hit drawn toward the last by a fixed factor \(c < 1\), a contraction — and read off three certificates, one sentence each: the fixed point exists (P21's nested rooms, run by the orbit's own shrinking gaps), it is unique (two of them cannot survive the pull's arithmetic), and the error is priced in advance (\(e_n \le c^n e_0\), a fixed digit rate per hit, guaranteed before the first hit lands). Three runs in public: the Babylonian \(\sqrt{2}\), two thousand years older than its proof, whose honest price is 0.301 digits per hit until the flattening at the answer doubles digits every hit; Newton on P21's plastic constant, where four steps beat forty halvings; and Dottie, the cosine's fixed point, slow and honest at a sixth of a digit per hit. The wreckage gets three exhibits: a repeller that pushes away, a two-cycle that circles forever on a pull of exactly one, and a leak that crowds at a point the room does not contain.

New word: fixed point. A fixed point of a map \(g\) is a spot the map leaves alone: \(g(x^*) = x^*\). Feed it in, get it back. It is where an orbit could settle — and, for a contraction, the only place an orbit ever does. New word: orbit. An orbit is the record a repetition leaves: start anywhere, \(x_0\), and apply the map forever, \(x_1 = g(x_0)\), \(x_2 = g(x_1)\), endlessly. The orbit is this page's endless record, and the whole question is where it crowds — this page's answer: not merely where, but provably there, provably alone, and provably by hit \(n\). New word: contraction. A map is a contraction on a room if it pulls any two of the room's points toward each other by at least a fixed factor: \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le c\,|x - y|\) for one constant \(c < 1\), every pair, no exceptions. The pull is the hypothesis that turns repetition into certainty; §5 shows, one exhibit per clause, what each part of it is worth.

1 · Before we begin

The tier-2 ladder so far, one breath: P18 lifted a stuck problem into a room with one more dimension and descended the answer, P19 proved things exist by showing a random pick finds them, P20 certified identities by testing at enough points instead of expanding, and P21 caught the crowd an endless record forms in a bounded room. Four moves, four infinities tamed by finite work — and notice what P21's machine deliberately never promised: which spot, whether only one, and what it costs to get close. This page buys exactly those three promises, and pays for all three with one hypothesis. The ladder arrives here carrying three tools this page leans on hard. P21, because an orbit is an endless record of hits in a room and the whole apparatus applies — this page is P21's engine with a turbocharger bolted on, and every part of §3's certificate is a P21 part wearing a new label. P15, the prerequisite, because its ε-trap is where digits get certified, and because P15's own fourth section ran this page's first example as a contrast — errors 0.414, 0.0858, 0.00245, \(2.1 \times 10^{-6}\), \(1.6 \times 10^{-12}\), doubling per step against bisection's steady 0.301 — with the receipt deferred to exactly this page. And P03, because the pull's fine print is where all the danger lives, and fine print is attacked, not skimmed: the §5 exhibits are hostile maps, and each one kills a version of the move that forgot to check a clause.

Here is the tension this page resolves. Repetition is the oldest computing machine there is — average, again, again — and it is the one machine on this ladder that is genuinely endless: the recipe never halts, never checks a stopping rule, certifies nothing on its own. A card of hits can suggest settling; nothing in the card can prove it, and P02's discipline — stop at the data — is precisely the discipline a forever-rule cannot follow. What would a guarantee even look like? Three sentences: the hits settle somewhere (existence); they settle at one spot only (uniqueness); and the distance from hit \(n\) to the settled spot is at most \(c^n\) times the starting error (price). The good news: all three follow from one hypothesis — the pull, \(c < 1\), checked once — by arguments this short and this old: nested rooms, one line of algebra, a geometric series. The bad news, paid up front: the pull is a license with three separate clauses — the map returns into the room, the constant is strictly below one, the room is closed — and each clause has its own wreckage in §5, run in public, not asserted. And one honest footnote the whole book keeps having to write: the receipt prices the floor, not the penthouse. Runs typically beat their certificates; §4 measures the gap three times; the lesson is P20's — read what the receipt licenses, never more.

3 · Name the move

P22 · Iterate to a fixed point — repeat a contraction; it can only settle at the answer

When a map returns into its room — \(g\) sends every point of a closed room back into that same room — and pulls, drawing any two of the room's points together by a fixed factor: \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le c\,|x - y|\) for one constant \(c < 1\), a contraction — then repeating \(x_{n+1} = g(x_n)\) from any start in the room settles, settles in exactly one place, and is priced: the fixed point \(x^*\) with \(g(x^*) = x^*\) exists, is unique, and satisfies \(|x_n - x^*| \le c^n e_0\). The machine is a recipe plus a receipt: repeat forever, and read three one-sentence certificates off one hypothesis, checked once, before the first hit lands. What the machine buys is the answer to every question of the form the rule is fixed and the repetition endless — so where must the hits land, and how fast?

In words: repetition is the cheapest machine ever built, and the pull is the hypothesis that stops it being a gamble. The orbit is an endless record of hits — P21's very object — and the pull arranges the record's own geometry: the gaps between consecutive hits shrink geometrically, so the tail of the orbit is trapped in rooms the orbit builds around its own latest hits, nested, collapsing — P21's machine, run by the record itself, with no halving rule to choose. The move has three working parts. First, name the room: closed, and genuinely self-mapping — the map returns into it, a clause to check and never assume; §5's first exhibit is a map that flings guesses out of every room near its fixed point, and both of its fixed points exist. Second, prove the pull: one constant \(c < 1\), strict, for the whole room; §5's second exhibit prices the strictness exactly — a map whose pull is exactly \(1\), circling a two-cycle forever with its fixed point sitting unvisited between. Third, read the certificates: existence, uniqueness, price — three sentences, each load-bearing, each proved in Box P22.A, each one sentence long.

Box P22.A · The three certificates

The receipt splits into three independent statements, and each one is exactly where it is needed.

Certificate 1 — the catch exists (P21's machine, run by the orbit itself). Consecutive gaps obey the pull: \(|x_{n+1} - x_n| = |g(x_n) - g(x_{n-1})| \le c\,|x_n - x_{n-1}|\), so gap \(n\) is at most \(c^n\) times gap \(0\). Stack the pull along the tail: every hit after hit \(n\) stays within \(c^n d_0/(1 - c)\) of hit \(n\), where \(d_0 = |x_1 - x_0|\) — the tail's total future wandering, a geometric series, priced in advance. So the orbit builds its own nested rooms: the interval of radius \(c^n d_0/(1 - c)\) around hit \(n\) contains every later hit, the next room sits inside the last, and the widths shrink to zero. P21's Half 2 catches the chain: exactly one point \(x^*\) survives, the room is closed so \(x^*\) is in it, and the pull doubles as the continuity clause — \(|g(x^*) - x_{n+1}| \le c\,|x^* - x_n| \to 0\), so \(g(x^*) = x^*\). A contraction needs no separate continuity assumption: the pull is so strong it is continuous for free.

Certificate 2 — the catch is unique (the pull's arithmetic). Suppose two fixed points, \(p\) and \(q\). Then \(|p - q| = |g(p) - g(q)| \le c\,|p - q|\), so \((1 - c)\,|p - q| \le 0\), and since \(c < 1\) strictly, \(p = q\). The whole argument is one line; the whole weight is the strictness — at \(c = 1\) it degenerates to \(0 \le 0\) and certifies nothing (§5, Exhibit B).

Certificate 3 — the catch is priced (the geometric price). \(e_n = |x_n - x^*| = |g(x_{n-1}) - g(x^*)| \le c\,e_{n-1} \le \cdots \le c^n e_0\): after \(n\) hits, the error is at most \(c^n\) times the start's, and the guaranteed digit rate is \(\log_{10}(1/c)\) per hit. The price is a floor on the worst start in the room, not a forecast of the run — §4 measures three runs against their receipts, and all three beat them; the bonus comes from the flattening of the map at its own fixed point, and the certificate stays honest precisely because it never promises the bonus.

Neither certificate stands alone: existence without uniqueness is P21's honest plurality, uniqueness without existence is a vacant promise, and both without the price is a machine sold without a manual. The receipt is the triple, and the triple is why the move feels like closing a hand: the repetition runs, and something is forced to be in the fist.

Box P22.B · Where it shows up, and the three-key signature

Appearances. Square roots by averaging — Heron's recipe, §4's first run, old enough to be Babylonian. Newton's method near a simple root — the tangent line's map, a contraction once the room is small enough, §4's second run. The cosine's fixed point — Dottie, §4's third run, the slow honest one. Picard's successive approximations — orbits of an integral map that prove differential equations have solutions, §7. Banach's fixed point theorem — the whole receipt promoted to abstract rooms, §7. And at the research frontier, P57's Nash–Moser fast Newton, built directly on this page. One shape under all of them: a rule repeated, a room respected, a pull proved.

When to reach for it — three keys. First, the map genuinely returns into the room: self-mapping is a clause to verify, and §5's repeller is a map that passes the eye and fails the clause. Second, the pull is strict: one \(c < 1\) for the whole room — and if the best \(c\) you can prove is \(1\), you have proved nothing: Exhibit B's map pulls by exactly \(1\) and settles nowhere. Third, the room is closed: the catch must be inside the walls when the trap closes — Exhibit C's leak crowds at a point the room does not contain. If all three keys turn, the receipt reads: exists, unique, priced. No other move on the ladder sells all three at once.

4 · The move at work

Example 1 · The craftsman's recipe — \(\sqrt{2}\) by averaging, certified

Moves used: P15 (the ε-trap: digits certified by walls, the currency of every error below), P21 (the nested rooms: the orbit's own gaps run the machine).

Name the room. Every sensible guess for \(\sqrt{2}\) lives in \([1, 2]\), and even a silly start walks in: one averaging step lands at or above \(\sqrt{2}\) wherever the start was positive, by the floor below. The map returns into the room, twice over. The floor: by AM–GM, \(g(x) = (x + 2/x)/2 \ge \sqrt{x \cdot (2/x)} = \sqrt{2} \ge 1\) — averaging never undershoots. The ceiling: \(g(x) - 3/2 = (x-1)(x-2)/(2x) \le 0\) on \([1,2]\), so \(g(x) \le 3/2\). Together: \(g([1,2]) \subseteq [\sqrt{2},\, 1.5]\), a strictly smaller room inside the first — the self-mapping clause, checked, not assumed.

Now the pull, and it is two lines of class-12 algebra. For any two guesses \(x, y\) in the room, \[ g(x) - g(y) \;=\; \frac{x - y}{2} + \frac{2/y - 2/x}{2} \;=\; (x - y)\left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{xy}\right). \] On the room, \(xy \ge 1\), so \(0 \le 1/(xy) \le 1\) and the bracket sits between \(-1/2\) and \(1/2\): \(|g(x) - g(y)| \le \tfrac{1}{2}|x - y|\). The averaging map is a contraction with \(c = 1/2\), and Box P22.A's whole receipt applies: the card settles, at one spot, priced. The price, quoted in advance: \(e_n \le (1/2)^n e_0\) with \(e_0 = 0.4142135624\) — a guaranteed \(\log_{10} 2 = 0.301\) digits per hit, bisection's own rate, P15's currency. Compute \(g(x) - \sqrt{2}\) directly: putting the two terms over the common denominator \(2x\) gives \[ g(x) - \sqrt{2} \;=\; \frac{x^2 - 2\sqrt{2}\,x + 2}{2x} \;=\; \frac{(x - \sqrt{2})^2}{2x}. \]

Three consequences, each read straight off the identity. Impostors are impossible: a settle point must satisfy \(g(x^*) = x^*\), and for this map that equation is \((x^*)^2 = 2\) — the positive solution is \(\sqrt{2}\) itself, so the catch is the true diagonal, not an impostor the recipe happens to like. One-sidedness: the identity is a square over a positive denominator, so every hit after the first overshoots — the card's errors are all overshoots from hit 1 on. The squaring: \(e_{n+1} = e_n^2/(2x_n)\) — errors square, then halve — so correct decimals go \(1, 2, 5, 11, 24\), doubling per hit. The receipt priced the whole room's worst pair; the run rides the flattening at the answer, where the pull factor is \(g'(\sqrt{2}) = 0\) — flat, the map's one tangent-horizontal spot, sitting exactly at the catch. That is where the doubling comes from: a floor you can quote before running is worth more than a sharp guess you cannot, and the doubling is the map paying its own bonus.

Example 2 · Newton on the plastic constant — the four-step race

Moves used: P07 (the auxiliary object: Newton's map, built to spec), P21 (the forty-level trap this run races).

P21's third example trapped the plastic constant — \(\rho \approx 1.3247179572\), the real root of \(x^3 = x + 1\) — by bisection: forty halvings for walls \(9.1 \times 10^{-13}\) apart, one bit per level, honest and slow. Build (P07) the auxiliary object that does better: Newton's map, which replaces the curve by its tangent line and solves the tangent's equation instead, \[ N(x) \;=\; x - \frac{f(x)}{f'(x)} \;=\; x - \frac{x^3 - x - 1}{3x^2 - 1}. \] The fixed points of \(N\) are exactly the roots of \(f\) — \(N(x^*) = x^*\) exactly when \(f(x^*) = 0\) — so the recipe "apply \(N\) forever" has \(\rho\) as its natural catch. The question is the room. On the big room \([1,2]\) no certificate can be issued at all: \(N'(1) = f(1)f''(1)/f'(1)^2 = (-1)(6)/4 = -1.5\), the pull clause fails outright — a map that pushes this hard near \(1\) is a gamble, not a theorem. Shrink the room to \([1.25,\, 1.45]\) and the picture turns: the lab's scan (2001 points) certifies \(|N'| \le 0.1849\) there, and then the pull proves the self-map for free: for any \(x\) in the room, \(|N(x) - \rho| \le 0.1849\,|x - \rho| \le 0.1849 \times 0.1252820 = 0.0232\), so the image sits inside \([\,1.3015,\, 1.3479\,]\), well within the walls. (The exact image is smaller still — \(N\) dips to its minimum at the root itself, \(N(\rho) = \rho\), and peaks at \(N(1.45) = 1.3372114932\), with \(N(1.25) = 1.3305084746\) an interior value — but the pull's cruder bound is the one the receipt needs.) Certificate issued: \(c = 0.1849\), guaranteed \(0.733\) digits per hit, from every start in the room.

The run — started at \(x_0 = 1\), one step outside the room, honestly labelled: the first hit is unguarded, a lucky guess with a famous name; from hit 2 on the orbit is inside \([1.25, 1.45]\) and the certificate governs every step:

The prices, out loud. Newton's map is not a contraction on \([1,2]\) and no argument can make it one — the room had to shrink to where the pull drops below \(1\); "Newton's method always converges near a root" is folklore, and the room check is what turns it into this page's theorem. The two-step ladder — unguarded hits until the orbit wanders into a certified room — is honest, priced as such, and it recurs in §5's first exhibit in extreme form. And the 4-versus-40 race is real but debt-financed: bisection needs no pull, no self-map, no room — it buys its 0.301 digits per step with assumptions this page's receipt would never sign. Different machines, different clauses, different prices: the whole ladder's economics in one race.

Example 3 · Dottie — the cosine's fixed point, priced honestly

Moves used: P02 (the card of hits, read for its rate), P15 (the price of digits, quoted in digits per step).

The slowest honest machine on the page. Take the map \(g(x) = \cos x\) on the room \([0, 1]\) — a room of radians, and the only one needed: outside it the cosine's answers live too. The self-map is a monotonicity check: \(\cos' = -\sin \le 0\) on \([0,1]\), so the cosine decreases there, and the image is exactly \([\cos 1, \cos 0] = [0.5403023,\, 1]\) — inside the walls, with room to spare. The pull is the class-12 identity \(\cos x - \cos y = -2\sin\big((x+y)/2\big)\sin\big((x-y)/2\big)\) with two honest bounds: \(|\sin t| \le |t|\) for the second factor, and — since \((x+y)/2\) lies in \([0,1]\), where the sine is increasing — \(\sin\big((x+y)/2\big) \le \sin 1\) for the first. Multiply: \(|\cos x - \cos y| \le \sin(1)\,|x - y|\). The certificate: \(c = \sin 1 = 0.8414710\) — under one, barely — so a unique fixed point exists in \([0,1]\), priced at \(\log_{10}(1/0.8414710) = 0.075\) digits per hit, guaranteed. It is called \(d = 0.7390851332\ldots\), the cosine's fixed point, and the run from \(x_0 = 0\):

The prices, out loud. Dottie costs what it costs: six decimals for 35 hits, twenty-four for about 140 — the certificate honestly quotes 320 — and no flattening identity bails it out, because the pull at the catch is \(0.67\), not \(0\). A machine this slow is worth having anyway, and here is why it earns its place on the page: of the three runs, it is the only one whose receipt is tight. Example 1's receipt undersells by \(10^{22}\), Example 2's by a squaring constant — lovely, but unpriced luck of the flattening — while Dottie's receipt is within a factor of \(2.3\) of the truth, because its room is small enough that the worst point and the flat spot are nearly the same place. The smaller the room, the sharper the receipt; that is the design lesson, and it is why the grown-up theorem is stated the way §7 states it.

5 · When it fails — three exhibits of fine print

The certificate of Box P22.A is the whole license, and the license has three clauses: the map returns into the room, the pull is strictly below one, and the room is closed. Here is the wreckage when a clause fails, one exhibit per clause, each one run, not asserted.

Exhibit A · The repeller — the map that does not return. Take \(g(x) = x^2\), the squaring map, and start near its fixed point \(1\). The orbit from \(1.01\): \(1.0201,\, 1.0406,\, 1.0829,\, 1.1726,\, 1.3749,\, 1.8905,\, 3.5738,\, 12.77,\, 163,\, 26{,}610\) — past any wall the reader cares to name, and the pattern is a proof: each hit squares, and squaring a number above \(1\) is multiplication by a factor above \(1\) that only grows. The orbit from \(0.99\), one step on the other side of the same fixed point: \(0.9801,\, 0.9606,\, 0.9227,\, 0.8515,\, 0.7250,\, 0.5256,\, 0.2763,\, 0.0763,\, 0.0058,\, 0.00003\) — walking down to \(0\), settling there. Two orbits, two fates, one map — and both fixed points, \(0\) and \(1\), exist. What fails near \(1\) is the license, both clauses at once: no room containing \(1\) in its interior is self-mapping (\(g(1 + \varepsilon) = 1 + 2\varepsilon + \varepsilon^2\) has already left any such room), and the pull factor there is \(g'(1) = 2\) — pushing, not pulling. The repair, when the start is on the attracting side, is the orbit's own: from \(0.99\), the walk descends into ever-flatter country, and from hit 7 on (\(0.2763\) and below) a room like \([0, 0.28]\) carries a pull of \(0.56 < 1\) and the certificate could finally be issued — Example 2's two-step ladder in extreme form. The lesson, priced: fixed points can exist in abundance while the iteration reaches none of them; existence is cheap, the certificate is the whole purchase.

Exhibit B · The two-cycle — the pull of exactly one. Take \(g(x) = 1 - x\) on the closed room \([0,1]\). Self-mapping: perfect — the map trades the room with itself. Closed room: yes. The pull: \(|g(x) - g(y)| = |x - y|\) exactly, for every pair. The best \(c\) is \(1\), and \(1\) is not strictly below \(1\): the clause fails by a hair, and the hair is load-bearing. Run the recipe from \(0.3\): the orbit is \(0.7, 0.3, 0.7, 0.3, \ldots\) — a two-cycle, forever, no settling, no divergence, the most well-behaved failure in the book. Audit the certificate, clause by clause, and watch each one die: the uniqueness sum \((1 - c)|p - q| \le 0\) degenerates to \(0 \le 0\), certifying nothing; the nested rooms of Certificate 1 have widths \(c^n d_0/(1-c)\) — at \(c = 1\) the tail bound is division by zero, the rooms never shrink, and P21's machine has nothing to catch with; the price \(c^n e_0\) never drops at all. And the fixed point exists, of course — \(g(1/2) = 1/2\) — sitting exactly between the two cycle points, unvisited, unvisitable by the recipe. The repair, here, is instant: average each hit with its predecessor, \(x_{n+1} = (x_n + g(x_n))/2\), and the damped map collapses to the constant \(1/2\) — the fixed point, in one step. In general the boundary \(c = 1\) is its own regime — the grown-up word is nonexpansive — and the blanket receipt is gone; the honest statement is that each map on the boundary needs its own repair, priced separately.

Exhibit C · The leak — the room that is not closed. The cleanest pull on the page, worn by the wrong room. Take \(g(x) = x/2\) on the open room \((0, 1]\) — the strip without its left wall. Contraction: \(|g(x) - g(y)| = |x - y|/2\), so \(c = 1/2\), exactly the Babylonian's pull. Self-mapping: \(x/2 \in (0, 0.5]\) for every \(x \in (0,1]\) — inside. Two clauses turned, the pull proven, the receipt one clause short — and this page's certificate, unlike P21's, can tell you exactly where the catch would be: the equation \(g(x^*) = x^*\) has the single solution \(x^* = 0\), which the room does not contain. Run it anyway, from \(0.9\): \(0.45,\, 0.225,\, 0.1125,\, 0.05625,\, 0.028125,\, 0.014063,\, 0.007031,\, 0.003516, \ldots\) — the hits halve forever, crowding toward \(0\), and for once the receipt is exactly honest: the error is the hit (each hit's distance to the missing catch is the hit itself), the price \(c^n e_0\) is met, not beaten — a machine running perfectly toward a point that is not there. This is P21's Exhibit A wearing this page's clothes: the same leak, the same crowd at the removed point, the same repair — close the room, admit the boundary, and the certificate stamps the catch \(x^* = 0\) at once. And the same price, out loud: the repaired claim is about the repaired room \([0,1]\), not the original one; whoever needed the answer inside \((0,1]\) has learned that no answer lives there — sometimes the leak is the finding.

Scope and price of P22

What the move gives. Given a self-mapping closed room and one proven pull \(c < 1\): convergence from every start in the room — robustness is part of the receipt, not a luck — to the unique fixed point, with the error priced in advance. One hypothesis, checked once, buys three theorems' worth; no other move on the ladder sells existence, uniqueness, and a price in one certificate.

What it does not give, honestly. First, the price is a floor, and runs typically beat it — the flattening bonus of Example 1, the squaring constant of Example 2, the tail rate of Example 3; measure the run if a forecast is wanted, but trust the receipt for the guarantee. Second, the three clauses are separate licenses with separate wreckage (§5, Exhibits A, B, C), and the self-mapping clause is the one most often assumed and least often checked — Example 2's map self-maps its big room and still carries no certificate there, because a room must also be small enough for the pull. Third, the catch comes with no closed form: the iteration is the formula — \(\sqrt{2}\)'s digits are computed by the recipe and certified by P15's walls, never delivered by algebra. Fourth, the closed-room clause is the class-12 costume of completeness — the same no-holes coin P21 spent, worn here by the geometric tail bound — and the grown-up theorem of §7 is stated in complete metric spaces; a treatment that hides the costume is selling a machine with an unpaid bill. Fifth, Newton's method without the room check is a heuristic with a famous name — Example 2's four-step win was won inside a certified room, and the certificate is what makes the recipe a theorem. Sixth, and the honest boundary of the whole page: some of the deepest results of the century were built by iterating maps that are not contractions on any obvious room, and the repair of that failure is a research-grade pattern in its own right — P57, signposted here, built there.

6 · What next

The doors this page opens

The nearest door: P23 · Interpolate and continue. Every orbit this page ran began as a table — the Babylonian card is a table of fractions — and the answer arrived as the table's continuation, the entry the table never reaches. P23 makes that move deliberate: hold on to a formula that works on the integers, and carry it across, to new values and new ground. This page's certificates are the receipt that one particular continuation — repeat forever — deserves its answer; P23's pattern is the general shop where continuations are sold, and its honesty checks are this page's, one tier up: continue, then audit exactly what the continuation claims and no more.

The middle door: P25 · Check every case by machine. This page's certificates were partly machine-checked, honestly: the pull of Example 2 is a 2001-point scan, the cosine's pull a 101-by-101 grid, the level-6 Pell prediction 25-digit whole-number arithmetic done in chunks. Each is a finite check standing in for a claim about a whole interval — a machine case, with its resolution stated. P25 promotes that move to a pattern with its own honesty rules: when the possibilities are finite, let the computer count them all — and say exactly what the count licenses. This page's labs are P25's method, in miniature, arriving one door early.

The far door: P57 · Nash–Moser fast Newton. Newton's map is a contraction only near its root, in rooms made small enough by hand — Example 2's \([1.25, 1.45]\), chosen and checked. P57 faces problems where no single room works: the map loses derivatives as the iteration proceeds, and the recipe must re-smooth its own steps as it goes — Newton's method where the ground keeps changing under the tangent. The whole receipt of this page — room, pull, price, and the two-step honesty when the orbit wanders between rooms — is P57's foundation, and the far door is this page's engine rebuilt to run on terrain this page would refuse.

The default habit

When an answer is defined as the place a repetition settles — or can be rephrased as one, which is more claims than it sounds: a huge share of "solve the equation" problems rewrite as \(x = g(x)\) and iterate — reach for this move first. Rewrite the claim as a map, name a room the map returns into, prove the pull (one constant \(c < 1\) for the whole room — shrink the room until it holds, and say so), then read the three certificates and run. Two audits, every time: check the self-mapping clause (the one silently assumed most often), and measure the run against the price (the certificate is the guarantee; the run is the forecast; when they disagree, the certificate still wins the argument, the run wins the schedule). If the pull fails, repair first — shrink the room, as Newton's does, or damp the step, as the two-cycle's averaging does — and price the repair out loud: the repaired claim is about the repaired room. The discipline is the whole difference between a recipe that happens to work and a machine that must.

7 · Where it came from

The recipe is the oldest thing on this page — old enough to have a plaque — and the receipt is one of the youngest guarantees in this book. The story runs from Babylon to Lwów in five hops, and every date on it was verified before printing.

YBC 7289, c. 1800–1600 BC. A Babylonian clay tablet, now in the Yale collection, carries a square with its diagonal and the diagonal's length in base sixty: \(1;24,51,10\) — that is, \(1 + 24/60 + 51/3600 + 10/216000 = 1.4142129630\), off from the true \(\sqrt{2} = 1.4142135624\) by \(6 \times 10^{-7}\), within one part in two million: the greatest computational accuracy known from the ancient world. All four sexagesimal figures are exact — the true value continues \(1;24,51,10,07,\ldots\), and the tablet stopped one figure short of perfection. The tablet records the value, not the method; that some Babylonian procedure could hit six-decimal accuracy is certain, and the averaging recipe is a scholarly reconstruction — honestly labelled as inference here, because the receipt this page teaches did not exist for another three and a half thousand years.

Heron of Alexandria, 1st century AD. In the Metrica, Heron states the averaging recipe for general square roots — half the sum of the guess and the number divided by it — the move of §4's first example in its first full dress. Two thousand years in deployment, zero years in proof, at that point: the recipe was certified craftsmanship long before it was a theorem.

Newton 1669, Raphson 1690, Simpson 1740. Newton's De analysi (written 1669, printed 1711) shows the method for polynomials; Joseph Raphson's Analysis aequationum universalis (1690) gives the reusable iterative form, each correction drawn from the original equation; Thomas Simpson (1740) states it in calculus terms and generalizes to systems. The lineage point this page owns: near a simple root, Newton's map is a contraction — the certificate of §4's second example was in daily use for two centuries before anyone could write it down.

Picard, 1890. Émile Picard's Mémoire sur la théorie des équations aux dérivées partielles et la méthode des approximations successives proved that differential equations have solutions by iterating an integral map: start with any candidate, feed it through the equation, and the orbit settles on a solution — existence by convergence, the receipt of Box P22.A promoted from arithmetic to analysis. The orbit under the integral map is called Picard iteration to this day, and it is the standard existence proof taught for exactly the equation P26 will model.

Banach, 1922. Stefan Banach's Sur les opérations dans les ensembles abstraits et leur application aux équations intégrales (Fundamenta Mathematicae, vol. 3) states the abstract theorem: a contraction on a complete metric space has a unique fixed point, with the error priced — everything this page proved in school arithmetic, worn by its general home, complete with the closed-room clause wearing its grown-up name. The three certificates readers checked in decimals are Banach's, in any currency; the receipt is the same receipt, and the pull is the same pull.

Dottie, disclaimed. The cosine's fixed point is folklore's contribution to this page: the name "Dottie number" is a modern classroom coinage with no historical Dottie behind it, and §7, which gives dates, gives this one a disclaimer instead — the number itself is as old as the cosine, its twenty-four decimals were never a secret, and the name is a joke that stuck. Folklore gets the citation because even a joke deserves an honest provenance.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 · The first hits, by hand ●○○
Back to §2's card. (a) Run the recipe on fractions from \(x_0 = 1\), no decimals allowed: show the first four hits are exactly \(1/1\), \(3/2\), \(17/12\), \(577/408\). (b) Prove the error identity \(g(x) - \sqrt{2} = (x - \sqrt{2})^2/(2x)\), and deduce both consequences printed in §4: every hit after the first overshoots, and \(e_{n+1} = e_n^2/(2x_n)\).

Hint

For (a), averaging a fraction: \((a/b + 2b/a)/2 = (a^2 + 2b^2)/(2ab)\) — the next hit's numerator and denominator, in whole numbers. For (b), put both terms of \(g(x) - \sqrt{2}\) over the common denominator \(2x\), and recognize the numerator.

Worked

(a) \(1/1 \mapsto (1 + 2)/2 = 3/2 \mapsto (3/2 + 4/3)/2 = (9 + 8)/12 = 17/12 \mapsto (17/12 + 24/17)/2 = (289 + 288)/204 = 577/408\) — the Pell chain of §4's table, level by level. (b) \(g(x) - \sqrt{2} = (x + 2/x)/2 - \sqrt{2} = (x^2 - 2\sqrt{2}\,x + 2)/(2x) = (x - \sqrt{2})^2/(2x)\). The numerator is a square and the denominator positive, so the difference is positive except at \(x = \sqrt{2}\): overshoot, one-sided, from hit 1 on. Substituting \(x = x_n\): \(e_{n+1} = |g(x_n) - \sqrt{2}| = (x_n - \sqrt{2})^2/(2x_n) = e_n^2/(2x_n)\). Check it on the card at \(x = 3/2\): \((0.0857864376)^2/3 = 0.0024531\) — hit 2's error, exact.

Exercise 2 · The Dottie audit ●●○
(a) Prove the pull: for \(x, y\) in \([0,1]\), \(|\cos x - \cos y| \le \sin(1)\,|x - y|\). (b) Prove the self-map: \(\cos([0,1]) = [0.5403023, 1] \subseteq [0,1]\). (c) The audit: show the run from \(x_0 = 0\) delivers error below \(5 \times 10^{-7}\) by hit 35, while the certificate only promised it by hit 83 — verify both counts, with a calculator for the second. (d) The tail rate: the ratios of successive errors tend to \(\sin(0.7390851) = 0.6736120\), worth 0.1716 digits per hit, against the certificate's 0.075 — explain where each number comes from.

Hint

For (a), the identity \(\cos x - \cos y = -2\sin\big((x+y)/2\big)\sin\big((x-y)/2\big)\), the bound \(|\sin t| \le |t|\), and the fact that \((x+y)/2 \in [0,1]\) where the sine is increasing. For (b), monotonicity of the cosine on \([0,1]\). For (c), the certificate's count is the smallest \(n\) with \(0.8414710^n \times 0.7390851 < 5 \times 10^{-7}\); the run's count is a loop with a cosine key. For (d), compare the pull factor at the worst point of the room with the pull factor at the catch.

Worked

(a) \(|\cos x - \cos y| = 2|\sin((x+y)/2)|\,|\sin((x-y)/2)| \le 2 \cdot \sin(1) \cdot |x - y|/2 = \sin(1)\,|x - y|\), using \(|\sin t| \le |t|\) on the second factor and \(\sin((x+y)/2) \le \sin 1\) on the first (both arguments in \([0,1]\), sine increasing). (b) \(\cos' = -\sin \le 0\) on \([0,1]\), so the cosine decreases: the image is \([\cos 1, \cos 0] = [0.5403023, 1]\), inside the room. (c) Run: iterate \(\cos\) from \(0\) and watch \(|x_n - 0.7390851332|\) — it crosses below \(5 \times 10^{-7}\) at \(n = 35\) (the lab recounts it). Certificate: \(0.8414710^n \times 0.7390851\) falls below \(5 \times 10^{-7}\) first at \(n = 83\). (d) The certificate prices the worst point of the room, \(x = 1\), where the pull factor is \(|\cos' (1)| = \sin 1 = 0.8414710\); the orbit's tail lives near the catch \(d\), where the pull factor is \(|\cos'(d)| = \sin d = 0.6736120\). Digits per hit: \(\log_{10}(1/0.6736120) = 0.1716\) actual, \(\log_{10}(1/0.8414710) = 0.075\) guaranteed — an undersell factor of \(2.3\), the room's worst point versus the orbit's favourite.

Exercise 3 · Newton's room, bought and paid for ●●●
(a) Compute \(N(1.25)\) and \(N(1.45)\) by hand to four decimals, and verify the printed values 1.3305 and 1.3372. Then explain why endpoint checks alone do not certify the image: where does \(N\) take its minimum on the room, and what is the image exactly? (b) Compute \(N'(1)\) and explain why no certificate can be issued on \([1,2]\), however nicely this particular orbit behaves. (c) The race: P21's bisection needed forty halvings for walls \(9.1 \times 10^{-13}\) apart; count the Newton hits from \(1.5\) needed to beat that, and say exactly which hits ran inside the certified room \([1.25, 1.45]\).

Hint

For (a), \(N(x) = x - f(x)/f'(x)\) with \(f(x) = x^3 - x - 1\): four-digit arithmetic suffices. For the minimum, ask where \(N' = f f''/f'^2\) vanishes. For (b), same formula at \(x = 1\). For (c), the errors of §4's table: which is the first below \(9.1 \times 10^{-13}\)?

Worked

(a) \(f(1.25) = -0.296875\), \(f'(1.25) = 3.6875\): \(N(1.25) = 1.25 + 0.296875/3.6875 = 1.3305\). \(f(1.45) = 0.598625\), \(f'(1.45) = 5.3075\): \(N(1.45) = 1.45 - 0.1128 = 1.3372\). But \(N'\) vanishes exactly at the root \(\rho\): below \(\rho\) the map dips, and \(N(\rho) = \rho\) — the fixed point itself is the image's floor. The image is \([\rho, 1.3372114932]\), not \([N(1.45), N(1.25)]\): endpoint checks without the shape check would have mis-stated it, and §4's pull bound — \(|N(x) - \rho| \le 0.1849 \times 0.1252820 = 0.0232\), image inside \([1.3015, 1.3479]\) — is the certificate's honest route, cruder but self-proving. (b) \(N'(1) = f(1)f''(1)/f'(1)^2 = (-1)(6)/4 = -1.5\): on \([1,2]\) the best \(c\) is at least \(1.5\), the pull clause fails outright, and no shrinking of the argument fixes it — only shrinking the room does. (c) From \(1.5\): the hits \(1.3478260870\) (error \(2.3 \times 10^{-2}\)), \(1.3252003990\) (\(4.8 \times 10^{-4}\)), \(1.3247181740\) (\(2.2 \times 10^{-7}\)), \(1.3247179572\) (\(4.4 \times 10^{-14} < 9.1 \times 10^{-13}\)) — four steps, the fourth the first inside P21's walls. The first hit lands in the room \([1.25, 1.45]\) unguarded (\(1.5\) was outside it); from hit \(1.3478260870\) on, every hit ran inside the certified room. Four against forty.

Exercise 4 · The diagnosis ◆
A student iterates \(g(x) = x^2 - 1\) from \(0.5\). The card: \(0.500000, -0.750000, -0.437500, -0.808594, -0.346176, -0.880162, -0.225315, -0.949233, -0.098956, -0.990208, -0.019489, -0.999620\) — the hits tightening around \(0\) and \(-1\), alternating. Her conclusion: "The iteration does not converge, so this map has no fixed point." Diagnose the error precisely: what does non-convergence of one orbit certify about a map's fixed points? What are this map's fixed points, why does the iteration avoid them, and what is it converging to instead? Where has the book seen this type error before?

Hint

Solve \(x^2 - 1 = x\). Then compute the pull factor \(g'(x) = 2x\) at each solution. Then read the card again and ask what pairs of values the hits are settling toward — and check the map on them.

Worked

Fixed points: \(x^2 - x - 1 = 0\), so \(x = (1 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\): \(\varphi \approx 1.618034\) and \(-1/\varphi \approx -0.618034\). Both exist — non-convergence of one orbit certifies nothing about existence, a type error: the recipe's failure promoted to the map's. The pull factors: \(|g'(\varphi)| = 3.236\) and \(|g'(-0.618034)| = 1.236\), both above \(1\) — both fixed points repel, and no certificate could be issued near either; the orbit cannot settle there, and the card's oscillation is the honest witness. What it converges to instead: the two-cycle \(0 \leftrightarrow -1\), since \(g(0) = -1\) and \(g(-1) = 0\), and the convergence is real — the cycle's multiplier is \(g'(0) \cdot g'(-1) = 0 \cdot (-2) = 0\), superattracting, the flattest spot on the map (the two-sided analogue of §4's flattening at \(\sqrt{2}\), one level down: a cycle the iteration locks onto instead of a point). She watched the orbit settle beautifully and concluded nothing settles. The confusion is P20's census lesson and P21's uniqueness lesson in one: a run certifies what it ran on — here, a two-cycle — and existence claims are settled by solving equations, not by watching orbits decline. The honest repair of her sentence: "the map has two fixed points, both repelling; my orbit converged to a superattracting two-cycle; the pull explains all three facts."

Exercise 5 · The Pell chain, and a prediction about level 6 ◐
(a) Prove the residual identity: if a hit is the fraction \(a/b\), the next hit is \((a^2 + 2b^2)/(2ab)\), and its residual satisfies \((a')^2 - 2(b')^2 = (a^2 - 2b^2)^2\). (b) The level-0 hit is \(1/1\), with residual \(-1\). Deduce every later level's residual is \(+1\), and verify levels 1 through 5. (c) For a hit \(a/b\) with residual \(1\), the error is exactly \(e = 1/\big(b^2 (a/b + \sqrt{2})\big)\). Compute hit 5's error (\(b = 627013566048\)) and confirm the card's \(9 \times 10^{-25}\); then predict level 6 — its residual, the order of its error, and its digit count — and say how the prediction could be checked without floating point.

Hint

For (a), expand \((a^2 + 2b^2)^2 - 2(2ab)^2\) and recognize the result. For (b), a square is positive. For (c), clear denominators in \((a/b)^2 - 2 = (a^2 - 2b^2)/b^2\), then divide by \(a/b + \sqrt{2}\); for the prediction, Exercise 1's identity \(e_{n+1} = e_n^2/(2x_n)\) is faster than the exact formula.

Worked

(a) \((a')^2 - 2(b')^2 = (a^2 + 2b^2)^2 - 2(2ab)^2 = a^4 + 4a^2b^2 + 4b^4 - 8a^2b^2 = (a^2 - 2b^2)^2\). (b) Levels 1–5: \(3/2\), \(17/12\), \(577/408\), \(665857/470832\), \(886731088897/627013566048\), residuals \(+1\) at every level — each the square of the last, and \((-1)^2 = +1\) squares to \(+1\) forever. (c) \(e_5 = 1/\big(627013566048^2 \cdot (886731088897/627013566048 + \sqrt{2})\big) \approx 1/(3.931 \times 10^{23} \times 2.8284) \approx 8.99 \times 10^{-25}\): twenty-four decimals, the card's figure. Level 6: residual \(+1\) again (the identity of (a): a residual of \(+1\) squares to \(+1\)); error by Exercise 1's identity: \(e_6 = e_5^2/(2x_5) \approx (8.99 \times 10^{-25})^2/2.8284 \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-49}\) — forty-eight decimals. The check without floating point: whole-number arithmetic in chunks — the lab computes the level-6 pair exactly, \(a_6 = 1572584048032918633353217\), \(b_6 = 1111984844349868137938112\), and verifies \(a_6^2 - 2b_6^2 = 1\), digit by digit, in base ten. The prediction is the certificate's whole point: priced before the run, paid by it.
