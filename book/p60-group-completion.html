The Patterns Atlas · P60 · the ninth move of tier 5

Group completion

Some worlds only know how to add. When the questions turn to differences — net worth, one bundle minus another, what survives cancellation — the honest move is not to answer inside the world but to complete it: build the one group the world's addition was always trying to become. The differences are formal; the invariant they assemble into is universal, and every honest accounting factors through it.

New word: the monoid. A monoid is a set with an addition that associates and a zero that adds nothing — a group minus its inverses. You have lived in one since class 1: the counting numbers \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\) add associatively and have \(0\), but \(3 - 5\) is not a counting number, and the monoid cannot hear the question. Monoids are everywhere once you look: finite sets under disjoint union, isomorphism classes of vector bundles under direct sum, the positive whole numbers under multiplication, the closed subsets of a plane under union. This page is about the move that makes differences lawful in such a world — not by leaving for some other world, but by completing the one you have, in the most economical way there is. P31 taught the method in advance: don't describe the object, describe its job — and this page's construction is that method made into a machine.

1 · Before we begin

Tier 5 keeps one thing and discards another, honestly. P59 kept the count and discarded the arrangement: a family too entangled to list was trapped by the product of its shadows, and the slack between bound and truth measured the interaction the cover could not see. This page refuses even the count. Some collections are never asked "how many" — the questions about them are algebraic from the start: what is this object minus that one? what survives when these two summands cancel? — and the move is to build, once, the group in which such questions have answers, and to prove that nothing about that group was a choice. It is the same strategic retreat as P59's, one step further down the same road: where P59 stopped listing and started bounding, this page stops counting and starts adjoining.

What you need from earlier pages is small and named. From P31: the universal property argument — an object is pinned down by its job, not by its material; to define something, say exactly which maps out of it must exist, and prove that anything with the same job is the same object up to a unique translation. The group this page builds is defined by its job before it is built at all: receive the monoid's additions, answer the monoid's difference-questions, and stay the most economical world that does both. From school: the integers as differences of counting numbers — if you have ever checked \((-2) + (-3) = -5\) by rewriting it as \((0,2) + (0,3) = (0,5)\), you have already run this page's construction by hand. What school left unsaid — and what this page pays for — is that the construction works on any monoid, that it is forced rather than chosen, and that on monoids without cancellation it quietly destroys information, which is a price worth naming out loud.

The new words, all of them, are the page's spine: monoid (a group minus inverses — the on-ramp box above), group completion or Grothendieck group (the built world, named for where it first earned its keep), universal invariant (the completed group's job description: every additive map out of the monoid into any group factors through it), and the witness \(k\) — the quiet hero of the construction, the patch that keeps the equivalence relation honest exactly when cancellation fails. The engine is one construction and one proof of universality, both complete below; the applications reach the research frontier — from the \(K_0\) that gave the subject its name to the stable homotopy groups of spheres, which turn out to be a group completion wearing topology.

2 · Meet the problem

The ledger that will not subtract. A shopkeeper's ledger has two columns: received and owed. Yesterday's page reads 5 received, 3 owed — write it as the pair \((5, 3)\). Today's page reads \((2, 0)\): two received, nothing owed. The accountant's first question: are these pages the same position? The ledger's own arithmetic — the only arithmetic it knows — answers: yes, because \(5 + 0 = 3 + 2\). Both columns are counting numbers; the ledger adds them; nothing else is available. Pages add, column by column: \((5, 3) + (2, 4) = (7, 7)\), a page that balances.

Now the question that breaks the world. A page reads \((2, 5)\): two received, five owed. The shopkeeper asks the natural thing: which single number is this page worth? — the same question that \((5, 3)\) answered with 2, since \((5,3)\) and \((2,0)\) are the same position. For \((2,5)\) the answer must be a counting number \(n\) with the position of \((n, 0)\), that is, \(n + 5 = 0 + 2\). No counting number solves it. The monoid of counting numbers receives the question with silence — and the silence is not a failure of the shopkeeper, it is the boundary of the world. School's remedy, seen now with adult eyes, is not a number but a construction: stop searching the old world, and decree the page \((2,5)\) itself to be a new object — the thing we eventually call \(-3\) — subject to the ledger's only law, sameness: two pages \((a, b)\) and \((c, d)\) are the same position exactly when \(a + d = b + c\). Under that law, \((2,5)\) is the same position as \((1, 4)\), as \((0, 3)\), and as no page with an empty owed-column at all.

Watch the silence turn into answers, each one a line of arithmetic. The position of \((5, 3)\) can be asked for as a single counting number — which \(n\) sits in the same position as \((n, 0)\)? — and the ledger answers \(2\), because \(5 + 0 = 3 + 2\). Ask the same of the page \((2, 5)\) and the ledger goes silent: \(n + 5 = 2 + 0\) has no counting-number solution. The completed world answers anyway, and the answer is the position itself: the class of \((2,5)\), the object school writes \(-3\). Subtraction, in both directions, becomes lawful bookkeeping. The question "what, added to the position of \((3,0)\), gives the position of \((5,0)\)" is answered by \((2,0)\), since \((2,0) + (3,0) = (5,0)\). Its evil twin — "what, added to the position of \((5,0)\), gives \((3,0)\)" — is the question the old world could not hear at all; the completed world answers with the class of \((3,5)\), which is the position of \((0,2)\) because \(3 + 2 = 5 + 0\), and the answer checks: \((3,5) + (5,0) = (8,5)\), and \((8,5)\) is the position of \((3,0)\) since \(8 + 0 = 5 + 3\). No new arithmetic was invented; the only thing added to the world is the world.

The bundles that will not subtract. The ledger is not a toy — the same silence once stopped a research field. Call a vector bundle over the two-point space \(X = \{p, q\}\) the data of a vector space at each point; its isomorphism class is the pair of dimensions \((r_p, r_q)\), and the direct sum operation adds these: \((3, 1) \oplus (1, 2) = (4, 3)\). So the isomorphism classes form a monoid — \(\mathbb{N}^2\), the ledger with two independent columns. In 1957, Grothendieck's work on the Riemann–Roch theorem needed to state formulas about differences of bundles — \([E] - [F]\), an invariant that behaves additively, constructed from one bundle minus another — and the monoid of bundles cannot hear that question, for exactly the ledger's reason: no bundle is the difference of two others in general. The move that let the theorem be stated was the ledger's move, run on this monoid: pairs of bundles, the same equivalence, and the completed world — named \(K_0(X)\), its \(K\) from the German Klasse, "class". In the completed world of the two-point space, the element \([(1,2)] - [(2,0)]\) is a perfectly lawful object — a virtual bundle of dimensions \((-1, 2)\) — although no actual bundle has those dimensions. Nothing was counted; a world was completed; the questions became askable.

Two worlds, one move, and the suspicion that the result was not a choice. The integers from the ledger, \(K_0\) from the bundles — both were built by the same recipe, and both, this page will prove, are forced: there is exactly one job description for "the group that receives a monoid's addition and answers its difference-questions most economically", and any two groups doing that job are the same group up to a unique translation. That is P31's argument, upgraded from a method to a construction. The pattern gets its name in the next section.

3 · The pattern, named

Pattern P60 — Group completion. Three steps, each a construction with a universality proof for its verdict. (1) Name the monoid. Say what adds and what the zero is: counting numbers under addition, pages of a ledger, isomorphism classes of bundles under direct sum, finite sets under disjoint union, positive whole numbers under multiplication. Then ask the only diagnostic question: do the questions you need answered involve differences — formal subtraction, negative coefficients, "what remains when this cancels" — that the monoid cannot hear? (2) Complete. Build the pairs \(M \times M\), read \((a, b)\) as "\(a\) minus \(b\)", and quotient by the ledger's law, patched to survive monoids that do not cancel: \((a, b) \sim (c, d)\) exactly when some witness \(k \in M\) satisfies \(a + d + k = b + c + k\). Classes add columnwise, the zero is \([(0,0)]\), the inverse of \([(a,b)]\) is \([(b,a)]\), and the monoid enters through \(\eta(a) = [(a,0)]\). (3) Factor everything. Call an invariant additive when it is a monoid map from \(M\) into a group — and then it is not yours to design: every additive invariant \(f : M \to G\) factors uniquely through the completion, \(f = \bar{f} \circ \eta\). The completion is the universal invariant: the one all others descend from.

Or, in words: don't hunt for the differences — build the world where differences are native, and force that world to be the most economical one there is. "Most economical" is not praise; it is the content of the universality proof: the completion receives the monoid's addition and answers its difference-questions, and nothing else survives — any group that receives the addition honestly must accept the completion's answers wholesale, uniquely, with no room for invention. The move's strength is what it refuses to promise: if the monoid does not cancel, the completion forgets — the map \(\eta\) can merge distinct objects, and §5's max monoid merges everything — and the completion is then a quotient of hopes rather than a container of facts. What the completion always delivers, honestly, is the algebra of differences: the questions the monoid could not hear, answered in the one world where every additive invariant gets the same answer.

The move applies when three keys line up, and each key names its own jam-mode:

Key 1 — the objects must genuinely add. One associative operation, one zero, and the questions must be difference-questions about it. Jam-mode: two operations at once. A ring carries addition and multiplication, and they interact: adjoining additive inverses turns the counting numbers into the integers, but adjoining multiplicative inverses while respecting addition — the rationals from the integers — is a sibling construction with its own universal property, not this page's machine run twice. The ◆ exercise below walks that diagnosis, and the honest answer is that the two jobs are different.

Key 2 — the relations must be the honest ones. If the questions come from direct sums — "what is this object minus that object" — Box P60.A's relation is the right one. If they come from exact sequences — "what remains after cancellation along a sub-object" — the honest completion quotients further, imposing \([X] = [Y] + [X/Y]\)-style relations, and the two completions agree only in well-behaved worlds (the resolution theorem, named here and proved nowhere on this page). Jam-mode: completing with direct-sum relations when the invariants you care about are only exact-sequence-additive silently hands you the wrong universal object — too big, carrying classes that should have merged.

Key 3 — cancellation decides the price. The natural map \(\eta\) embeds \(M\) into its completion exactly when \(M\) cancels — \(a + k = b + k\) forcing \(a = b\). Without cancellation, \(\eta\) merges, and the merge is information destroyed: measured in §5, not mourned. Jam-mode: the max monoid of §5, where \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) while \(2 \neq 3\), and the completion — as universality demands — is the one-element group.

Box P60.A — the construction, the witness, and the universality proof. The naive version of the ledger's law — declare \((a, b)\) and \((c, d)\) the same when \(a + d = b + c\) — is honest on the counting numbers but a lie on general monoids: it need not even be an equivalence relation. On the max monoid \((\mathbb{N}, \oplus)\), where \(a \oplus b = \max(a, b)\), the naive law holds between \((2,3)\) and \((3,3)\) — \(\max(2, 3) = 3 = \max(3, 3)\) — and between \((3,3)\) and \((3,2)\), but fails between \((2,3)\) and \((3,2)\) — \(\max(2,2) = 2\) against \(\max(3,3) = 3\) — so "same position" would not be transitive, and no quotient could be built. The patch is the witness \(k\): declare \((a, b) \sim (c, d)\) when some \(k \in M\) satisfies \(a + d + k = b + c + k\). On the counting numbers the witness changes nothing (cancellation removes it); on the max monoid it repairs transitivity — \((2,3) \sim (3,2)\) via \(k = 3\), since \(\max(2, 2, 3) = 3 = \max(3, 3, 3)\) — and the repair is honest about its price: the max monoid's completion collapses (§5). The verification that \(\sim\) is an equivalence is two lines: reflexivity and symmetry take \(k = 0\); for transitivity, from \(a + d + k_1 = b + c + k_1\) and \(c + f + k_2 = d + e + k_2\), add the second equation's sides into the first and the common witness \(K = d + k_1 + k_2\) appears, giving \(a + f + K = b + e + K\). Addition of classes is well-defined by the same move: if \((a, b) \sim (a', b')\) with witness \(k\), then adding \(c + d\) to the witness equation shows \((a + c, b + d) \sim (a' + c, b' + d)\).

The classes now form a group: \([(a,b)] + [(c,d)] = [(a+c, b+d)]\) is associative and commutative because \(M\)'s addition is; the zero is \([(0,0)]\); and the inverse of \([(a,b)]\) is \([(b,a)]\), since \((a,b) + (b,a) = (a + b, a + b) \sim (0,0)\) with witness \(0\). The monoid enters by \(\eta(a) = [(a,0)]\), and \(\eta(a + b) = [(a + b, 0)] = [(a,0)] + [(b,0)] = \eta(a) + \eta(b)\): the completion receives the addition. Universality is where the group's own lawfulness pays for the monoid's lawlessness: given any monoid map \(f : M \to G\) into a group, define \(\bar{f}([(a,b)]) = f(a) \cdot f(b)^{-1}\). This is well-defined precisely because the witness can be consumed where cancellation is lawful — from \(a + d + k = b + c + k\), applying \(f\) gives \(f(a) f(d) f(k) = f(b) f(c) f(k)\), and the group cancels \(f(k)\), leaving \(f(a) f(b)^{-1} = f(c) f(d)^{-1}\). It is a homomorphism because \(G\)'s image of a commutative monoid is commutative; it satisfies \(\bar{f} \circ \eta = f\) by definition; and it is the only such map, because any homomorphism \(g\) with \(g \circ \eta = f\) must send \([(a,b)] = [(a,0)] + [(0,b)]\) to \(f(a) \cdot f(b)^{-1}\), which is \(\bar{f}\)'s value, so \(g = \bar{f}\). One existence proof, one uniqueness proof: the completion does its job, and nothing else can be talked into doing it — two groups with this job are the same group up to a unique isomorphism, which is P31's argument, now a construction.

Box P60.B — where the move appears. K-theory: Grothendieck's \(K_0\), born in the 1957 work on Riemann–Roch that Borel and Serre wrote up and published in 1958, with its \(K\) from the German Klasse; for a field \(k\), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\), the dimension invariant factored through; for the ring of integers of a number field, the same construction refines the classical class group. Geometry: the Euler characteristic \(\chi\) is additive over disjoint unions and multiplicative over products — \(\chi(\mathrm{pt}) = 1\), \(\chi(S^1) = 0\), \(\chi(S^2) = 2\), \(\chi(S^2 \times S^2) = 4 = 2 \cdot 2\) — and an additive invariant with a group target factors through the completion of the disjoint-union monoid of finite complexes. Stable homotopy: the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen theorem identifies \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+}\) — the group completion of the monoid built from the symmetric groups' classifying spaces — with \(\Omega^\infty S^\infty\), so the stable homotopy groups of spheres are a group completion; the general engine behind such identifications, the group-completion theorem of McDuff and Segal (1976), is quoted here as consistent with the literature, not source-verified. The failure door: monoids that do not cancel — §5's exhibits — where the same construction, run honestly, hands back the one-element group and a lesson about listening for silence.

4 · Three encounters

Encounter 1 — the integers from the counting numbers, built two ways. The ledger's route is now familiar: pairs \((a,b)\), the law \(a + d = b + c\), and every class settling into exactly one normal form — \([(n, 0)]\) for net \(n \geq 0\), \([(0, n)]\) for net \(-n\) — with no page in both columns at once. School's route is different in costume and identical in spine: adjoin a formal inverse, write reduced words like \(3 - 5 + 2\), and simplify by deleting \(n + (-n)\) pairs. Each route claims to have built the integers, and the completion's answer to the quarrel is that there is nothing to quarrel about: both worlds do the same job — receive the counting numbers' addition, answer every difference-question, generate no answers beyond what the job forces — and any two groups doing that job are the same group up to a unique translation. The translation itself is forced: the word \(3 - 5\) must go where it must go, \(\eta(3) + \eta(5)^{-1} = [(3,0)] + [(0,5)] = [(3,5)]\), which is the position of \((0,2)\) since \(3 + 2 = 5 + 0\). Every word meets its page this way, and the two routes close over each other: one construction, two costumes, zero choices.

Encounter 2 — the rationals from the positive whole numbers. Multiplication gives the positive whole numbers a second monoid structure — associative, identity \(1\), and no inverses: one-third is not a whole number, and the monoid cannot hear "what times \(3\) gives \(5\)". The completion answers with pages read as fractions: \([(a,b)]\) is \(a/b\), the law is cross-multiplication \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\) — the same ledger, with the columns multiplied now — and the question is answered by \([(5,3)]\), since \([(5,3)] \cdot [(3,1)] = [(15,3)]\) and \((15,3)\) is the position of \((5,1)\) because \(15 \cdot 1 = 3 \cdot 5\). The fundamental theorem of arithmetic explains why this completion feels so orderly: every positive whole number is a product of prime powers with counting-number exponents, so the monoid is a free commutative monoid on the primes, and completing it just lets the exponents go negative — \(18/12 = (2 \cdot 3^2)/(2^2 \cdot 3) = 2^{-1} \cdot 3 = 3/2\), one little ledger per prime. The positive rationals are the free abelian group on the primes; the whole numbers sit inside as the classes \([(n,1)]\); and the zero of multiplication is banned from the start — an absorbing element, which §5 shows would collapse the whole construction.

Encounter 3 — the two-point world of virtual bundles. Return to the bundles over \(X = \{p, q\}\): isomorphism classes are dimension pairs \((r_p, r_q)\), direct sum adds componentwise, and the monoid \(\mathbb{N}^2\) cancels — \(a + k = b + k\) componentwise forces \(a = b\) — so the completion embeds it, and \(K_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2\): one integer's worth of freedom per point. In the completed world the question "which bundle \(E\) satisfies \(E \oplus (2, 0) \cong (1, 2)\)?" — silent among actual bundles — has the answer \([(1,2)] - [(2,0)]\), a virtual bundle of dimension vector \((-1, 2)\), an object no actual bundle instantiates but every additive invariant must price correctly. The pricing is the whole point: "dimension at \(p\)" and "dimension at \(q\)" are additive invariants, so they are forced to extend to the virtual world as the two projections \(\mathbb{Z}^2 \to \mathbb{Z}\) — and they extend uniquely, which is to say the extension was never in anyone's gift to design.

Three completions — \(\mathbb{Z}\) from the ledger, the positive rationals from the primes, \(K_0\) of a two-point space from the bundles — and one job description behind all of them. The pattern box said it as a construction; the picture says it as a demand: whoever builds a group that receives the monoid honestly and answers its difference-questions must accept that every other additive invariant passes through their group, uniquely. That demand is what makes the completed world not a convenience but a coordinate system: the differences were formal, but the invariant they assemble into is as real as any invariant ever gets — measured by what it forces of every other measurement.

SKETCH — the research theorems behind this page: Barratt–Priddy–Quillen (early 1970s) identifies the group completion of the block-sum monoid of the symmetric groups' classifying spaces — \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+}\) — with \(\Omega^\infty S^\infty\), making the stable homotopy groups of spheres the completion's own homotopy. The general engine behind such identifications, the group-completion theorem of McDuff–Segal (1976), reads a topological monoid's completion off its components; this page quotes it as consistent with the literature, not source-verified. The parts this page deliberately does not build: the plus-construction's homology argument and the group-completion theorem's proof. What this page verified directly: the construction and its universal property (Box P60.A is self-contained), the 1957 manuscript's publication history (Borel–Serre, 1958), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\) for a field, \(K_0\) of the two-point space, and the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen statement as the lineage phrases it.

5 · Exhibits: when the completion forgets

Every pattern earns its keep by failing. Three exhibits — a monoid that forgets everything, an element that absorbs the world, and a completion built from the wrong relations — with the mitigation, the price, and the default.

Exhibit A — the monoid that forgets everything. Take the counting numbers with \(a \oplus b = \max(a, b)\): associative, zero \(0\), and \(2 \oplus 5 = 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) while \(2 \neq 3\) — cancellation fails at the smallest honest example. Run the construction anyway, honestly: the witness relation \((a,b) \sim (c,d)\) reads \(\max(a, d, k) = \max(b, c, k)\) for some \(k\), and \(k = \max(a,b,c,d)\) makes both sides \(k\) — every page is the same position as every other. The completion is the one-element group: not a defect of the construction but its verdict, delivered twice. Once by the collapse itself, Fig P60.3; once by universality: \(k \oplus k = k\) forces \(f(k) \cdot f(k) = f(k)\) in any group receiving the monoid, and a group's only idempotent is its identity — every additive invariant of the max monoid is trivial, so no group bigger than a point could have done the job. Difference-questions about max-addition are not merely hard; they are meaningless, and the completion says so by collapsing.

Mitigation: check cancellation before completing — the check is a search for \(a, k, b\) with \(a \oplus k = b \oplus k\) and \(a \neq b\), and at desk size it is a table scan. If the monoid fails, believe the collapse: it says the monoid has no nontrivial additive invariants, which is itself a theorem in disguise, and the honest invariant for max-addition is order-theoretic, not additive — no completion will conjure one.

Price: the collapse is total, and it is honest: "net worth" is a fiction for max-bookkeeping, and the completion is the fiction's auditor, returning zero.

Default: cancellation is the pre-flight check of the whole move; run it first, and if it fails, trust the one-element group — it is a verdict, not a malfunction.

Exhibit B — the absorbing element. Multiply the whole numbers \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\): associative, identity \(1\), and \(0 \times n = 0\) for every \(n\) — zero absorbs. The witness relation on this multiplicative monoid reads \(a \cdot d \cdot k = b \cdot c \cdot k\), and the witness \(k = 0\) makes both sides \(0\): every page is every other page, and the completion is again the one-element group. The absorbing element drags the whole world to a point — not because division is unnatural, but because "what times \(0\) gives \(1\)" deserves silence, and the construction, honest, gives it. Encounter 2 excised the zero and completed the positive whole numbers instead; the repair is exactly that: the completion of a monoid with an absorbing element is trivial, and the only honest completion is of the punctured world.

Mitigation: remove absorbing elements before completing — say which world the differences are about, and complete that world.

Price: the punctured monoid answers questions about the punctured world; anything phrased with the absorbing element in it is outside the completion's jurisdiction, honestly.

Default: an absorbing element is a certificate of collapse: if some \(z\) satisfies \(z \cdot a = z\) for every \(a\), the witness \(k = z\) merges all classes — banish \(z\) or accept the point.

Exhibit C — the wrong relations. Suppose the questions are not "this object minus that object" but "what remains when this cancels along that": \(X\) built from \(Y\) and its quotient \(X/Y\) wants the relation \([X] = [Y] + [X/Y]\) — an exact-sequence relation, not a direct-sum one. The direct-sum completion does not impose it: the pair world prices only sums, so the completed group comes out too big, carrying distinctions the questions never wanted, classes that should have merged. The honest object is a further quotient, imposing the exact-sequence relations on top of the completion; and the two quotients agree in well-behaved worlds — the resolution theorem says that when every object has a finite resolution by the nice ones, the direct-sum completion already satisfies the exact-sequence relations. Named here, proved nowhere on this page. In badly-behaved worlds the disagreement is not noise; it is the invariant that made non-regular rings a subject.

Mitigation: ask which relations the invariants you care about actually satisfy — direct-sum-additive, exact-sequence-additive, or both — and complete against the honest list.

Price: the further quotient is harder to compute (its elements are differences and relations), and in the well-behaved case the price was paid for nothing; the resolution theorem is the refund.

Default: "additive" is not one word: say direct-sum or exact-sequence before completing, and let the say-so be checked like any other promise.

Scope, honestly. What this page establishes, it establishes by exact receipts, machine-checked: the ledger's identities, normal forms, and witness arithmetic by exhaustive bounded search over pages with small entries; the two-routes bijection of Encounter 1 over words and pages with entries at most 6; the fraction arithmetic and prime-exponent vectors of Encounter 2, including the cross-relation's transitivity over \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\) at bounded digits; the two-point space's \(K_0 \cong \mathbb{Z}^2\) by componentwise bookkeeping; the Euler characteristic receipts \(\chi(\mathrm{pt}) = 1\), \(\chi(S^1) = 0\), \(\chi(S^2) = 2\), \(\chi(S^2 \times S^2) = 4\) by cell counts and the product rule; the naive relation's transitivity failure on the max monoid at the witness \((2,3), (3,3), (3,2)\); the collapse of the max monoid and of the absorbing-zero monoid by explicit witnesses; and the triviality of every hom from the max monoid to a group — the idempotent argument, cross-checked on the multiplication tables of \(\mathbb{Z}/5\), \(\mathbb{Z}/6\), and \(S_3\). What this page does not establish: the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen theorem and the group-completion theorem of McDuff and Segal (1976) are quoted as consistent with the literature and are not source-verified here; the resolution theorem is named, not proved; \(K_0\) beyond the two-point space is sketched, not computed. The verification this page performed directly covered the Grothendieck group construction and its universal property, the 1957 manuscript's publication history (written up by Borel and Serre, published 1958), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\) for a field by dimension, and the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen statement as the lineage phrases it. 6 · Doors out

  • Door — sheafify on a site (P62): the nearest door, and this page's footer. The completion answered for one monoid, in one place; a sheaf answers for a whole space of monoids at once — local answers that must agree on overlaps, gluing laws where local data is forced to assemble into global structure. The next move is this page's demand — the most economical honest world, and a proof it was not a choice — run point by point over a space.
  • Door — representable functor (P48): the universal property, one language up. Box P60.A proved that additive maps \(M \to G\) correspond exactly to homomorphisms \(K(M) \to G\); that correspondence is a functor in \(G\), and the completion is what represents it. P48 taught that an object is its maps; this page's group is the representing object for the additive-invariant functor — "forced, not chosen", made functorial.
  • Door — virtual classes (P64): the completed world's citizens, with their own geometry. This page built virtual bundles as classes of pages; the next move gives differences of geometric objects a life beyond bookkeeping — intersection theory, where virtual classes earn their keep as the honest currency of excess and defect.

7 · Lineage

  • 1957 — Grothendieck; 1958 — Borel–Serre. The Riemann–Roch manuscript: formulas about differences of vector bundles, \([E] - [F]\), priced by a completed monoid — its \(K\) from the German Klasse, "class". Grothendieck wrote the 1957 manuscript; Borel and Serre wrote it up and published it in 1958. For a field \(k\), \(K_0(k) \cong \mathbb{Z}\) by dimension; for the integers of a number field, the same construction refines the classical class group; and the same \(K_0\), transported from algebraic bundles to topological ones by Atiyah and Hirzebruch, became topological K-theory.
  • Early 1970s — Barratt, Priddy, Quillen. \(\mathbb{Z} \times B\Sigma_\infty^{+} \simeq \Omega^\infty S^\infty\): the group completion of the monoid of the symmetric groups' classifying spaces under block sum is the infinite loop space of the sphere spectrum — so the stable homotopy groups of spheres are a group completion, and the homotopy groups of the left-hand side are the stable stems. The "K-theory of \(\mathbb{F}_1\)" — the field with one element's would-be K-groups — is this page's honest metaphor for that statement.
  • 1976 — McDuff, Segal. The group-completion theorem: the engine that makes group completions of topological monoids computable, behind the Barratt–Priddy–Quillen door and many since. Quoted on this page as consistent with the literature, not source-verified — the scope box and Exercise 5 hold the receipt and the debt.

None of this history is load-bearing for the pattern; the receipts above are internal to this page, and the lineage is a map of doors, not a proof.

8 · Exercises

Exercise 1 ●○○ — the ledger, by hand. (a) Verify that \((2,5)\) and \((3,6)\) are the same position — \(2 + 6 = 5 + 3\) — and find the normal form (a single \([(n,0)]\) or \([(0,n)]\)) of each. (b) Compute \([(3,7)] + [(5,2)]\), reduce to a normal form, and name the integer school calls it. (c) Show \([(4,4)]\) is the zero and check \([(2,0)] + [(0,2)] = 0\) by the ledger's law.

Hint

For (a), the normal form of \((a, b)\) is \((a - b, 0)\) if \(a \geq b\) and \((0, b - a)\) otherwise — the net is the ledger's whole story. For (b), classes add columnwise: \((3,7) + (5,2) = (8,9)\), and then the net speaks. For (c), any page \((n,n)\) balances against \((0,0)\): \(n + 0 = n + 0\).

Worked answer

(a) \(2 + 6 = 8 = 5 + 3\), same position; both have net \(-3\), normal form \([(0,3)]\). (b) \([(3,7)] + [(5,2)] = [(8,9)]\), and \((8,9)\) is the position of \((0,1)\) since \(8 + 1 = 9 + 0\) — the integer \(-1\): net \(-4\) plus net \(+3\). (c) \((4,4) \sim (0,0)\) by \(4 + 0 = 4 + 0\), so \([(4,4)]\) is the zero — as is every \([(n,n)]\); and \([(2,0)] + [(0,2)] = [(2,2)]\), the position of \((0,0)\) by \(2 + 0 = 2 + 0\) — the two answers are negatives, as they must be: nets \(+2\) and \(-2\).

Exercise 2 ●●○ — the two-point space, by hand. (a) Verify \([(3,1)] - [(1,1)] = [(2,0)]\): the left side is the class of the page \(((3,1), (1,1))\); verify it is the position of the bundle \((2, 0)\) by the ledger's law, applied column by column. (b) Compute the dimension vector of \([(3,1)] - [(2,0)]\) and reduce to a normal form. (c) Confirm that \(\mathbb{N}^2\) cancels componentwise — every instance with entries \(\leq 4\) — and say why that licenses \(K_0(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}^2\) rather than a quotient of it.

Hint

For (a), the page \(((3,1), (1,1))\) is the position of \(((2,0), (0,0))\) exactly when \((3,1) + (0,0) = (1,1) + (2,0)\), column by column. For (b), the class is the page \(((3,1), (2,0))\); find the bundle \(v\) with \((3,1) + (0,0) = (2,0) + v\). For (c), cancellation means the map \(\eta\) embeds: no two distinct bundles ever merge.

Worked answer

(a) The page \(((3,1), (1,1))\) is the position of \(((2,0), (0,0))\) because \((3,1) + (0,0) = (1,1) + (2,0) = (3,1)\), column by column — first column \(3 = 1 + 2\), second column \(1 = 1 + 0\) — the witness \(k = (0,0)\) does it, so \([(3,1)] - [(1,1)] = [(2,0)]\). (b) The class is the page \(((3,1), (2,0))\): its dimension vector is \((3 - 2, 1 - 0) = (1,1)\), and the normal form is the page \(((1,1), (0,0))\) — the same position, since \((3,1) + (0,0) = (2,0) + (1,1)\), column by column — a virtual bundle, one-dimensional at each point, that no actual bundle instantiates with negative parts... and none needs to: the vector is honest because it is a class of pages, not a bundle. (c) Componentwise: \((a_1, b_1) + (c, d) = (a_2, b_2) + (c, d)\) forces \(a_1 = a_2\) and \(b_1 = b_2\), digit by digit — every bounded instance passes. Cancellation embeds \(\eta\), so the monoid survives into its completion intact, and the completion is exactly the integer lattice \(\mathbb{Z}^2\) — one ledger per point, two points, no merging.

Exercise 3 ●●● — the collapse, proved twice. (a) Verify \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5 = 5\) on the max monoid, and conclude that any group receiving the monoid honestly must merge the images of 2 and 3. (b) Show \((a, 0) \sim (b, 0)\) for all \(a, b \leq 8\): exhibit the witness and check the full \(9 \times 9\) table. (c) Prove that every hom from the max monoid to a group is trivial, using the idempotent \(k \oplus k = k\), and confirm the group fact it leans on — a group's only idempotent is the identity — over the multiplication tables of \(\mathbb{Z}/5\), \(\mathbb{Z}/6\), and \(S_3\).

Hint

For (a), apply \(f\) to both sides of \(2 \oplus 5 = 3 \oplus 5\) and cancel in the group. For (b), the witness wants both sides equal to it: \(k = \max(a, b)\) wants a look. For (c), \(f(k \oplus k) = f(k) f(k)\), and the left side is also just \(f(k)\).

Worked answer

(a) \(f(2) f(5) = f(5) = f(3) f(5)\), and the group cancels \(f(5)\): \(f(2) = f(3)\) — the merge is forced in every group at once, which is why the completion itself must merge them. (b) At \(k = \max(a,b)\): \(\max(a, 0, k) = k = \max(0, b, k)\), both sides reading \(k\) — all \(81\) pairs \(a, b \leq 8\) pass, so every page \((a,0)\) is the position of every other: one class. (c) \(k \oplus k = k\) gives \(f(k) f(k) = f(k)\), hence \(f(k) = e\) by cancelling one \(f(k)\); and \(x x = x\) forcing \(x = e\) is confirmed by scanning the three tables — \(25\), \(36\), and \(36\) products respectively, no idempotents but the identities. So every \(f(k) = e\): the completion is the one-element group, and Exhibit A's collapse is not one example but the whole story, twice over.

Exercise 4 ◆ — the rationals, diagnosed. Which pattern builds \(\mathbb{Q}\) from \(\mathbb{Z}\)? Name the move, and say what part of it is not this page's machine: exhibit the relation a naive fraction construction would use, prove that relation transitive on \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\), and then watch the same-shaped relation fail transitivity on the max monoid — and say what the two outcomes together teach about the machine's honest jurisdiction.

Worked answer

The move is P31's, one layer up: define by the job — the ring that receives \(\mathbb{Z}\) and inverts every nonzero element, most economically. The machine that does it is localization, a sibling of group completion, not this page's machine run on some monoid of \(\mathbb{Z}\). Group-completing the multiplicative monoid \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\) does produce the nonzero rationals as a multiplicative group — classes \([(a,b)]\) read as \(a/b\), the relation \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\), the ledger with its columns multiplied — but it says nothing about addition, and \(\mathbb{Q}\) is its addition as much as its multiplication; the localization must extend both operations at once, and their interaction (distributivity) is precisely what a one-operation construction cannot see. The naive relation \(a \cdot d = b \cdot c\) is transitive on \(\mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}\) because that monoid cancels: from \(a d = b c\) and \(c f = d e\), multiply to get \(a c d f = b c d e\) and cancel \(c d\). On the max monoid the same-shaped relation — \(\max(a, d) = \max(b, c)\) — fails at the witness \((2,3), (3,3), (3,2)\), which is Box P60.A's opening exhibit. The two outcomes draw the jurisdiction line: the pair construction is lawful exactly where the monoid cancels, patched by the witness where it does not; and a world with two interacting operations needs the job stated for both at once — P31's argument, run on a ring.
