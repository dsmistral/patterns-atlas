27	<p><strong>Problem one.</strong> Add the surds: \(\sqrt{50} - \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{8}\). School training says "simplify each one first" — but watch what the simplification actually is. Each number under a root hides a <em>square factor</em>: \(50 = 2 \cdot 5^2\), \(18 = 2 \cdot 3^2\), \(8 = 2 \cdot 2^2\). So \(\sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}\), \(\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}\) — all three are whole multiples of the <em>same</em> standard surd \(\sqrt{2}\). The sum is now transparent: \((5 - 3 + 2)\sqrt{2} = \)<strong>\(4\sqrt{2}\)</strong>. The check is exact and needs no decimals: squaring both sides, \((\sqrt{50} - \sqrt{18} + \sqrt{8})^2 = 50 + 18 + 8 - 2 \cdot 30 + 2 \cdot 20 - 2 \cdot 12 = 32\), and \((4\sqrt{2})^2 = 32\) — integers only, agreement. The "simplification" was a <em>reduction to a common standard form</em>: each surd to its canonical shape \(a\sqrt{d}\) with d square-free, after which the arithmetic happened in canonical coordinates, coefficient by coefficient.</p>
28	<p><strong>Problem two.</strong> Is \(2026^{77} - 2026\) divisible by 7? The number \(2026^{77}\) has 255 digits (the lab confirms the count from the logarithm: \(77 \cdot \log_{10} 2026 \approx 254.6\)). Nobody sensible multiplies it out. B01 computed the leftover for you: \(2026 = 7 \cdot 289 + 3\), so 2026 is on team 3. By B02's working rule (reduce first), \(2026^{77}\) is on the team of \(3^{77}\). The powers of 3 walk the teams mod 7 in a cycle of length 6: 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1, back to 3. Since \(77 = 6 \cdot 12 + 5\), the 77th step lands where the 5th step lands: \(3^{77} \equiv 3^5 \equiv 5 \pmod{7}\). So \(2026^{77} - 2026 \equiv 5 - 3 = 2 \pmod{7}\): <strong>not divisible — leftover 2</strong>. A 255-digit question, answered in single digits, and the answer is <em>no</em>, which no amount of confident multiplying would have made obvious.</p>
29	<p>Both problems were solved by the same shape of move: <em>replace each object by the appointed representative of its class</em> — surds by \(a\sqrt{d}\), numbers by their leftover — do the work among the representatives, and read the answer off. Neither problem mentioned the move; the move is what the solutions had in common. It has a name, and it is the fifth pattern of the spine.</p>
41	<li><strong>Legality.</strong> The reduction must be defined on the whole class, and when the answer must return to the original world it must run both ways. Squaring both sides of an equation fails this demand as a "reduction" — it is defined everywhere but not reversible, and P01's ghost roots (\(x = -1\) walking into \(\sqrt{x + 2} = x\)) are the standard wreckage: a transform that merges classes can create impostors that translate back as false answers. The cure is P01's Box P01.C: carry the side condition, or check candidates back.</li>
43	<li><strong>Uniqueness.</strong> The form must be <em>the</em> form. \(\sqrt{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}\) has two algebraic square roots, \(2 + \sqrt{3}\) and \(-2 - \sqrt{3}\); the radical sign's convention — √ means the non-negative root — is what makes "the" canonical value a function at all. Conventions that pin choices are not bureaucracy; they are demand three being enforced. When a reduction seems to give "two canonical forms", P01's discipline applies: a pinning is missing, and finding it is the work.</li>
