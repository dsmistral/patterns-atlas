<h1>Complete the square</h1>
<p class="tagline">A quadratic is a perfect square with one corner missing. Pay for the corner honestly — and the leftover number answers your question.</p>
</header>

<aside class="onramp"><strong>New word: vertex form.</strong> You have completed squares in school — the algorithm is class-10 work. What school rarely names is the <em>destination</em>: writing a quadratic as <strong>a(x − h)² + k</strong>, a <em>square plus a plain number</em>. That shape is called the <em>vertex form</em>, because it displays the vertex (h, k) instead of hiding it. This page is not about the mechanics you already own. It is about the <em>move</em>: recognizing, mid-problem, that the square is hiding there and that paying for its corner will answer the question.</aside>

<section id="bridge">
<h2>1 · Before we begin</h2>
<p>The spine so far has been a course in honesty. <a href="p01-pin-down-the-problem.html">P01</a> pinned claims down, <a href="p02-small-cases.html">P02</a> built conjectures from small cases, <a href="p03-special-cases.html">P03</a> attacked them with hostile configurations. All three are <em>epistemics</em>: moves about what to believe and when.</p>
<p>Here is the tension this page resolves. P02 and P03 keep ending at the same wall. The table shows the minimum of some quadratic — but tables detect, they never prove. The stress test kills a false formula — but the repair needs "a better form of the expression", and nobody said where better forms come from. Every honest method in your kit eventually asks you to <em>change the shape of an algebraic expression on purpose</em> — and nothing so far taught you how. This page is the first constructive move of the spine: not testing claims, but building the form a claim needs.</p>
</section>

<section id="encounter">
<h2>2 · Meet the problem</h2>
<p>Two problems, both about the same ten. First: <em>is x² + 10x + 41 ever equal to zero, for any real x?</em> Your factoring toolkit goes hunting and returns empty-handed — and that is not bad luck, it is structure: this quadratic has no roots at all, so no factorization will ever be found. Second, and older: solve <em>x² + 10x = 39</em>. This is al-Khwārizmī's own problem, stated around the year 820 as "a square and ten roots are equal to thirty-nine dirhams" — and note that trial factoring <em>does</em> work here (39 = 3 × 13, and x² + 10x − 39 = (x + 13)(x − 3)), but only if you think to try. Change 39 to 38 and the hunt finds nothing forever; the equation is not obliged to factor nicely.</p>
<p>Now watch the move that solves both, from a geometry problem al-Khwārizmī literally drew. Take the square x² and the ten roots as two 5-by-x rectangles glued to its east and north edges. The assembled gnomon is one corner short of a bigger square — the missing corner is 5 × 5 = 25. So: <em>pay for the corner.</em> In the equation, add 25 to both sides (a square's worth of balance):</p>
<p style="text-align: center">x² + 10x + 25 = 39 + 25, &nbsp;so&nbsp; (x + 5)² = 64,</p>
<p>and the square root hands over the answer at sight: x + 5 = 8, so x = 3. The modern number line adds the second sign, x + 5 = −8, so x = −13 — the lab checks both: 9 + 30 = 39 and 169 − 130 = 39. (Al-Khwārizmī kept only the 3; negatives were not yet numbers to him. The mathematics did not change — the address space did.)</p>
<p>And the first problem? Same corner, paid differently: not across an equals sign (there is none to balance), but out of your own pocket — add 25 and subtract 25 in the same breath, changing nothing but the shape:</p>
<p style="text-align: center">x² + 10x + 41 = (x² + 10x + 25) + 41 − 25 = (x + 5)² + 16.</p>
<p>A square plus 16. Squares are never negative, so the whole expression is always at least 16, and it equals 16 exactly at x = −5. Done — not detected, <em>proved</em>. Your P02 table (the values dip to 16 at x = −5 and climb on both sides) was right, and now it is certified. One move, two payments, two kills: an equation solved and a proof manufactured. It has a name.</p>
</section>

<section id="name">
<h2>3 · Name the move</h2>
<div class="pattern-box" id="box-p04">
<p><strong>Pattern P04 — Complete the square.</strong> Given an expression that is quadratic-like — a squared thing, plus a multiple of the thing, plus the rest — rebuild it as (thing + half the middle)² plus a plain leftover number. In an equation, keep the balance: whatever you add to one side, add to the other. In a bare expression, add and subtract the same quantity: the value never changes, only the shape. Then read off what the new shape knows: the vertex, the minimum, the roots' existence, the distance.</p>
<p class="words">Or, in words: a quadratic is a perfect square with its corner missing. Half the middle term tells you exactly how big the corner is. Pay for it honestly — both sides of an equation, or plus-and-minus inside an expression — and the square tells you the truth the scattered form was hiding.</p>
</div>
<div class="pattern-box" id="box-p04a">
<p><strong>Box P04.A — the recipe, both payments.</strong> For x² + bx + c, the corner is (b ⁄ 2)² and the leftover is c − b² ⁄ 4:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Equation</strong> x² + bx = d: add (b ⁄ 2)² to <em>both</em> sides, then take roots — remembering <em>both signs</em> (P03's boundary discipline, applied to the ±).</li>
<li><strong>Expression</strong> x² + bx + c: add and subtract (b ⁄ 2)²: you get (x + b ⁄ 2)² + (c − b² ⁄ 4). Nothing was added overall — this is why the move is safe inside proofs.</li>
<li><strong>Leading coefficient</strong> a ≠ 1: factor a out of the first two terms first (Example 1 does this at full scale), or divide the equation by a.</li>
<li><strong>The doubled form, for integer hygiene:</strong> multiply the monic identity by 4 and everything fractional disappears: 4(x² + bx + c) = (2x + b)² + 4c − b². No halves, no quarters — the lab verifies this exact integer identity over 10,625 input combinations with zero mismatches.</li>
</ul>
</div>
<div class="pattern-box" id="box-p04b">
<p><strong>Box P04.B — when to reach for it, and the honest boundary.</strong> Three-key diagnosis: the <em>claim type</em> is "quadratic-like" — any expression with a squared thing, a multiple of that thing, and leftovers; "quadratic-like" is broader than "quadratic" (Exercise 5 squares x² itself). The <em>problem shape</em> is invisible structure: a vertex or minimum you must locate or prove, a curve you must identify, a positivity claim, a factorization over the reals, an integral with a quadratic denominator. The <em>tools</em>: half the middle term, and the willingness to pay for a corner.</p>
<p><strong>The boundary, named honestly:</strong> the registry's full signature of this technique is bigger than this page — "add a carefully-chosen <em>term or parameter</em> so that one side becomes a perfect square." Cardano depresses the cubic with a substitution; Ferrari completes the square <em>with a parameter y</em> chosen so the quartic collapses, and y is then found from a resolvent equation; Lagrange subtracts a linear term to force equal endpoints before invoking Rolle. That generalization — choosing the corner's size on purpose, as a variable — belongs to <a href="p05-canonical-form.html">P05</a> and beyond. This page runs the fixed-corner core: half the middle term, no parameters.</p>
</div>
</section>

<section id="exemplars">
<h2>4 · The move at work</h2>
<p>Three worked examples, one per domain. In each, the square is paid for early and the leftover does the answering.</p>

<h3>Example 1 (algebra) — the quadratic formula, derived, not memorized</h3>
<p>Solve ax² + bx + c = 0 once and for all, with a ≠ 0. Divide by a (legal since a ≠ 0 — P01 would make you say why): x² + (b ⁄ a)x + c ⁄ a = 0. Now the middle coefficient is b ⁄ a, so the corner is (b ⁄ 2a)². Pay it across the equation:</p>
<p style="text-align: center">x² + (b ⁄ a)x = −c ⁄ a &nbsp;→&nbsp; (x + b ⁄ 2a)² = (b ⁄ 2a)² − c ⁄ a = (b² − 4ac) ⁄ 4a².</p>
<p>Take roots, both signs: x + b ⁄ 2a = ±√(b² − 4ac) ⁄ 2a, so <strong>x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ⁄ 2a</strong>. Look at what the move produced: the discriminant b² − 4ac was not memorized into the formula — it <em>emerged</em> as the leftover's numerator. Its sign is now a visible question about the leftover: positive leftover, two real roots; zero, one; negative, none (the square cannot equal a negative number — the move fails to produce roots precisely when it is proving there are none). The lab runs this derivation's output against a second route (the vertex form read directly) over 786 real-coefficient cases: the two routes agree everywhere; all 229 perfect-square-discriminant cases pass an exact integer root check; Vieta's sum-and-product identities hold in every case. <span class="pid">Moves used: P01 (the division by a, and what "solve" demands), P04.</span></p>

<h3>Example 2 (numbers) — a Diophantine equation tamed</h3>
<p>Find all integer solutions of x² + 6x = y² + 4y. Two variables, no obvious foothold — but each side is quadratic-like in its own letter. Complete both squares: x² + 6x = (x + 3)² − 9 and y² + 4y = (y + 2)² − 4, so the equation says</p>
<p style="text-align: center">(x + 3)² − 9 = (y + 2)² − 4, &nbsp;i.e.&nbsp; (x + 3)² − (y + 2)² = 5.</p>
<p>Now the new shape knows something the old one hid: a <em>difference of two squares</em> factors. (x + 3)² − (y + 2)² = (x − y + 1)(x + y + 5) = 5, and since x, y are integers, both factors are integers multiplying to 5 — so they must be a factor pair of 5: (1, 5), (5, 1), (−1, −5), (−5, −1). Each pair is a 2×2 linear system; solving gives exactly four integer solutions: (0, 0), (0, −4), (−6, −4), (−6, 0). The lab checks this two ways — the factor-pair route, and a brute scan of the whole box [−20, 20]² — and the two agree completely. Note the honest division of labour: the scan is a detector (P02's honesty applies to machines too); the factor-pair route is the proof that no fifth solution hides outside any box. <span class="pid">Moves used: P04, then the difference-of-squares factorization (class-12 algebra), with P02's detector-honesty discipline.</span></p>

<h3>Example 3 (geometry) — the circle that was wearing a disguise</h3>
<p>Identify the curve x² + y² − 6x + 4y − 12 = 0. Two letters, so the move runs twice, once per letter: x² − 6x = (x − 3)² − 9 and y² + 4y = (y + 2)² − 4. Substitute:</p>
<p style="text-align: center">(x − 3)² + (y + 2)² − 9 − 4 − 12 = 0, &nbsp;i.e.&nbsp; (x − 3)² + (y + 2)² = 25.</p>
<p>That is the CBSE class-11 definition of a circle, center (3, −2), radius 5 — the equation never mentioned either. The lab verifies the rewrite as a round trip: over an integer box around the curve, a point satisfies the original equation exactly when it satisfies the completed one (221 agreements, zero disagreements), the 12 lattice points on the curve are enumerated, and 240 points of the parametric circle (3 + 5 cos θ, −2 + 5 sin θ) all satisfy the original equation to within 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴. Beware the near-miss the disguise invites: (3, 2) uses the right numbers with the wrong signs — it satisfies nothing (the original gives −9 there), because the y-corner belongs to (y + 2)², not (y − 2)². <span class="pid">Moves used: P04, twice; P03's near-miss discipline on the sign of the corner.</span></p>
</section>

<section id="fails">
<h2>5 · When the move fails (or seems to)</h2>
<p>The move is small and honest, and its failures are instructive enough to catalog.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The cross term is not a stop sign — but it is a boundary.</strong> Given x² + 4xy + 5y², completing in x with y held as a parameter still works: (x + 2y)² + y², and the lab confirms the identity and the positivity (a sum of two squares, never negative — a genuinely useful proof). What the fixed corner cannot do is <em>classify</em> two-variable quadratics: telling an ellipse from a hyperbola requires rotating axes, and that is change-of-basis machinery — <a href="p05-canonical-form.html">P05</a>'s territory, honestly deferred.</li>
<li><strong>The negative square is a success wearing a failure costume.</strong> Complete the square on x² + 4x + 13 = 0: (x + 2)² = −9. The move "fails" to produce roots — because it has just <em>proved</em> there are none over the reals. When the leftover turns negative at root-taking time, read it as a verdict, not an obstruction. (Example 2's Diophantine, by contrast, was saved by exactly this shape: two squares with a small gap between them.)</li>
<li><strong>Cubics and quartics: out of this page's jurisdiction.</strong> x³ + 3x + 1 is not quadratic-like in any substitution of this page's kind. The registry's parameter generalization (Box P04.B) <em>does</em> reach the cubic and quartic — Cardano and Ferrari built it in the 1540s — but it is a different move with a different price, and this page will not pretend otherwise.</li>
<li><strong>Overkill has a price, and factoring has a lane.</strong> For x² + 10x − 39 = 0, the factoring route (x + 13)(x − 3) is faster if you see the pair. <em>Mitigation:</em> reach for P04 first when the question is about shape — vertex, minimum, positivity, identification — where factoring is silent, or when the factoring hunt fails. <em>Price:</em> the corner costs fractions when b is odd; the doubled form of Box P04.A removes them at the cost of larger numbers. <em>Default:</em> roots by factoring when the integers cooperate, everything else by the corner.</li>
</ul>
<aside class="aside warn"><strong>Scope note.</strong> This page owns the fixed-corner quadratic core: one square at a time, corner = half the middle term, in equations and expressions, including the doubled integer form. The parameter version (choosing the corner's size as a variable), the two-variable classification, and sums of many squares are real mathematics with their own pages ahead (P05, P06, and the bridge track). Nothing here should be stretched beyond its proof.</aside>
</section>

<section id="lineage">
<h2>7 · Lineage interlude</h2>
<aside class="aside"><strong>History, lightly — and machine-checked.</strong> The move is older than algebra. Babylonian scribes around 1800 BCE solved problems like "one number exceeds another by 7; their product is 60" (x − y = 7, xy = 60, so x² − 7x = 60) by the same corner — and their integer trick survives in this page's Exercise 4: double everything first, (2x − 7)² = 289 = 17², so x = 12 (the tablet's answer, y = 5) — and, on the modern number line, also x = −5, y = −12, which the lab confirms satisfies both conditions exactly. Al-Khwārizmī (c. 820) organized the move geometrically — the missing corner of this page's encounter is his drawing — and needed six separate case-forms because he had no negative numbers to unify them. The parameter generalization arrived with Cardano's <em>Ars Magna</em> (1545) and Ferrari's resolvent, and the registry credits the same idea with a modern cameo: Lagrange's trick of subtracting a linear term to force equal endpoints before invoking Rolle's theorem is completing the square on the hypothesis itself. None of this is load-bearing; it says the corner you just paid for has been worth paying for for four thousand years.</aside>
</section>

<section id="exercises">
<h2>8 · Exercises</h2>
<p>Graded by fill: ●○○ comfortable · ●●○ a real step · ●●● a climb · ◆ diagnose · ◐ explore. Hints and worked answers are collapsible; the ◆ exercise has no hint — the diagnosis <em>is</em> the exercise.</p>

<h3>Exercise 1 ●○○</h3>
<p>Write x² − 8x + 3 in vertex form, and state its minimum value and where it is attained. Then say, in one sentence, why the minimum is now <em>proved</em> rather than merely tabled.</p>
<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Half of −8 is −4, and the corner is (−4)².</p></details>
<details><summary>Worked answer</summary><p>Add and subtract 16: x² − 8x + 3 = (x − 4)² − 16 + 3 = (x − 4)² − 13. The square is never negative, so the expression is at least −13, with equality exactly at x = 4 — proved, because the add-and-subtract payment changed nothing about the value, only the shape. The lab confirms: the identity holds with zero mismatches over x = −30…30, and the integer-grid minimum is −13 at x = 4.</p></details>

<h3>Exercise 2 ●●○</h3>
<p>Write 2x² + 12x + 7 in vertex form a(x − h)² + k, and state its minimum. Why must the leading coefficient come out <em>before</em> the corner is paid?</p>
<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Factor 2 out of the first two terms only: 2(x² + 6x) + 7 — now complete inside the parentheses.</p></details>
<details><summary>Worked answer</summary><p>2(x² + 6x) + 7 = 2((x + 3)² − 9) + 7 = 2(x + 3)² − 11. Minimum −11 at x = −3. The coefficient must come out first because the middle term of the <em>whole</em> expression is 12x only when read through the factor 2: the corner inside the parentheses is half of 6, not half of 12. Paying the corner before factoring a out is the classic slip — it buys a corner of the wrong size. The lab confirms: the identity 2x² + 12x + 7 = 2(x + 3)² − 11 holds with zero mismatches over x = −30…30, and the grid minimum is −11 at x = −3 (and not −9 at x = −2, which is what the wrong corner would have suggested).</p></details>

<h3>Exercise 3 ●●○</h3>
<p>Compute ∫ dx ⁄ (x² + 4x + 13). Name the class-12 template the completed form unlocks, and verify your answer by differentiating it.</p>
<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>x² + 4x + 13 = (x + 2)² + 9 — a sum, not a difference, so the arctan template (not the log template) applies: ∫ du ⁄ (u² + a²) = (1 ⁄ a) arctan(u ⁄ a) + C.</p></details>
<details><summary>Worked answer</summary><p>With u = x + 2 and a = 3: ∫ dx ⁄ ((x + 2)² + 9) = (1 ⁄ 3) arctan((x + 2) ⁄ 3) + C. Verification by differentiation (the lab runs it as a round trip): the derivative matches the integrand at every half-integer grid point from −12 to 12, with largest error 5.2 × 10⁻¹¹ — numerical-differentiation noise, nothing more — and Simpson's rule on ∫ from −6 to 4 agrees with the antiderivative to 7.2 × 10⁻¹⁴. The completing-the-square step is the entire difficulty; the template is stock.</p></details>

<h3>Exercise 4 ●●●</h3>
<p>Find <em>all</em> integer solutions of x² + 14x = y² + 6y + 1 — by the route of Example 2, and then say why your route proves there are no others (a machine scan cannot).</p>
<details><summary>Hint</summary><p>Complete both squares; the difference of squares will factor into two integers whose product is prime.</p></details>
<details><summary>Worked answer</summary><p>(x + 7)² − 49 = (y + 3)² − 8, so (x + 7)² − (y + 3)² = 41, so (x − y + 4)(x + y + 10) = 41. Since 41 is prime, the factor pairs are exactly (1, 41), (41, 1), (−1, −41), (−41, −1) — four 2×2 systems, four solutions: (14, 17), (14, −23), (−28, −23), (−28, 17). The lab checks all four in the original equation (both sides equal 392 each time) and confirms by a scan of [−40, 40]² that there are no others in that box. Why the scan cannot certify: it visits finitely many points; the factor-pair route visits <em>all</em> of them, because any solution whatsoever must reduce to one of the four factor pairs of 41. Finiteness of the factor pairs of a prime is doing the real work — P02's honesty rule, sharpened into a proof.</p></details>

<h3>Exercise 5 ◆ Diagnose the move</h3>
<p>Prove that x⁴ − 4x² + 4 is never negative for any real x — and name the pattern from the shelf that does the job, including the substitution that makes it legal. No hint: the diagnosis is the exercise.</p>
<details><summary>Worked answer</summary><p>The expression is not a quadratic in x — but Box P04.B's claim type is "quadratic-like", and that is the diagnosis: with u = x², the expression is u² − 4u + 4, quadratic in u. Complete the corner: (u − 2)², so x⁴ − 4x² + 4 = (x² − 2)², a square, hence never negative. The lab verifies the identity over the integer grid with zero mismatches and finds no negative values anywhere in [−40, 40]. One honest wrinkle the grid teaches: over integers the minimum shown is 1 (at x = ±1), but the true minimum 0 is attained at x = ±√2 — outside the grid. Detectors detect only where they look; proofs (the square argument) see all of ℝ at once. The move: P04, with the substitution reading "quadratic-like" generously.</p></details>

<h3>Exercise 6 ◐ The leftover and the discriminant</h3>
<p>Complete the square on x² + bx + c and study the leftover c − b² ⁄ 4. (a) How does the leftover's sign decide how many real roots the equation x² + bx + c = 0 has? (b) For the monic case, find the exact relation between the leftover and the discriminant b² − 4c — then explain why Example 1's formula "emerges" from the corner the way it did. (c) Test your rule on a table of your own making, and say precisely what the table can and cannot confirm. No single answer is expected; the lab's grid checks (values below the leftover: zero) are the shared ground truth.</p>
</section>
